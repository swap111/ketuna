EMC basics for board designers: loops, return paths and edges

Systems & product engineering Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Why edge rate, not clock frequency, sets how high a board's emissions reach, why every signal's return path must stay unbroken, and why filtering belongs at the connectors where cables become antennas.

In this article
  1. Edges, not clocks, set the spectrum
  2. Loops radiate
  3. Return current takes the path of least impedance
  4. Cables are the antennas
  5. Common mistakes
  6. Try it yourself

Most boards that fail an emissions test were not designed carelessly; they were designed with attention on the signals and none on the currents coming back. Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) problems on boards mostly reduce to three mechanisms: fast edges that put energy at high frequencies, current loops that radiate it, and cables that carry it off the board as antennas. This article explains each mechanism with enough numbers to make design decisions, and the layout habits that follow.

Edges, not clocks, set the spectrum

A digital clock is roughly a trapezoid, and its spectrum has a well-known envelope. Up to a first corner at 1/(πτ), where τ is the pulse width, harmonics stay roughly flat. Above it, the envelope falls at 20 dB per decade. Above a second corner at 1/(πtr), set by the rise time tr, it falls at 40 dB per decade.

For a 10 MHz clock at 50 % duty, τ = 50 ns and the first corner is about 6.4 MHz. The second corner depends only on the edge:

Rise time Second corner, 1/(πtr)
5 ns about 64 MHz
1 ns about 318 MHz

At 300 MHz, the 1 ns edge is still on the gentler 20 dB per decade slope, while the 5 ns edge has been falling at 40 dB per decade since 64 MHz. The extra roll-off is 20 log10(300 / 64) ≈ 13 dB on the envelope. The clock frequency is the same; only the edge changed.

This is why a slow microcontroller can fail emissions at hundreds of megahertz: its I/O drivers have sub-nanosecond edges. The design levers follow directly. Use the slowest drive strength or slew-rate setting that meets timing. Add small series resistors at source on fast lines, which slow edges and damp ringing. Consider spread-spectrum clocking where the system allows, which spreads harmonic energy over a band rather than eliminating it.

Loops radiate

A current flowing out along a trace and back through its return path forms a loop, and a loop is a small antenna. For a small loop, radiated field strength grows with the square of frequency, linearly with loop area, and linearly with current:

E ∝ f2 × A × I

Each factor is a lever. You reduce f by slowing edges, as above. You reduce I by not driving more current than needed, and by supplying switching current locally from decoupling capacitors so it circulates in a small loop near the chip rather than across the board. You reduce A by making the return path run directly beside the signal. Halving the loop area halves the field, about 6 dB; on a board, the difference between a return path directly underneath and one that detours around an obstacle can be far larger than a factor of two.

Return current takes the path of least impedance

At low frequencies, return current spreads out and takes the path of least resistance. Above a few tens of kilohertz on typical boards, inductance dominates, and return current takes the path of least inductance, which is the path that minimises loop area: directly under the signal trace, on the nearest reference plane. A continuous plane gives every trace a tight return automatically. Problems start when the plane is interrupted.

Common ways boards break the return path:

  • Slots and splits in planes, from a row of closely spaced vias or a split between analogue and digital ground. Never route a fast signal across a split.
  • Changing layers without a nearby return path. When a signal moves from a trace referenced to one plane to a trace referenced to another, place a ground stitching via beside the signal via (if both planes are ground) or a capacitor between the planes (if one is power).
  • Traces near the board edge, where the field fringes beyond the plane.
  • Connectors with too few ground pins, so many signals share one distant return.

Cables are the antennas

A board is usually small compared with the wavelengths at which it fails. Cables are not. A cable of a metre or so is an efficient antenna in the tens to hundreds of megahertz, and it can be driven by a tiny common-mode voltage: noise that appears on all conductors of the cable together, relative to the board's ground, often caused by ground bounce or return currents flowing through the connector's ground pin. Because common-mode current does not return within the cable, its loop is the cable and its surroundings, which is enormous. Small common-mode currents can therefore dominate emissions.

The remedy is at the boundary. Treat every connector as a gate:

  • Route noisy signals away from connector areas and keep the connector region's ground quiet.
  • Filter at the connector: a series ferrite bead or resistor with a capacitor to ground, a common-mode choke on differential pairs, and ESD protection close to the pins.
  • If the product has a metal enclosure or chassis, bond the connector shells and cable shields to it at the point of entry, with a low-inductance connection.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the fastest I/O drive by default. It buys emissions, not performance.
  • Splitting the ground plane "to keep noise apart" and then routing signals across the split.
  • Testing the board without its real cables. Emissions with a short bench cable can look nothing like the product.
  • Leaving EMC to the certification lab. A pre-compliance scan at the first prototype costs far less than a respin after formal testing.

Try it yourself

  1. Compute the spectral envelope of a trapezoidal clock with numpy: build a waveform with 1 ns and then 5 ns edges, take an FFT, and plot both on a log frequency axis to see the second corner move.
  2. In KiCad, review a board you designed: find every fast signal that crosses a plane split or changes reference plane, and mark where a stitching via or capacitor is missing.
  3. Make a simple near-field loop probe from a short length of coaxial cable, connect it to a software-defined radio, and use GNU Radio or a spectrum display to compare a board's emissions with a fast and a slow I/O slew-rate setting. Treat the result as relative, not as a compliance measurement.
