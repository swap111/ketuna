What a drone flight-controller SoC needs: latency, sensor interfaces and safety

Computer architecture & RISC-V Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A flight controller is a hard real-time system with many serial and PWM interfaces and no tolerance for hangs. Derive its SoC requirements from a control-loop latency budget, the sensor list and the fail-safes.

In this article
  1. The control loop sets the timing
  2. Many interfaces, all at once
  3. Safety needs hardware, not just firmware
  4. Common mistakes
  5. Try it yourself

A multirotor drone cannot fly without a computer. Its frame is unstable by design, and only a control loop running many times a second, reading motion sensors and adjusting motor speeds, keeps it in the air. That makes the flight controller a hard real-time system: a late answer is as bad as a wrong one, and a hung processor means a crash. Choosing or designing an SoC for it is less about peak speed and more about determinism, interfaces and fail-safes. This article derives those requirements from the job.

Drones are also regulated aircraft. Anything you build and fly must follow the current rules of the national civil aviation authority, and testing should start with propellers removed.

The control loop sets the timing

At the core of the firmware is a cascade of loops. The innermost rate loop reads the gyroscope and adjusts motor commands to hold the commanded rotation rates; an outer attitude loop turns desired angles into rate commands; slower loops handle altitude, position and navigation. The inner loop runs fastest, commonly at hundreds of hertz to several kilohertz, depending on the airframe and firmware.

Take an illustrative 1 kHz rate loop: a 1000 µs period. Within it, the controller must:

  1. Read the inertial sensor. A 6-axis IMU sample (accelerometer and gyroscope, plus temperature) is about 14 bytes. Over SPI at 8 MHz, that is 14 × 8 / 8 MHz = 14 µs of transfer, plus a few microseconds of command overhead.
  2. Filter and estimate. Low-pass and notch filters on the gyro data, and an update of the attitude estimate. Floating-point arithmetic makes this far easier to write and verify.
  3. Run the controllers. PID or similar controllers for three axes.
  4. Mix and output. Convert axis commands into individual motor commands and update the PWM or digital motor outputs.

The total compute is modest for a 32-bit core with a floating-point unit, so the period is rarely the problem. Jitter is. If the loop sometimes runs at 1000 µs and sometimes at 1300 µs, because an interrupt for the radio or the logger got in the way, the controller's effective gain and phase vary and the tuning degrades. Requirements follow directly:

  • A data-ready interrupt from the IMU, with high priority, to start each cycle at a consistent moment.
  • DMA for sensor transfers, so the core is not waiting on SPI bytes.
  • An interrupt controller with priorities and preemption, so a low-priority logger never delays the control loop.
  • Deterministic memory: tightly coupled or on-chip SRAM for the loop's code and data, because a cache miss to external flash in the middle of the loop adds unpredictable delay.

Many interfaces, all at once

A flight controller is unusual in how many peripherals it uses simultaneously. A typical list:

Device Typical interface Why it matters to the SoC
IMU (gyro and accelerometer) SPI with data-ready interrupt Fast, low latency, sometimes two IMUs for redundancy
Barometer I2C or SPI Slow, but shares buses with other sensors
Magnetometer I2C Often inside the GNSS module
GNSS receiver UART Continuous stream, needs DMA and a ring buffer
Radio control receiver UART-based serial protocol Link-loss detection drives the fail-safe
Telemetry radio UART Bidirectional, lower priority
Motor controllers (ESCs) PWM or a digital serial protocol, 4 to 8 outputs Synchronous update across all motors
Logging SPI or SDIO to flash or a card High throughput, lowest priority

Counting these, the SoC needs several SPI controllers, several UARTs (each with DMA), I2C, and a timer block with many channels. Timers deserve attention. Motor outputs must be updated together, so all channels of a motor timer should load new values from shadow registers at the same period boundary, rather than changing mid-pulse. Classic servo-style PWM uses pulses of 1000 to 2000 µs; with a 1 MHz timer tick that is 1000 steps of resolution across the range. At an illustrative 400 Hz update rate, the period is 2.5 ms, which still leaves room for the longest pulse. Digital motor protocols need fast timers plus DMA to generate their bit patterns without loading the core.

Safety needs hardware, not just firmware

Firmware can hang, and the consequences of a hung flight controller are physical. Several protections belong in the SoC:

  • An independent watchdog. Clocked from its own oscillator, not the main clock, so a clock failure cannot stop it. Firmware must service it from the control loop itself, not from a background task, so that a stalled loop triggers a reset.
  • Brownout detection. A battery sag under full throttle can drop the supply; the SoC must reset cleanly rather than run with corrupted state.
  • Clock monitoring. Detect a failed external crystal and fall back to an internal oscillator.
  • A defined safe output state. On reset or fault, motor outputs must go to a known "stop" or idle level immediately, set by hardware defaults, not by firmware that may not be running yet.
  • Link-loss fail-safe. When the radio receiver stops delivering valid frames for a set time, firmware switches to a fail-safe behaviour such as hover-and-land or return-home. This logic is firmware, but it depends on reliable timestamps and a UART receiver that flags framing errors.
  • Memory protection. An MPU or RISC-V PMP can keep a crashing logger or telemetry task from corrupting the control loop's memory.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring average loop time instead of worst-case jitter. The tail is what destabilises the aircraft.
  • Servicing the watchdog from an idle task. The loop can stall while the idle task keeps the watchdog happy.
  • Running the control loop from external flash with a cache. Execution time then depends on what else ran.
  • Leaving motor pins floating at reset. A brief random pulse can spin a motor.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a 1 kHz control-loop skeleton for Zephyr on an emulated board in QEMU or Renode, toggle a GPIO at loop start, and measure the jitter as you add a busy logging thread at lower priority.
  2. Simulate a PWM timer with shadow registers in Verilog and check in Icarus Verilog that four channels update together at the period boundary.
  3. Model a rate loop for one axis in Python with a simple plant, add random loop-period jitter, and plot how the step response changes.
