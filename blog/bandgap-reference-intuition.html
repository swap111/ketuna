How a bandgap reference makes a voltage that ignores temperature

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A bandgap reference adds a base-emitter voltage that falls with temperature to a scaled thermal voltage that rises, so the sum stays flat. Work through the numbers, the circuit and what limits its accuracy.

In this article
  1. Two voltages that move in opposite directions
  2. Why it is called a bandgap
  3. The circuit that does the addition
  4. What limits accuracy
  5. Try it yourself

Every ADC needs a reference voltage, every regulator needs something to regulate to, and every brown-out detector needs a threshold. On a chip, almost all of these trace back to one block: the bandgap reference. Its job is to produce a voltage, typically near 1.2 V, that barely moves as the die heats from −40 °C to 125 °C and as the supply wanders. It does that not by finding a component that ignores temperature, but by adding two quantities whose temperature dependences cancel.

Two voltages that move in opposite directions

The first ingredient is the base-emitter voltage VBE of a forward-biased bipolar junction at constant current. It falls as temperature rises, roughly linearly, by around 2 mV per kelvin in silicon (the exact slope depends on current density and process). A voltage with this behaviour is called CTAT, complementary to absolute temperature.

The second ingredient comes from two junctions run at different current densities. A junction's voltage depends logarithmically on its current density, so if transistor Q2 has n times the emitter area of Q1 and both carry the same current, the difference in their VBE is:

ΔVBE = VT × ln(n), where VT = kT/q

This difference is PTAT, proportional to absolute temperature, and remarkably clean: the process-dependent saturation currents cancel in the difference, leaving only fundamental constants, the temperature and an area ratio you set in layout. At 300 K, VT ≈ 25.85 mV, so for n = 8, ΔVBE ≈ 25.85 × ln 8 ≈ 53.8 mV, with a slope of k·ln(8)/q ≈ 0.179 mV/K.

The PTAT slope is about ten times smaller than the CTAT slope, so it must be amplified. If you scale ΔVBE by a factor K and add it to VBE, the slopes cancel when K × 0.179 mV/K ≈ 2 mV/K, giving K ≈ 11.2.

Why it is called a bandgap

Add the numbers. With VBE = 0.65 V at 300 K (an illustrative value) and K = 11.16, the reference is 0.65 + 11.16 × 0.0538 ≈ 1.25 V. Using the same simple linear model at −40 °C and 125 °C gives values within a fraction of a millivolt of that.

The result is no coincidence. Extrapolate VBE back to absolute zero and it approaches roughly the bandgap energy of silicon, expressed in volts. Cancelling the temperature slope at room temperature effectively recreates that extrapolated value, so every bandgap reference built in silicon lands near 1.2 V regardless of the exact currents. That near-universal value gives the circuit its name.

The circuit that does the addition

A common CMOS implementation uses two bipolar transistors that come almost free in a standard CMOS process: the substrate acts as a collector, an n-well as the base and a p+ diffusion as the emitter, forming a vertical PNP. Q1 is one unit; Q2 is eight units in parallel.

  • Branch A: a resistor R2 from the output node to the emitter of Q1.
  • Branch B: an equal resistor R2 from the output node to a resistor R1, then to the emitter of Q2.
  • An op-amp senses the voltage at the top of Q1 and the top of R1, and drives the output node until they are equal.

With the two sensed nodes equal, the voltage across R1 is VBE1 − VBE2 = ΔVBE. The branch current is therefore ΔVBE/R1, a PTAT current, and because the R2 resistors are equal and see equal voltages, both branches carry it. The output is the Q1 emitter voltage plus the drop across R2:

VREF = VBE1 + (R2 / R1) × VT ln(n)

The scaling factor K is simply a resistor ratio, which matches well on chip and has little temperature coefficient when both resistors are the same type. For K ≈ 11.2 and R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 ≈ 112 kΩ and the branch current at 300 K is 53.8 mV / 10 kΩ ≈ 5.4 µA. These values are illustrative; a real design picks currents for noise and area, and then trims.

What limits accuracy

A first bandgap simulation often looks perfect over temperature. Monte Carlo and corners tell a different story.

  • Op-amp offset is amplified. An offset Vos appears in series with ΔVBE, so it is multiplied by about (1 + R2/R1). With K ≈ 11, a 2 mV offset shifts the output by roughly 24 mV. This is usually the dominant error, which is why bandgaps use large input devices, chopping, or trimming.
  • Curvature. VBE is not exactly linear in temperature, so cancelling the linear slope leaves a small bowed residual, with the output peaking near the temperature where the slopes cancel. Curvature-correction techniques add a small nonlinear term to flatten it.
  • Process spread of VBE. The absolute VBE varies from die to die, shifting both the output and the temperature at which the slopes cancel. Production parts usually trim a resistor ratio at test.
  • Resistor and transistor mismatch. An error in the area ratio n, or between the two R2 resistors, enters directly. Common-centroid layout of the bipolar array (one unit surrounded by eight) and matched resistor segments are standard.
  • Start-up. The circuit has a second stable state in which all currents are zero and the op-amp is content. Every bandgap needs a start-up circuit that injects current at power-up and then disconnects. Forgetting it gives a chip that works in simulation, where solvers often find the intended state, and fails on the bench.
  • Supply rejection. Noise on the supply reaches the output through the op-amp and the current sources; the op-amp's loop gain at the noise frequency sets how well it is rejected.

Try it yourself

  1. Write the linear model above in Python: VBE(T) as a straight line, ΔVBE(T) from kT/q·ln(n), and sweep K to find the value that makes the output flattest from −40 °C to 125 °C.
  2. In ngspice with the sky130 models, find the PDK's parasitic PNP device, bias it with a constant current, and run .dc temp -40 125 5 to measure its real VBE slope.
  3. Build the full circuit with an ideal op-amp (a high-gain voltage-controlled source), sweep temperature, and look for the curvature. Then add a 2 mV offset source at the op-amp input and measure the output shift against your estimate.
