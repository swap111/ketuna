Placement and congestion: reading the heat map before routing fails

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Global placement spreads cells, legalisation and detailed placement make them fit, and the congestion map predicts whether routing will succeed. Learn to read that map and relieve hot spots early.

Routing is where congestion becomes a hard failure: the router runs for a long time, gives up, and leaves shorts or unrouted nets. But the cause almost always existed earlier, in where the cells were placed. The placement step leaves a prediction of routing trouble in the form of a congestion map, and reading it takes minutes. Acting on it then is far cheaper than discovering the problem two hours into detailed routing.

What placement does in three passes

Placement takes the floorplan, with its rows, pins, macros and power grid, plus the netlist, and gives every standard cell a legal location.

  • Global placement treats cells as movable points and finds positions that minimise total estimated wire length while keeping density below a target. Modern placers such as the one inside OpenROAD (RePlAce, run as global_placement) model cells like charged particles that repel each other, so they spread out of dense regions while nets pull connected cells together. The result is good but not legal: cells overlap and sit between rows.
  • Legalisation snaps each cell onto a row site, removing overlaps with the smallest possible movement.
  • Detailed placement makes local improvements: swapping neighbouring cells, flipping orientation, shifting cells within a row, all to shorten wires without breaking legality.

Between these passes, timing-driven optimisation resizes cells, inserts buffers on long or heavily loaded nets, and may move critical cells closer together. That is why the cell count after placement is usually higher than after synthesis.

The density target and why it matters

Global placement aims for a target density, the fraction of each small region (bin) filled with cell area. If the floorplan utilisation is 50% and the target density is 55%, the placer may spread cells fairly evenly across the core. If the target density is close to 100%, the placer is allowed to pack cells tightly where wire length benefits, leaving empty space elsewhere.

Packed regions are a problem because routing demand follows cell density: more pins per square micrometre means more wires competing for the same tracks above them. A dense cluster can be perfectly legal for placement and impossible to route.

Reading the congestion map

After placement, a quick global route (or a placement-time routability estimate) divides the core into a grid of global routing cells (gcells). For each gcell and each layer, it compares demand (how many wires want to pass through) with capacity (how many routing tracks exist there, minus blockages such as power straps and macros). The congestion map colours each gcell by demand / capacity, and a value above 1 is overflow.

What to look for:

  • Isolated hot spots a few gcells wide, usually around one high-fanout cell, a wide multiplexer or a tight cluster of logic.
  • Hot bands along macro edges or between two macros, where wires are squeezed into a narrow channel.
  • Hot spots near pins, where a wide bus enters and fans out.
  • Direction imbalance. Horizontal overflow and vertical overflow are reported separately. Overflow in only one direction means one set of layers is short of tracks, and the shape of the block or the power grid on those layers may be to blame.

A small illustrative calculation makes overflow concrete. Suppose a gcell has 14 horizontal tracks on a layer, the power strap takes 2, so capacity is 12. If global routing wants 15 nets through it, demand / capacity = 15 / 12 = 1.25, and 3 nets must detour. A few such gcells are routine and the router absorbs them. Hundreds in one region become shorts after detailed routing.

Relieving congestion before routing

Fix congestion with the lightest change that addresses the cause.

  1. Lower the target density so the placer spreads cells more evenly. This is often enough for mild, scattered hot spots.
  2. Add placement blockages or padding. Partial blockages limit how full a specific region may be; cell padding adds a little space around each cell in a hot area so pins are less crowded.
  3. Move macros or change their halos. If congestion lines a macro edge, a wider channel or turning the macro so its pins face the core helps more than any placement setting.
  4. Reduce utilisation at the floorplan if the whole core is warm, not just spots.
  5. Change the logic. A huge multiplexer or crossbar is congested by nature. Restructuring it in RTL, for example pipelining or splitting it, is sometimes the only real fix.

Each step back up the flow costs more time but addresses a deeper cause. A common mistake is to keep turning router effort knobs on a design whose placement guarantees overflow.

Timing and congestion pull together

Timing-driven placement pulls cells on critical paths close together, which raises local density. Congestion relief spreads them apart, which lengthens wires. The two goals conflict exactly where the design is hardest, so look at timing and congestion together. A hot spot that sits on your worst timing path is a sign that the logic in that region needs a structural change, not just more space.

Common mistakes

  • Reading only total overflow. A single number hides whether the problem is spread thin or concentrated in one area.
  • Setting density from habit. The right target depends on the design's wiring character, so sweep it.
  • Ignoring the power grid. Straps consume tracks; a dense grid on a routing layer lowers its capacity everywhere.

Try it yourself

  1. Run a small design through LibreLane on sky130 up to detailed placement, using its option to stop after a given step (--to followed by the step name).
  2. Open the result in the OpenROAD GUI and view the placement density and routing congestion heat maps.
  3. Rerun with PL_TARGET_DENSITY_PCT set higher and lower, and compare the heat maps and the global routing overflow reported in the logs.
  4. Write a short note predicting where the hot spots will be, based on the RTL, before you look. Then check your prediction.
