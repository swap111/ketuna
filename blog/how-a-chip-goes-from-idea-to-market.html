How a chip goes from idea to market, step by step, from an Indian team's point of view

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

The stages a chip passes through, from specification and design to fabrication, test, qualification and the product approvals that follow, and the decision each stage exists to make.

Students usually meet chip design one step at a time: a Verilog lab here, a synthesis report there. A real chip is a chain of those steps, and each one exists to catch a specific kind of mistake before it becomes expensive. Seeing the whole chain helps you understand why teams spend so long on verification, why a "working" chip can still not be sold, and where your own skills fit in. This walkthrough follows a small team in India building a chip for a product, from the first idea to the day it ships.

The chain at a glance

The diagram has one idea built in: everything before tape-out is cheap to change, everything after is not. Most of the discipline in chip development follows from that.

Specification: deciding what to build

A chip starts from a product need, not from a circuit. Suppose the team wants a controller for a battery-powered field sensor. The specification turns that need into numbers someone can test: which interfaces, how much memory, the supply voltage range, the power budget in sleep and active modes, the operating temperature range, the package and the target cost.

This is also where the team decides what the chip will not do. Every feature costs area, verification time and risk. A good specification is short enough to read in one sitting and specific enough that two engineers would build the same thing from it.

Design and verification: making it right in simulation

Designers write RTL for the digital blocks and design the analog blocks at transistor level. Verification engineers, often more of them than designers, build testbenches, write checks against a reference model, measure coverage and hunt for corner cases. When they find a bug, it goes back to design. The loop runs many times, which is why the dashed arrow in the diagram matters.

The habit to learn here is that "it passed my test" means little. A block is ready when an independent check, ideally written by someone else from the specification, cannot break it.

Physical design and tape-out: turning logic into geometry

Synthesis maps RTL to standard cells, then placement, clock tree synthesis and routing turn the netlist into a layout. Signoff checks follow: static timing analysis across process, voltage and temperature corners, design rule checks, layout-versus-schematic and power integrity. Only when all are clean does the team tape out, sending the final layout to a foundry.

For a first chip, many teams use a multi-project wafer run, where several designs share one set of masks so each pays a fraction of the cost. Open PDKs such as sky130 and gf180 let students and small teams go through this entire flow with open-source tools.

Fabrication, assembly and test: getting physical parts

The foundry processes wafers over many weeks. The wafers then go to an assembly and test facility, where each die is tested on the wafer, cut out, mounted in a package, bonded and tested again. An Indian team usually works with partners for these steps, so the handoffs matter: test programs, package drawings and test hardware must be ready before the wafers arrive, not after.

When first parts come back, the team does bring-up: power the chip carefully, check that clocks and reset work, then run through every function. A bring-up plan written before tape-out saves weeks.

Qualification and product approvals: earning the right to ship

A chip that works on the bench is not yet a product. Qualification shows it keeps working: stress tests at high temperature and voltage, temperature cycling, moisture exposure, electrostatic discharge and latch-up tests, run on samples from more than one wafer. The goal is evidence that the chip will survive its intended life, not just its first hour.

Then comes the product the chip goes into. The finished device may need safety registration with the national standards body, equipment approvals for any radio, and electromagnetic compatibility testing. Those approvals belong to the product maker, but a chip team that ignores them makes its customers' lives hard. Radiated noise from a fast clock, or a radio block that drifts off frequency, can block a product's approval. Check the current official requirements for each product category early, because they shape design choices.

Common mistakes along the chain:

  • writing the specification after the RTL, so nothing can be tested against it
  • treating verification as a phase at the end instead of a parallel activity
  • leaving package selection and test plans until the wafers are already in the fab
  • forgetting that the end product, not just the chip, has to pass approvals

Try it yourself

  1. Write a one-page specification for a tiny chip, for example a PWM controller with an SPI interface, including what it will not do.
  2. Implement it in Verilog, simulate with Icarus Verilog, and ask a friend to write an independent testbench from your specification alone.
  3. Harden it with LibreLane on the sky130 open PDK and read the timing and DRC reports.
  4. Draft a bring-up checklist listing which pins you would drive first and what you expect to see.
