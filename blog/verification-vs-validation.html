Verification and validation: building the right product vs building it right

Systems & product engineering Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Verification checks that the product meets its requirements; validation checks that it meets the user's real need. Examples of each, the V-model that links them, and how a product can pass one and fail the other.

Imagine a soil moisture sensor that passes every test in its plan: accuracy within limits, battery life met, radio range met. Then farmers start using it and stop after a month, because it has to be pulled out of the ground to read, and nobody wants to dig it up every week. The team did everything right and still built the wrong product. Verification and validation are two different checks, and this story is what happens when you do only one of them.

Two questions, two answers

The short versions are worth memorising:

  • Verification: did we build the product right? Does the design meet its written requirements and specifications? The reference is the requirements document.
  • Validation: did we build the right product? Does the product meet the needs of its real users, in their real environment? The reference is the user and the problem.

Verification can be done largely in a lab, by engineers, against numbers. Validation needs users, or at least realistic conditions and someone who represents the users. Both are necessary because requirements are themselves a translation of user needs, written by people who can misunderstand. Verification proves you followed the translation. Validation checks that the translation was right.

The V-model

A common way to picture the relationship is the V-model. The left side goes down from needs to requirements to design to implementation. The right side climbs back up, testing at each level against the matching item on the left.

The V-model is sometimes criticised for suggesting a strict sequence, with no testing until the bottom is reached. Treat it as a map of what is checked against what, not as a schedule. Good teams start validation early, with prototypes and mock-ups, long before the right-hand side of the V is complete.

Examples of each

For a clip-on safety badge that detects a fall and alerts a supervisor:

Activity Verification or validation Checked against
Measuring sleep current on the bench Verification Requirement: average current limit
Running the fall-detection model on a recorded test set Verification Requirement: detection and false-alarm rates
Drop test of the enclosure from a set height Verification Requirement: mechanical robustness
Night-shift workers wearing prototypes for a week Validation Need: workers will actually wear it all shift
Supervisors responding to alerts in a drill Validation Need: help arrives quickly when someone falls
Checking that the alert tone is audible in a noisy plant Validation (and later a new requirement) Need: the wearer knows the alert was sent

The last row shows something important. Validation often discovers needs that were never written down. When it does, the finding becomes a new requirement, and that requirement then gets verified. The two activities feed each other.

Passing one, failing the other

The opening story is a product that passed verification and failed validation: correct to its specification, wrong for its users. The cause was a missing need ("readable without removing the probe") that never became a requirement.

The opposite also happens. A team builds a quick prototype that users love in a field trial, so validation looks good, but the design has never been checked against its temperature range or its battery-life requirement. It will fail in summer or need charging too often. Enthusiastic feedback does not replace measurement.

For chip design the vocabulary is the same but the emphasis shifts. A large part of a chip team's effort is design verification: proving the RTL matches the specification, with simulation, coverage and formal methods. Validation of the chip happens in the system, often first on an FPGA prototype running real software, and then on silicon in the target product.

Planning both from the start

A good plan names both activities for every major need:

  • each requirement has a verification method (inspection, analysis, demonstration or test) and a test ID
  • each key user need has a validation activity: who, where, under what conditions, and what result would count as success
  • validation activities are scheduled early, on prototypes, when findings are cheap to act on
  • results of both feed the same change process, so a validation finding becomes a tracked requirement

Common mistakes

  • Treating the requirements as the truth. They are a translation of needs and can be wrong. Validation is how you find out.
  • Validating only at the end. By then a missing need can cost a redesign.
  • Letting users stand in for measurement. Positive feedback on comfort says nothing about battery life at 45 °C.
  • Calling every test "validation". Using the words loosely hides which question has actually been answered.

Try it yourself

  1. Take a project you have built and list five checks you made. Label each as verification or validation, and write what it was checked against.
  2. For the same project, write two user needs that you never validated, and design a short trial with real users to check each one.
  3. If your project includes RTL, write a cocotb test with Icarus Verilog that verifies one requirement, then describe in a paragraph how you would validate the same feature on an FPGA board with real inputs.
