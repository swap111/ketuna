Getting the most out of a mentor: bring questions, show work

Careers & skills Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A mentor's time is limited, so the value comes from your preparation. Bring specific questions, share work in progress early, and act on feedback between meetings so each session starts further ahead.

In this article

  2. Prepare every session
  3. Show work in progress, not only finished work

A good mentor can save you months: they have made the mistakes, they know which topics matter, and they can look at your design and see in a minute what you would find in a week. But a mentor is not a lecture you attend. The same hour can be wasted or transformative depending almost entirely on what you bring to it. This article is about the mentee's side of the relationship: preparing, sharing unfinished work, and doing something with the feedback.

What a mentor is for

It helps to be clear about what you are asking for. A mentor is not there to teach you a subject from scratch; books, guides and labs do that better and at your own pace. A mentor is most useful for the things those sources cannot give you:

  • Judgement: "Is this the right approach, or am I solving the wrong problem?"
  • Review: looking at your actual design, code or document and finding what you missed
  • Priorities: which of the ten things you could learn next matters most for your goal
  • Context: how work is really done on a team, and which habits experienced engineers rely on
  • Unsticking: a hint when you have tried properly and are going in circles

If most of your sessions are spent on questions a search or the documentation would answer, you are using the most expensive resource for the cheapest job.

Prepare every session

A short written agenda, sent a day before, changes the session completely. It lets your mentor think about your questions in advance, and it forces you to decide what you actually need. Keep it brief:

Session 4 agenda

Since last time:
- Finished the async FIFO RTL; directed tests pass
- Did NOT finish: random test with reference model (2/3 done)

Questions (most important first):
1. My gray-to-binary conversion is on the read side
   only. Is that a problem for the full flag timing?
   (diagram attached, page 2)
2. Coverage: is "full reached at least once" enough,
   or should I cover full with simultaneous read?
3. Should I learn formal next, or finish UART coverage?

Want feedback on: the attached block diagram.

Notice the shape. It reports progress honestly, including what is not done. The questions are specific, ordered by importance and point at evidence. Specific questions get specific answers; "can you explain CDC?" gets a lecture you could have read.

If the session is shorter than expected, the first question still gets answered, because you put it first.

Show work in progress, not only finished work

Many mentees wait until something is polished before showing it, because unfinished work feels embarrassing. This is backwards. Feedback is cheapest when the work is a sketch: a block diagram, a half-page spec, a list of test cases. A mentor who sees your plan before you write the RTL can point out the missing clock-domain crossing in five minutes. The same comment after two weeks of coding means rewriting.

A good rhythm is to bring something at every stage:

Stage What to show
Idea A one-paragraph problem statement and a hand-drawn block diagram
Plan Interface list, key decisions and the tests you intend to write
Build One working piece with its test, and the part that is confusing you
Finish Results, limits, and what you would do differently

Drawing by hand first is worth keeping as a habit here. A sketch on paper shows your understanding, including its gaps, much more honestly than code generated quickly with a tool. If you did use an AI assistant for a piece, say so and show how you checked it; your mentor can then judge whether your checking was enough, which is a lesson in itself.

Act on feedback between meetings

The real work happens between sessions. Write down feedback during the meeting, in your own words, and read it back at the end to confirm you understood: "So the action is to move the synchronizer to the write pointer as well, and add a test that fills the FIFO while reading." Turn each piece of feedback into a concrete action with a rough date.

At the start of the next session, report what you did with it. This closes the loop, and it is the single strongest signal to a mentor that their time is well spent. If you decided not to follow a suggestion, say why. Disagreeing with evidence is fine and often leads to the best conversations; silently ignoring advice is not.

Over several sessions, look for patterns in the feedback. If three reviews in a row mention unclear naming, or missing reset behaviour, or tests that cannot fail, that pattern is the thing to work on, more than any single comment.

Common mistakes

  • Arriving with no agenda. The session drifts, and the important question comes up in the last two minutes.
  • Only showing finished work. Feedback arrives when changes are most expensive.
  • Asking for answers instead of judgement. "What is the answer to question 3?" teaches less than "Here is my answer; where is my reasoning weak?"
  • Not following up. The same feedback repeated at every session is a sign it is not being acted on.
  • Treating feedback as a verdict on you. It is about the work; take it, clarify it, and decide later with data.

Try it yourself

  1. Before your next mentoring or review session, write a one-screen agenda in the shape above and send it a day ahead.
  2. Choose one piece of work at the idea stage and draw its block diagram by hand. Show it before writing any code, and note what changed because of the feedback.
  3. Keep a feedback.md file: date, comment, action, done. Review it monthly and pick the most frequent theme to focus on.
  4. If you do not have a mentor, practise the same habits with a peer: exchange an agenda and a piece of unfinished work each week, for example a Verilog module and its test run under Icarus Verilog.
