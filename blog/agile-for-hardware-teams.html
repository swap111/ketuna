Agile for hardware teams: what works when you cannot ship every week

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Which agile habits carry over to hardware and chip projects, where boards take weeks to arrive: short planning cycles, visible work boards, demos of intermediate results and fixed gates for irreversible steps.

In this article

  2. What transfers very well
  3. Demos without a product
  4. A definition of done for hardware tasks
  5. An example cycle before a board order

Agile methods grew up in software, where a team can build, test and release a working increment every week or two. Hardware teams who try to copy that rhythm hit a wall: a circuit board takes weeks to fabricate, a chip takes months, and you cannot release half a board. Some teams conclude that agile does not apply to them. That throws away the parts that work very well in hardware. The trick is to separate the habits that depend on frequent releases from the ones that depend only on frequent feedback.

What does not transfer, and why

Two assumptions behind software agile break in hardware:

  • Cheap, fast iteration. Rebuilding software costs minutes. A board revision costs money and weeks; a chip revision costs far more. You cannot treat every build as disposable.
  • Incremental delivery to users. A board is either assembled or not. You cannot ship the power section this sprint and the radio next sprint.

So hardware projects keep milestones and gates for irreversible steps: specification freeze, board order, tape-out, production release. These have fixed dates, entry criteria and a review. Pretending they are just another sprint is how teams order boards with known open questions.

What transfers very well

Most agile habits are about making progress visible and getting feedback early. Neither requires shipping a product.

Short planning cycles. Plan in one- or two-week cycles, each with a goal stated in a sentence ("all schematic pages reviewed and power budget closed"). A short cycle forces the team to break big tasks into pieces small enough to finish, and it exposes slips within days rather than at the milestone.

A visible board. A shared board with columns such as to do, in progress, in review and done shows everyone where work is stuck. Adding a limit on how many items can be in progress at once, a work-in-progress limit, stops people starting new tasks while reviews pile up. In hardware the review column is often the bottleneck, and a board makes that obvious.

A daily stand-up of fifteen minutes: what I finished, what I am doing, what is blocking me. Problems are noted and solved afterwards by the people involved, not in the meeting.

Retrospectives. At the end of each cycle, half an hour on what to keep doing and what to change. Hardware teams benefit especially, because the lessons from a bring-up are easy to forget by the next board.

Demos without a product

The heart of agile is showing working results regularly to someone who can react. Hardware teams can demo a lot before any custom board exists, if they plan for it:

Stage What can be demonstrated
Architecture A trade study result, a power and latency budget, a block diagram reviewed against requirements
Before boards Firmware running on a development board with the same microcontroller; RTL passing tests in simulation; the design on an FPGA board
Layout A 3D view of the board in its enclosure model; a 3D-printed enclosure with a dummy board
Boards back Each subsystem brought up and measured against its requirement, one by one
Validation Field trial data, test results against the plan

Each demo answers a real question for someone: does the firmware architecture work, does the board fit, does the sensor give useful data. The habit of building a demonstrable result every cycle also keeps work integrated. A team that shows firmware on a development board every two weeks has far less integration pain when the custom board arrives.

A definition of done for hardware tasks

"Done" causes more slips than any other word in a project. Agree what it means for each kind of task, and put it on the board:

  • Schematic page: drawn, electrical rules check clean, reviewed by one other engineer, findings closed.
  • RTL block: lint clean, unit tests pass in continuous integration, coverage reviewed, code reviewed.
  • Firmware driver: runs on the development board, unit tests pass, reviewed, documented against the interface control document.
  • Bring-up of a subsystem: measured against its requirement, results logged, issues filed.

With a clear definition, "90 % done" stops being a status. A task is done or it is not.

An example cycle before a board order

Here is what a two-week cycle might look like for a team approaching a board order, with the order date as a fixed gate at the end of the following cycle.

Cycle goal: schematic complete and reviewed; layout placement agreed

Board                     Owner      Done means
------------------------  ---------  --------------------------------
Power page                hw-lead    ERC clean, reviewed, budget closed
Sensor + ADC page         hw-2       ERC clean, reviewed
MCU + debug page          hw-2       ERC clean, reviewed, pinout frozen
Pinout in ICD             fw-lead    ICD rev B approved by hw and fw
Drivers on dev board      fw-lead    ADC and radio read on dev board
Enclosure fit check       mech       placement exported, fit confirmed
Demo at end of cycle      all        schematic walk-through, dev-board
                                     readings, enclosure model

Every item has an owner and a done condition, and the demo shows results to the people who will approve the board order. If a page is not done by the end of the cycle, the team knows two weeks before the gate, with time to cut scope or move the date deliberately rather than by accident.

Common mistakes

  • Treating milestones as optional. Irreversible steps need gates with entry criteria.
  • Sprints with no demonstrable result. Without a demo, a cycle is just a status meeting with a deadline.
  • "Done" meaning drawn or written. Review and test are part of done.
  • Adding people to a late task near a gate. New people need time to learn the design and slow the team at first; cut scope or move the date instead.
  • Stand-ups that become design meetings. Note the problem, solve it after with the people involved.

Try it yourself

  1. Set up a board with to do, in progress, in review and done columns for your next project, with a work-in-progress limit of two per person, and use it for a month.
  2. Write a definition of done for each type of task in your project and display it on the board.
  3. Plan your next two-week cycle around one demonstrable result, for example firmware running on a development board under Zephyr, or RTL passing a cocotb test, and show it to someone outside the team at the end.
