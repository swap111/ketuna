Metal fill, density rules and why the fab cares about empty space

Physical design & tape-out Advanced 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Chemical-mechanical polishing needs even metal density, so empty regions get filled with dummy shapes. Learn how density windows are checked, how fill is inserted, and how its capacitance affects sign-off timing.

Look at a finished layout in KLayout with the fill layers turned on, and the empty spaces between your wires are crowded with small rectangles that connect to nothing. They are not decoration. Each metal layer of a modern process is flattened by chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP), and polishing only works evenly when every region of the wafer has a similar fraction of metal. Fill makes that happen. It also adds capacitance to nearby wires, which means fill is a timing question as much as a manufacturing one.

Why polishing needs even density

In a damascene metal process, trenches are etched into the dielectric, the wafer is covered with metal, and CMP grinds the excess away until only the metal in the trenches remains. The polishing pad is not perfectly rigid, and the materials polish at different rates.

  • In sparse regions, there is little metal to support the pad, so it presses into the dielectric and the surface ends up lower.
  • In dense regions, wide areas of metal polish faster than the surrounding dielectric and dish in the middle; neighbouring dielectric erodes too.

The result is a surface whose height varies with the local metal density. Thickness variations change wire resistance and capacitance, and height steps carry up into the layers above, where lithography has limited depth of focus. Foundries therefore require every region of every layer to have a metal density within a minimum and a maximum, and often limit how sharply density may change between neighbouring regions.

How density rules are checked

Density is measured in windows: square regions of a size set by the foundry, stepped across the die with some overlap. For each window and each layer, the check computes the metal area divided by the window area and compares it against the limits.

A worked example with illustrative numbers. Take a 50 µm × 50 µm window, so 2,500 µm², on one metal layer:

  • Drawn signal and power metal in the window: 300 µm², a density of 300 / 2,500 = 12%.
  • Suppose the minimum density rule is 20%. The window needs 0.20 × 2,500 = 500 µm² of metal, so 200 µm² more.
  • With 2 µm × 2 µm fill squares (4 µm² each), that is 200 / 4 = 50 fill shapes, placed where spacing rules allow.

The maximum rule works the other way. A region dominated by wide power straps can exceed it, and the fix is not fill but slotting: cutting openings into wide metal so its effective density drops, which the rules for wide metal often require anyway.

How fill is inserted

Fill is normally added near the end of the flow, after routing and before the final DRC, because it needs to know where the real wires are.

  • Rule-based fill places shapes of a fixed size and pitch everywhere there is room, respecting spacing to real metal. It is simple and fast and tends to overfill.
  • Model-based or density-driven fill computes how much each window needs and adds only enough to meet the target, which limits the capacitance it adds.

Fill can be floating (connected to nothing) or tied to a supply or ground. Tied fill has predictable capacitance because its voltage is fixed. Floating fill takes on a voltage set by its neighbours, which makes its effect harder to model exactly, but needs no connections. Which one your PDK and flow use is a foundry and flow decision; check your PDK's documentation. Open tools can insert fill: OpenROAD has a density fill command driven by a rules file, and KLayout and Magic scripts are used with open PDKs.

Fill is also added to layers other than metal, such as polysilicon and diffusion, for the same reason: even density during processing.

Fill and timing

Every fill shape near a wire adds capacitance, both to the fill itself and, through floating fill, between neighbouring wires. A wire running through a newly filled region becomes slower than it was when timing was signed off without fill.

Practical consequences:

  • Extract with fill. Sign-off parasitic extraction must run on the layout with fill in place, or use a model of its effect that the foundry accepts. Signing off timing before fill and inserting it afterwards is a classic way to lose the margin you thought you had.
  • Keep fill away from critical nets. Fill tools support keep-out distances around specified nets, such as clocks and the worst timing paths. The cost is that the density around them must be made up elsewhere in the window.
  • Leave margin. If fill is added late, keep some setup margin for the capacitance it will add.
  • Watch matched structures. In analog and clock circuits that rely on matching, uneven fill around matched wires creates mismatch. Use symmetric fill patterns or blockages.

Common mistakes

  • Running DRC before fill and calling it final. Density is part of DRC, and fill shapes must themselves be DRC clean.
  • Ignoring maximum density. Wide straps and macros can exceed it.
  • Extracting timing without fill. Post-fill capacitance is real capacitance.

Try it yourself

  1. Harden a small design with LibreLane on sky130 and open the final GDS in KLayout. Toggle the fill layers on and off and see where fill went.
  2. Write a short Python script, or a KLayout macro, that computes metal density in fixed windows for one layer, and compare sparse and dense regions.
  3. Extract parasitics with and without fill (if your flow lets you skip fill) and compare total capacitance on a long net in the SPEF files.
  4. Load both SPEF files into OpenSTA with read_spef and compare report_checks on the worst path.
