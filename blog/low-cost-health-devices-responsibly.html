Designing low-cost health devices responsibly: accuracy, safety and approvals

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Why medical devices are regulated, how to validate a device's accuracy honestly against a reference with a Bland-Altman analysis, and how to avoid claims that a low-cost device cannot support.

Low-cost health devices, such as a heart-rate monitor, a pulse oximeter, a thermometer or a fall detector, could make basic monitoring available to many more people. Engineering students build prototypes of them every year, often with good intentions and impressive demos. But a health device is different from a weather station: if it is wrong, someone may skip a doctor's visit they needed, or panic over nothing. Designing one responsibly means taking accuracy, safety and approvals seriously from the first day, and being honest about what the device can and cannot do.

Why medical devices are regulated

A device that claims to diagnose, monitor, treat or prevent a medical condition can harm people in two ways. It can harm directly, through electric shock, burns, toxic materials or a failed therapy. And it can harm through wrong information, a reading that leads to a wrong decision. Regulation exists to make sure a device's benefits outweigh those risks before it reaches patients.

In India, medical devices are regulated under rules administered by the central drugs regulator, and devices are classified by risk, from low-risk items to those that sustain life or are implanted. Higher-risk classes face stricter requirements for evidence, quality systems and approval. The details, including which products count as medical devices and what each class needs, change over time, so check the current official rules before you design anything you intend to sell.

What makes something a medical device is usually its intended use, the claims made for it. A wrist band that counts steps for fitness may not be one; the same hardware marketed to detect an irregular heart rhythm probably is. That is why claims are an engineering decision, not just a marketing one.

Safety starts with the hardware

Several safety concerns apply to almost every body-worn or patient-connected device:

  • Electrical isolation: anything connected to a person must never pass a dangerous current through them, even if a fault occurs or the device is plugged into a charger. Patient-connected parts need isolation from mains and from other equipment.
  • Battery safety: lithium cells worn against the skin must be protected against over-charge, over-discharge, short circuit and overheating.
  • Materials: parts in contact with skin should not irritate or sensitise it.
  • Software: a crash, a frozen display showing an old value or a silent failure can mislead. Show clearly when a reading is not valid.

The IEC standards for medical electrical equipment (the IEC 60601 family) and for medical device software set out how to handle these risks; check the editions that apply to your device. Even a student prototype should follow the spirit: assume faults will happen and design so that they fail safe.

Validating accuracy honestly

Testing a device on yourself and three friends and seeing that the numbers "look right" is not validation. Honest validation compares your device against an accepted reference method, on enough people, under the conditions the device will meet.

A good study plan decides in advance:

  • The reference: a recognised instrument or method, used correctly
  • The participants: a range of ages, body types and skin tones that represents future users, since optical sensors in particular can behave differently across skin pigmentation
  • The conditions: at rest and in motion, in heat and cold, at the edges of the measuring range
  • The acceptance criteria: how close is close enough, written down before you collect data

Correlation is a weak measure of agreement: two methods can correlate strongly while one reads consistently high. A standard approach is the Bland-Altman analysis: for each pair of readings, compute the difference, then report the mean difference (bias) and the range within which most differences fall (the limits of agreement). The data below is made up for illustration: heart rate in beats per minute, reference versus a prototype.

import numpy as np

# paired readings, made up for illustration: reference vs our device
ref = np.array([72, 88, 65, 101, 79, 93, 58, 110, 84, 70], float)
dev = np.array([74, 87, 68, 97, 80, 96, 61, 104, 85, 73], float)

diff = dev - ref
bias = diff.mean()
sd = diff.std(ddof=1)
lo, hi = bias - 1.96 * sd, bias + 1.96 * sd
print(f"bias {bias:+.1f}, limits of agreement {lo:+.1f} to {hi:+.1f}")
print("worst at reference", ref[np.argmax(abs(diff))])

It prints a bias of +0.5 bpm and limits of agreement from −5.8 to +6.8 bpm, with the worst error at a reference of 110 bpm. The average looks excellent, but look closer: the two largest errors are at the two highest heart rates, both reading low. That pattern suggests the device under-reads at high rates, exactly where it may matter most. Ten readings are far too few to conclude anything firmly; a real study needs many more participants and a plan reviewed by people with clinical research experience. Studies involving people also need ethics approval and informed consent.

Claims a device can support

The most common mistake is claiming more than the evidence supports. Good practice:

  • state the intended use narrowly and plainly, for example "displays heart rate during rest for general wellness"
  • never call a wellness or student device a diagnostic tool; a wearable classifier is not a diagnosis
  • show uncertainty and invalid readings honestly instead of always displaying a number
  • tell users when to see a doctor regardless of what the device says
  • publish the conditions under which you validated the device, and do not claim accuracy outside them

A cheap device that is honest about its limits can still be useful. A cheap device that overclaims can be dangerous.

Common mistakes

  • Validating only on the development team
  • Reporting correlation instead of agreement
  • Choosing acceptance criteria after seeing the data
  • Ignoring motion, temperature and skin tone effects
  • Letting marketing language turn a wellness gadget into a medical claim

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script above, then plot difference against the mean of each pair with matplotlib to make a Bland-Altman plot, and look for trends.
  2. Write a one-paragraph intended-use statement for a device you are building, and list which claims it rules out.
  3. Draw your prototype's power path in KiCad and mark every place a fault could connect the user to a charger or other equipment.
