Estimating hardware work: ranges, assumptions and the cone of uncertainty

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Why a single-number estimate for hardware work is almost always late, how to give ranges with stated assumptions, how a short simulation shows the real odds, and how estimates should narrow as the design matures.

In this article
  1. Why single numbers run late
  2. Give a range and the assumptions behind it
  3. Let a simulation show the odds
  4. The cone of uncertainty
  5. Calibrate yourself
  6. Common mistakes
  7. Try it yourself

"How long will the board take?" is a fair question with no honest single-number answer. The schematic might go smoothly or reveal that the chosen sensor needs a supply you do not have. The boards might come back on time or sit at a fabricator for a week longer. Bring-up might take a day or a fortnight, depending on one mistake nobody has found yet. Engineers who answer with one number are usually late, not because they are bad at estimating but because one number cannot carry uncertainty. This article shows how to give an estimate that can.

Why single numbers run late

When asked for one number, most people give roughly the most likely duration for each task. That feels reasonable, but task durations are skewed: a task can finish a little early, but it can finish a lot late. Bring-up cannot take less than zero days, yet it can take three times the plan. Adding up most-likely values for a chain of skewed tasks produces a total that the project beats only rarely, because it needs every task to land at or before its most likely value at once.

Hardware adds features that make the skew worse: waits you do not control (fabrication, shipping, part lead times), batch steps where one mistake costs a full cycle (a board respin), and discovery-heavy work such as bring-up, where the duration depends on bugs you have not met.

Give a range and the assumptions behind it

For each task, estimate three numbers: optimistic, most likely and pessimistic. Write them as "most likely 6 days, between 4 and 12". Then write down the assumptions that the range depends on. Assumptions are the part of an estimate people forget, and they are the most useful part, because when one breaks you know immediately that the estimate needs revisiting.

For a small sensor board, illustrative estimates in working days, for a chain of tasks done one after another:

Task Optimistic Most likely Pessimistic
Schematic and review 3 5 9
Layout and review 4 6 12
Fabrication and assembly wait 10 12 20
Bring-up 3 6 15
Integration test with firmware 5 8 14

Assumptions: parts in stock at order time; one layout review cycle; no respin; firmware drivers ready from a development board before the custom board arrives.

The most-likely values add to 37 days. Everyone's instinct is to quote 37. The next section shows why that is optimistic.

Let a simulation show the odds

A few lines of Python can draw a random duration for each task from its range, add them up, and repeat thousands of times. The result is a distribution of possible totals, from which you can read the chance of finishing by any date.

import random

tasks = {                      # (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic)
    "schematic":   (3, 5, 9),
    "layout":      (4, 6, 12),
    "fab wait":    (10, 12, 20),
    "bring-up":    (3, 6, 15),
    "integration": (5, 8, 14),
}
random.seed(1)
runs = sorted(
    sum(random.triangular(o, p, m) for o, m, p in tasks.values())
    for _ in range(20000)
)
for q in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9):
    print(f"P{int(q * 100)}: {runs[int(q * len(runs))]:.0f} days")
ml = sum(m for o, m, p in tasks.values())
hit = sum(r <= ml for r in runs) / len(runs)
print(f"chance of finishing in {ml} days: {hit:.0%}")

It uses a triangular distribution for each task, a simple shape defined by the three estimates. The output is roughly: a median (P50) of 44 days, an 80th percentile (P80) of 48 days, a P90 of 50 days, and only about a 5 % chance of finishing within the 37 days that the most-likely values add up to.

That last figure is the lesson. An estimate of 37 days would almost certainly be missed, not because any task was badly estimated, but because the skew in each task adds up. A better answer to "how long?" is: "about 44 working days, and I would plan on 48 to be fairly confident, assuming parts are in stock and no respin." If a respin is a real possibility, model it as a risk with its own probability rather than burying it in the ranges.

The cone of uncertainty

Early in a project, estimates are wide because the design is unknown. As decisions are made and work is done, uncertainty shrinks. Plotted over time, the range of honest estimates narrows like a cone.

The cone does not narrow by itself. It narrows because work retires uncertainty: a prototype proves the sensor works, a layout review confirms the board fits, a part order confirms stock. Two habits follow. First, re-estimate at each milestone and report the new range, so stakeholders see the cone narrowing. Second, schedule the most uncertain work early, such as a risky sensor or a new interface, because retiring its uncertainty narrows the whole estimate.

Calibrate yourself

Keep a simple log of estimate against actual for every task you do. After a few projects, you will know whether your "most likely" is usually right, or whether you consistently underestimate bring-up by half. That personal correction factor is real data, and it is worth more than any formula.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting the sum of most-likely values. It has a small chance of being met.
  • Estimating only the design work. Reviews, waits, debugging and documentation are often half the duration.
  • Hiding assumptions. When one breaks, nobody knows the estimate is stale.
  • Treating the estimate as a commitment. An estimate is a forecast; plans and commitments are made from it with margin.

Try it yourself

  1. Write optimistic, most-likely and pessimistic estimates for the tasks in your next lab or project, with the assumptions behind them.
  2. Run the script above with your numbers and compare its P50 and P80 with the sum of your most-likely values.
  3. Log the actual duration of each task as you go, and at the end compute your own ratio of actual to most-likely for each kind of task.
