Why owning design IP matters for an electronics ecosystem

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

The difference between assembling electronic products and owning their designs, how owning the design changes who controls cost, features and supply, and what it means for the skills engineers build.

  6. Try it yourself

Two factories can produce the same smart meter, router or fan controller. One buys a complete reference design, assembles boards to it and puts its label on the box. The other designed the board, wrote the firmware and perhaps designed the main chip. Both ship products, both employ people, and from the outside they look similar. But they are in very different positions when a part goes out of stock, a customer wants a new feature or a competitor cuts prices. The difference is who owns the design intellectual property (IP). This article explains what that means and why it matters for engineers and for an electronics ecosystem.

What "design IP" means

In electronics, design IP is the knowledge and the files that define how a product works:

  • Product-level designs: schematics, board layouts, mechanical designs and bills of materials
  • Firmware and software: the code on the device and the tools that configure, test and update it
  • Chip designs: RTL, verification environments, analog blocks, layouts and the test programs that check each chip
  • Know-how: test methods, calibration procedures, failure analysis records and the reasons behind design choices

Ownership here means having the rights, the files and the people who understand them well enough to change them. A licence to build a design without the ability to modify it is not the same thing.

Assembling versus designing

A product passes through a chain of activities, and value is distributed unevenly along it:

Activity What it requires Who controls change
Assembling to someone else's design Manufacturing skill, quality control, logistics The design owner
Customising a reference design Some board and firmware skill Partly the reference provider
Designing the product Systems, board, firmware, test The product company
Designing the key chips Chip design, verification, test The chip company

Assembly is valuable and needs real skill: process control, yield, testing and supply chain management. But an assembler's margin is constrained because the design owner can move production elsewhere. The design owner decides features, chooses components and sets the price of what only it can supply.

What owning the design changes

Owning the design gives a company, and by extension an ecosystem, several kinds of control.

Control over cost. A designer can trade components against each other, remove unnecessary parts and integrate functions into a custom chip once volumes justify it. An assembler can negotiate the price of parts the design specifies, but cannot change the design.

Control over supply. When a component becomes scarce or reaches end of life, the design owner can qualify an alternative and update the design. An assembler waits for the design owner to do so.

Control over features and fit. Local needs, such as wide-input power supplies for unstable grids, a regional language interface, a different radio band or a specific regulatory requirement, can be designed in by someone who owns the design. A reference design built for another market may not accommodate them.

Control over security and trust. For products such as meters, telecom equipment and vehicle electronics, the ability to inspect and change every part of the firmware and hardware matters for security and for long-term maintenance.

What it means for skills

The skills an ecosystem builds follow the work it does. Assembly builds manufacturing, quality and operations skills. Design builds systems engineering, architecture, analog and digital design, verification, firmware, test and the judgement to make trade-offs, which take years of practice on real products to develop.

Those design skills compound. A team that has designed one product's board and firmware is far better placed to design the next, to specify a custom chip for it and to support it in the field. A chip team that has taped out one design understands its next customer's needs better. That is why building design capability tends to be slow at first and then accelerate.

For individual engineers, the implication is practical: learn to own a design end to end, from specification to test. Even on a small project, practise the full loop: write the requirements, design the hardware or RTL, write the firmware, verify it, and document why each decision was made. That is the capability that design-owning companies need most.

Owning designs responsibly

Owning IP also brings obligations:

  • Respect others' IP. Use licensed IP within its terms, check open-source licences before reusing code or hardware designs, and never copy a competitor's design.
  • Keep the design alive. Files nobody understands are not really owned. Keep documentation, version control and design reviews so the knowledge does not walk out of the door with one engineer.
  • Decide what to build and what to buy. Owning everything is neither possible nor wise. Buy or license commodity blocks; put your own design effort where it differentiates the product or reduces a real dependency.

Open-source tools, open standards and open PDKs support all of this: they lower the cost of learning to design, let teams inspect what they depend on and let shared building blocks be reused without restrictive terms.

Try it yourself

  1. Take a product you own, such as a charger or fan regulator, and list which parts of its design you think the brand owns and which it likely bought.
  2. For a small project of yours, write a one-page design record: requirements, key decisions, alternatives considered and test results, as a design owner would.
  3. Read the licence of one open-source hardware project you might reuse, for example a design in KiCad or a core you could simulate with Verilator, and note what it allows and requires.
