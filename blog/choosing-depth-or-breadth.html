Choosing a specialisation after a few years: depth, breadth or both

Careers & skills Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

After a few years in chips or embedded work you must decide whether to go deep, go broad or combine the two. How to judge which skills are scarce, when depth pays, and why adjacent knowledge makes you more effective.

For the first two or three years, the choice mostly makes itself: you learn whatever your project needs, and every month brings new tools and failure modes. Then the learning curve flattens. You are competent at your job, and the question becomes deliberate: become the person who knows one area better than anyone nearby, become the person who can connect several areas, or try to do both. There is no universally right answer, but there is a sound way to reason about it.

Depth, breadth and the shape in between

Depth means mastery of one area, such as static timing signoff, analog data converters, formal verification or low-level boot firmware: knowing not only the procedures but why they exist, where they break and what to do when the tools disagree. Breadth means working knowledge across several neighbouring areas, enough to read their artefacts, ask good questions and anticipate their constraints.

Most effective senior engineers end up with a combination often described as T-shaped: one deep specialism plus working knowledge of the areas it touches. Some develop two deep areas over a long career. The useful question is not "depth or breadth" in the abstract but which deep area, and which adjacent areas around it.

How to judge which skills are scarce

Scarcity is what makes depth valuable. A skill is scarce when it is needed and hard to acquire, and you can judge both without relying on market reports or rumours.

Look for these signals inside your own team and project:

  • Who gets called when things go wrong. If the same one or two people are pulled in whenever a particular kind of problem appears, that knowledge is scarce.
  • Long learning paths. Skills that require years of feedback from real silicon or real field failures, such as analog layout matching, signal integrity, power integrity or post-silicon debug, cannot be learned quickly from a course, so fewer people have them.
  • Problems the tools do not solve. Tasks where automation produces an answer that still needs expert judgement, such as interpreting timing exceptions or deciding whether a formal proof's assumptions are realistic, stay valuable as tools improve.
  • Boundaries nobody owns. Problems that sit between disciplines, for example a firmware bug caused by a hardware reset sequence, often have no natural owner.

Be honest about the opposite signals too. A skill that is mostly the operation of one tool, following a fixed procedure, is the most exposed to better automation and to tool changes. That does not make it worthless, but it is a weak foundation for a deep specialism unless you go beneath the procedure to the reasons behind it.

When to go deep

Depth tends to pay off when three things line up:

  1. You find the problems genuinely interesting after the novelty is gone. Depth takes years; boredom ends it.
  2. The area has hard, recurring problems that reward accumulated experience rather than one-off cleverness.
  3. You have access to real feedback: silicon results, field returns, signoff reviews, senior people who will critique your work.

The third point is often decisive. Expertise grows from closing the loop between your predictions and reality many times. If your current role gives you that loop in one area, that is a strong reason to deepen there. If it does not, going deep means changing projects or roles to get it.

The risk of depth is narrowness: an area can shrink, or move to a different kind of team. You reduce that risk with the horizontal bar of the T.

Why adjacent knowledge makes you better at your specialism

Breadth is not a hedge for its own sake. Knowledge of neighbouring areas directly improves deep work, because most expensive problems happen at boundaries.

A verification engineer who understands synthesis and timing writes assertions that also catch structures likely to cause timing trouble. A firmware engineer who can read a schematic and a datasheet's electrical characteristics diagnoses a "software" brown-out in an hour. A physical design engineer who understands the architecture can push back on a floorplan that will never meet timing, early, with reasons. In each case the adjacent knowledge lets the specialist ask the right question of a colleague and be understood.

The practical test of enough breadth is this: can you read the main artefacts of the neighbouring team, such as their spec, their reports or their schematic, and spot something worth asking about? You do not need to produce those artefacts; you need to understand them well enough to work across the boundary.

A way to decide, and to revisit

Make the decision explicit, write it down and revisit it yearly.

  • List the two or three areas you could deepen, and score each on interest, scarcity signals and access to feedback, honestly and on your own evidence.
  • Choose one for depth. Choose two neighbours for working knowledge.
  • Define what progress looks like in a year: problems you can solve alone, reviews you can lead, a write-up or open-source contribution that shows the depth.
  • Revisit when your project, team or interests change. A specialism is a direction, not a contract.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing by what is fashionable this year rather than by interest and feedback; fashion changes faster than expertise grows.
  • Collecting tool names as breadth. Breadth is understanding another discipline's constraints, not listing its software.
  • Going deep in a procedure, not a principle. Learn why the flow does what it does, so the knowledge survives a tool change.
  • Never deciding. Drifting for years leaves you neither deep nor usefully broad.

Try it yourself

  1. Draw your own T on paper: your current depth, and the adjacent areas where you can and cannot read the neighbouring team's artefacts.
  2. Pick one adjacent area and read one real artefact from it this month, for example an OpenSTA timing report from your design, a KiCad schematic of your board or a Zephyr device-tree file, and write three questions it raises.
  3. Score two candidate specialisms on interest, scarcity signals from your own team and access to feedback, and discuss the result with a senior colleague.
  4. Set one depth goal for the year that produces visible evidence, such as leading a review in that area or contributing a fix to a related open-source tool.
