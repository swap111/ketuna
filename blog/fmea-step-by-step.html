FMEA step by step: severity, occurrence, detection and what to do next

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A walk through a failure mode and effects analysis on a water pump controller, from listing failure modes and rating them to turning the top risks into design changes and tests, then re-scoring.

In this article

  2. Listing and rating failure modes
  3. Ranking and acting on the results

Products rarely fail in the way their designers tested them. A probe gets coated in scale, a relay welds shut after a hard start, firmware freezes when a bus locks up. Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) is a structured way to imagine those failures while the design can still change, rank them, and turn the worst into concrete actions. The spreadsheet is the easy part; the value is in the questions it forces a team to ask.

The running example

We will analyse a controller for a domestic water pump. It reads a level probe in the overhead tank, switches the pump motor through a relay, watches motor current to detect dry running, and shows status on a small display. It is a mains-powered product, so any real work on it needs qualified supervision and the applicable electrical safety standards; here we only use it to practise the method.

Listing and rating failure modes

Step 1: list items, functions and failure modes

Go through the system block by block. For each item, write its function, then every way it could fail to perform that function. Useful prompts are: no output, wrong output, stuck output, intermittent output, output at the wrong time, and output that cannot be stopped. For each failure mode, write:

  • Effect: what the user experiences, not what the circuit does. "Tank overflows and the motor overheats" rather than "relay output high".
  • Cause: the mechanism that produces the failure mode. One mode may have several causes; give each its own row.
  • Current controls: what in today's design prevents the cause or detects the failure before the user suffers it.

Work as a group, with someone from hardware, firmware, test and, ideally, someone who has seen field returns. One person filling in an FMEA alone mostly records what that person already believed.

Step 2: rate severity, occurrence and detection

Each row gets three ratings on a 1 to 10 scale. Agree the scales before rating, with anchors written down, because a 6 means nothing otherwise. A compressed example of anchors, made up for illustration:

Rating Severity (effect) Occurrence (cause) Detection (controls)
9–10 Safety hazard or legal non-compliance Expected often No control; will not be caught
6–8 Product unusable or damaged Occasional Caught only by chance
3–5 Function degraded, user notices Rare Caught by a test with partial coverage
1–2 Barely noticeable Remote Certain to be caught before shipping

Note the direction of detection: a high number means poor detection. The rating describes how likely current controls are to miss the problem.

Here are four rows for the pump controller, with illustrative ratings:

Failure mode and cause Effect S / O / D RPN
Level probe reads full when empty; scale on probe Pump never starts, tank runs dry 6 / 5 / 8 240
Dry run not detected; current threshold set too low Pump runs dry and overheats 8 / 4 / 6 192
Relay contacts weld closed; inrush above rating Pump cannot be stopped; overflow, motor burnout, fire risk 9 / 2 / 9 162
Firmware hangs; I2C bus lock-up during display update Controls freeze in last state 7 / 3 / 3 63

Ranking and acting on the results

Step 3: rank, and do not rank by RPN alone

The risk priority number (RPN) is severity × occurrence × detection. It is a convenient sort key, but it has a known weakness: multiplying ordinal ratings lets a frequent nuisance outrank a rare catastrophe. In the table above the welded relay ranks third by RPN, yet it is the only row with a fire risk. The common rule is to act on any row with severity 9 or 10 regardless of RPN, then work down the rest. Some FMEA methods replace RPN with an action-priority lookup that weights severity first for exactly this reason.

rows = [
    ("probe fouled",     6, 5, 8),
    ("dry run missed",   8, 4, 6),
    ("relay welded",     9, 2, 9),
    ("firmware hang",    7, 3, 3),
]

def priority(r):
    name, s, o, d = r
    return (s >= 9, s * o * d)   # severity gate first, then RPN

for name, s, o, d in sorted(rows, key=priority, reverse=True):
    flag = "  <- severity gate" if s >= 9 else ""
    print(f"{name:16s} S={s} O={o} D={d} RPN={s*o*d}{flag}")

The script puts the relay first, then the probe, the dry-run detection and the firmware hang.

Step 4: turn risks into actions

An FMEA row is unfinished until it has an action with an owner and a date. Actions reduce one of the three ratings, and the cheapest reduction is usually different for each:

  • Reduce severity by changing the design so the failure does less harm. For the welded relay: add an independent thermal cut-out on the motor so a stuck relay cannot burn it out. Severity drops because the worst effect is now a stopped pump, not a fire.
  • Reduce occurrence by removing the cause. Choose a relay rated for the motor's inrush current with derating, or add inrush limiting.
  • Improve detection by adding a monitor or a test. For the fouled probe: firmware checks that the tank level changes within a set time after the pump starts, and raises a fault if it does not. For the dry-run threshold: a production test at low and high supply voltage, not just nominal.

Detection improvements are tempting because they are often firmware-only, but they do not make the failure less harmful. Where severity is high, prefer a design change.

Step 5: re-score and keep it alive

After each action is implemented and verified, re-rate the row. Suppose the probe plausibility check is in place and tested: detection might fall from 8 to 3, and the RPN from 240 to 90. Record both scores, so a reviewer can see what the action bought. Revisit the FMEA at each design milestone and whenever a field return reveals a failure mode nobody listed; that new row is the most valuable one in the sheet.

Common mistakes

  • Filling the sheet after the design is frozen. Findings that cannot change anything are paperwork. Run it at architecture and again at detailed design.
  • "Detection: testing" with no named test. Write which test, and what fraction of the failure it would catch.
  • Effects written in circuit terms. Severity is about the user; describe what they experience.
  • Rating without anchors. Two people's 5s will differ, and the ranking becomes noise.

Try it yourself

  1. Pick a product you know, such as a phone charger or an electric kettle, and write six FMEA rows in LibreOffice Calc, including at least one with severity 9 or above.
  2. Run the script above on your rows and compare the RPN order with the severity-gated order. Discuss with a teammate whether the difference makes sense.
  3. For your top two rows, write one design action and one test action each, then re-score them as if the actions were done, and note which rating each action actually moved.
