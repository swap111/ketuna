A PCB design review checklist for first boards

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A practical checklist for reviewing a first circuit board before you order it, covering power, decoupling, clocks, reset and boot, debug access, connectors and assembly, with the reason behind each check.

  2. Power and decoupling

The most expensive moment in a board's life is the few seconds after you click "order". Every mistake still in the files will be faithfully copied onto every board, and the next chance to fix it is weeks away. A design review before ordering, with a second pair of eyes and a checklist, catches the mistakes that the designer has stopped being able to see. This checklist is aimed at first boards: the kind where the goal is a working bring-up, not a perfect product.

How to run the review

A checklist works best inside a short, prepared review. Send the schematic PDF, the layout plots and the bill of materials a day ahead, so reviewers read before the meeting. In the meeting, a scribe records each finding with a severity (must fix before ordering, should fix, question) and an owner. Phrase findings as observation, impact and question: "U3's enable pin floats; the regulator may not start. Should it be tied to VIN?" That keeps the discussion on the design, not the designer.

The checks below are grouped by the questions that most often decide whether a first board comes up.

Power and decoupling

Power

  • Input protection. What happens if the supply is connected backwards, or a higher voltage is applied? A series diode or a reverse-protection MOSFET and a fuse or polyfuse are cheap insurance on a bench board.
  • Every regulator's datasheet requirements. Input and output capacitor values and types, enable pin state, minimum load, feedback divider values. Recompute the feedback divider by hand; it is a common source of a wrong output voltage.
  • Current capacity. Add up the worst-case load on each rail, including radios transmitting and motors starting, and compare with the regulator rating and its thermal limit at your input voltage.
  • Power sequencing. If any chip requires its core rail before its I/O rail, or the reverse, check the order and timing.
  • Test points on every rail and a way to measure current, such as a jumper or a zero-ohm link in series, so you can bring rails up one at a time.

Decoupling

  • A small capacitor at every supply pin, placed close with short connections to the power and ground planes. The reasons and the placement rules are covered in the decoupling article; here, check that the layout follows them.
  • Bulk capacitance near each regulator output and near loads that draw current in bursts.
  • Analogue supplies for ADCs, references and PLLs filtered as the datasheet recommends.

Clocks, reset and boot

Clocks

  • Crystal load capacitors computed, not copied. For a crystal specified with load capacitance CL, and two equal capacitors C on each pin, the crystal sees the two in series plus stray capacitance: CL = C/2 + Cstray. For CL = 12 pF and an assumed Cstray of 3 pF, C = 2 × (12 − 3) = 18 pF. Using the 22 pF values from some other board would pull the frequency off target.
  • Crystal close to its pins, with a ground guard and no fast signals routed underneath.
  • Clock outputs with series termination where traces are long.

Reset, boot and configuration

  • Reset pin with the pull-up and capacitor the datasheet asks for, and a reset button on a first board.
  • Boot-mode and strap pins tied to defined levels with resistors, not left floating. Many chips sample these at power-up; a floating strap can boot the chip into the wrong mode on some boards but not others.
  • Pins with default functions that could fight your circuit at power-up, before firmware configures them.

Debug access, connectors and layout

Debug access

  • A programming and debug connector (SWD, JTAG or the chip's equivalent) wired to the right pins, with a pinout matching the probe you own.
  • A UART console brought to a header for printing during bring-up.
  • Spare GPIOs to a header or test pads, so you can toggle pins and measure timing with a logic analyser.
  • LEDs on power rails and one on a GPIO. Seeing life at power-on saves a lot of guessing.

Connectors and interfaces

  • Pinouts checked against the mating part, not just against your own symbol. Mirror-image errors are common when two boards face each other, and on ribbon cables.
  • Pin 1 and polarity marked on the silkscreen and clear when the connector is fitted.
  • Signal levels compatible on both sides: a 5 V output into a 3.3 V input needs level shifting unless the input is tolerant.
  • ESD protection on anything a person can touch: USB, external buttons, sensor cables.
  • I2C pull-ups present once, not on every board on the bus, and addresses free of conflicts.

Layout and assembly

  • Design rule check run with your fabricator's actual rules, with zero unexplained violations.
  • Footprints checked against part drawings, especially pin 1, pitch and the exposed pad of QFN parts.
  • Mounting holes clear of tracks and matched to the enclosure.
  • Silkscreen readable: reference designators, polarity, board name, revision and date.
  • Assembly considerations such as part spacing, fiducials and panelisation, described in the DFM article.

Common mistakes

  • Reviewing only the schematic. Many errors live in the layout: a missing via, a split plane under a fast signal, a swapped footprint.
  • Copying reference designs without checking assumptions. Their load capacitors, current limits and strap settings were chosen for their board.
  • No way to isolate rails. If one short pulls down the supply, you cannot find it without cutting traces.
  • Skipping the review because the board is "just a prototype". Prototype respins cost the same weeks.

Try it yourself

  1. Open a KiCad project and run both the electrical rules check in the schematic editor and the design rules check in the board editor; explain or fix every warning.
  2. Print this checklist and review a classmate's board with it, recording each finding with a severity and an owner, then swap roles.
  3. Pick the crystal on a board you have and compute its load capacitors from its datasheet value of CL, then compare with what was fitted.
