Array, Booth and Wallace: how hardware multipliers trade area for speed

Digital & VLSI design Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A multiplier generates partial products, reduces them to two rows and adds those. Compare array reduction, radix-4 Booth recoding and carry-save Wallace and Dadda trees, and where each spends area and delay.

In this article

  2. Carry-save reduction trees

Every hardware multiplier, however it is drawn, does three things: it generates partial products, it reduces them to two rows, and it adds those two rows with a fast carry-propagate adder. The named architectures differ in how they handle the first two steps. Booth recoding attacks the number of partial products; Wallace and Dadda trees attack the depth of the reduction. Knowing where each spends area and delay lets you read a synthesis report intelligently and decide when a custom structure is worth the effort.

Partial products and the array multiplier

For an N × N unsigned multiply, partial product i is the multiplicand ANDed with bit i of the multiplier and shifted left by i. That is N rows of N bits, an N2 array of AND gates.

The array multiplier reduces the rows one at a time: row 0 plus row 1, then that sum plus row 2, and so on, using a grid of full adders. Its layout is beautifully regular, which mattered for hand-drawn layouts, and its wiring is local. Its weakness is delay. With ripple carries inside each row the critical path runs diagonally across the array, and even with carry-save rows (passing carries down instead of along) the reduction still takes about N adder levels. Delay grows linearly with width.

Signed (two's complement) operands add a complication: the top partial product carries negative weight. Schemes such as Baugh-Wooley rearrange the sign bits so the array still uses only positive additions plus a few constant correction bits.

Carry-save reduction trees

The key observation is that a full adder is a 3:2 compressor: it takes three bits of the same weight and outputs two bits, a sum of the same weight and a carry of the next weight, without propagating anything sideways. A layer of full adders applied across all columns therefore turns every three rows into two, in one full-adder delay, regardless of width.

Applying layers repeatedly shrinks the row count by about a factor of 1.5 per layer:

Rows of partial products Reduction layers to reach 2 rows
4 2
8 4
16 6

(The sequence of row counts is 8 → 6 → 4 → 3 → 2 for 8 rows.) That is logarithmic growth instead of linear. A Wallace tree compresses as aggressively as possible at every layer; a Dadda tree compresses only as much as needed to hit the next target height in the sequence 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 13 …, which uses fewer half adders and a slightly cheaper final adder for the same number of layers. Both end with two rows that a fast carry-propagate adder, often a prefix adder, combines into the product.

The price is irregularity. Wires from one layer to the next cross columns, and the tree is much harder to lay out by hand than an array. Modern place-and-route handles this, which is why trees are the usual choice in synthesised multipliers. Compressors larger than 3:2, such as 4:2 cells, are sometimes used to make the tree more regular.

Radix-4 Booth recoding

The tree shortens the reduction; Booth recoding shrinks what needs reducing. Instead of one partial product per multiplier bit, radix-4 Booth takes the multiplier two bits at a time, looking at one overlapping bit from the pair below, and maps each group to a digit in {−2, −1, 0, +1, +2}:

Bits y2i+1 y2i y2i−1 Digit
000, 111 0
001, 010 +1
011 +2
100 −2
101, 110 −1

Each digit selects 0, ±M or ±2M, where M is the multiplicand. All of these are cheap: 2M is a one-bit shift, and −M is the inverted bits plus a 1 that is added as an extra bit in the reduction tree. An N-bit multiplier produces about N/2 partial products instead of N.

Work it on a small case. Take the 8-bit multiplier 115, binary 0111_0011, with an implied 0 below bit 0. The groups, from the least significant end, are 110, 001, 110, 011, giving digits −1, +1, −1, +2. Check: −1 + 1 × 4 − 1 × 16 + 2 × 64 = 115. So M × 115 becomes four partial products: −M, +4M, −16M and +128M.

def booth_digits(y, n):
    """Radix-4 Booth digits of an n-bit two's complement y, LSB first."""
    y &= (1 << n) - 1
    digits, prev = [], 0               # prev is bit y[2i-1]
    for i in range(0, n, 2):
        b0 = (y >> i) & 1
        b1 = (y >> (i + 1)) & 1
        digits.append(-2 * b1 + b0 + prev)
        prev = b1
    return digits

d = booth_digits(115, 8)
print(d, sum(v * 4**i for i, v in enumerate(d)))   # [-1, 1, -1, 2] 115

Booth recoding also handles two's complement multipliers naturally: the top group carries the sign. For 8-bit operands, the tree now reduces about four rows (plus the extra negation bits, which can add one short row) instead of eight, saving one or two layers and roughly half the partial-product adders. The cost is the recoder and a 5-way selection mux per partial-product bit, which is more complex than an AND gate.

Comparing the three

Architecture Reduction delay Area and wiring
Array Grows linearly with N Regular, local wires
Wallace / Dadda tree Grows with log N Irregular wiring
Booth + tree Tree on about N/2 rows Recoders and muxes added

For wide multipliers, such as 16 bits and up, Booth plus a tree is the common choice where speed matters, because the halved row count saves more than the recoding costs. For narrow multipliers, a plain tree is often enough, and Booth's mux overhead may not pay for itself. For low-power designs, the choice is less clear: trees can glitch heavily because paths through them are unbalanced, and those glitches cost dynamic power.

What this means when you write a * b

Synthesis tools generate multipliers from a * operator, choosing the structure from the timing constraint and the operand widths. A hand-built multiplier is rarely worth it unless you have measured that the tool's result misses timing or area, or you need a property the tool will not give you, such as a pipeline register in the middle of the tree. Even then, compare your design against the tool's result on the same constraints before keeping it.

Try it yourself

  1. Recode three multipliers of your choice by hand, including a negative one, then check them with the booth_digits function above.
  2. Write an 8 × 8 array multiplier and a Dadda-tree multiplier in Verilog, and check both against a * b exhaustively (all 65,536 input pairs) in Icarus Verilog or Verilator.
  3. Synthesise both and a plain a * b with Yosys against a sky130 library, then compare cell counts with stat and critical paths in OpenSTA.
