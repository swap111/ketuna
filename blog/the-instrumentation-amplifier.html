The instrumentation amplifier: measuring tiny differences on large common-mode signals

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier gives high input impedance, gain set by one resistor and strong common-mode rejection. Learn how each stage works and why resistor matching sets the rejection.

In this article

A strain-gauge bridge on a 5 V excitation produces a few millivolts of signal sitting on about 2.5 V. A current-sense resistor in a supply line produces a small voltage drop that may sit near the supply rail. In both cases you want the small difference and nothing of the large common level. A single op-amp difference amplifier can do this in principle, but it loads the source and its rejection depends on four resistors matching perfectly. The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier fixes both problems, and understanding why tells you exactly what limits its performance.

Start with the plain difference amplifier

One op-amp with four resistors makes a difference amplifier. The inverting input connects to input V1 through Ra and to the output through Rb. The non-inverting input connects to input V2 through Rc and to a reference (often ground) through Rd. If Rb/Ra = Rd/Rc = Gd, the output is Gd(V2 − V1), and any voltage common to both inputs cancels.

Two weaknesses follow from the structure.

  • Low input impedance. Each input sees a resistor of tens of kilohms or less. A sensor with significant source resistance adds to Ra or Rc, which changes the ratios and spoils both gain and rejection.
  • Rejection depends on matching. Cancellation relies on the two ratios being exactly equal. Any mismatch lets part of the common-mode voltage through.

How much? If each resistor can be off by a fraction ε, the worst-case common-mode rejection ratio is approximately:

CMRR ≈ (1 + Gd) / (4ε)

For a unity-gain difference amplifier with 1 % resistors, that is 2 / 0.04 = 50, or about 34 dB. A 2.5 V common-mode level would leak through as 50 mV, larger than the bridge signal. With 0.1 % resistors it improves to 500, about 54 dB. Matched resistor networks or laser-trimmed on-chip resistors do better still.

Add a front stage

The instrumentation amplifier puts two op-amps, A1 and A2, in front of the difference amplifier.

  • V2 drives the non-inverting input of A1, and V1 drives the non-inverting input of A2. Each source now sees only an op-amp input, so input impedance is very high.
  • A1's output connects back to its inverting input through a resistor Rf; A2's does the same through an equal Rf. A single gain resistor RG connects the two inverting inputs together.

Each op-amp forces its inverting input to follow its non-inverting input, so the full input difference V2 − V1 appears across RG. The resulting current flows through both Rf resistors as well, so the difference between A1's and A2's outputs is (V2 − V1)(1 + 2Rf/RG). The difference amplifier then takes this differential output and adds its own gain:

G = (1 + 2Rf / RG) × Gd

Worked example: with Rf = 10 kΩ and a unity-gain difference stage, a total gain of 100 needs 1 + 20 kΩ/RG = 100, so RG = 20 kΩ / 99 ≈ 202 Ω. Changing one resistor changes the gain, and in integrated instrumentation amplifiers RG is often the only external part.

Why the front stage improves rejection

Here is the key property. Apply a pure common-mode input, V1 = V2 = Vcm. Both inverting inputs sit at Vcm, so no voltage appears across RG, no current flows through it, and no current flows through the Rf resistors. Both outputs simply equal Vcm. The front stage passes common mode at unity gain, whatever its own gain setting and however well its two Rf resistors match.

The differential signal, meanwhile, is amplified by (1 + 2Rf/RG). The difference amplifier then sees a signal that is G1 times larger relative to the common mode it has to reject. Its resistor-limited rejection is unchanged, but referred back to the input it is multiplied by the front-stage gain. With G1 = 100 and the 1 % difference stage above, the overall resistor-limited CMRR improves from 34 dB to roughly 74 dB.

That is why instrumentation amplifiers put most of their gain in the first stage. It is also why their CMRR specification usually rises with gain.

How spread really behaves

The worst-case formula assumes every resistor sits at its tolerance limit in the worst direction. A quick Monte Carlo shows the typical case:

import numpy as np

def diff_amp_cmrr(tol, gain=1.0, n=100_000, seed=0):
    """CMRR of a four-resistor difference amplifier with random tolerances."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    r = 10e3 * (1 + rng.uniform(-tol, tol, size=(4, n)))
    r_in_n, r_fb = r[0], r[1] * gain      # inverting side
    r_in_p, r_gnd = r[2], r[3] * gain     # non-inverting side
    a_cm = r_gnd / (r_in_p + r_gnd) * (1 + r_fb / r_in_n) - r_fb / r_in_n
    a_dm = gain
    return 20 * np.log10(a_dm / np.abs(a_cm))

for tol in (0.01, 0.001):
    cmrr = diff_amp_cmrr(tol)
    print(f"+/-{tol:.1%} resistors: worst {cmrr.min():.1f} dB, "
          f"median {np.median(cmrr):.1f} dB")

With resistors spread uniformly over ±1 %, the worst of 100,000 trials is about 34.5 dB, close to the worst-case bound, while the median is about 47.9 dB. With ±0.1 % resistors the figures are about 54.5 dB and 67.9 dB. Designing to the median would leave a fraction of boards failing; that is why datasheets quote minimum CMRR, and why you should too.

Practical limits beyond resistor matching

  • Op-amp CMRR. Each op-amp has finite rejection of its own. At high gain the resistor term is suppressed, and op-amp rejection can dominate.
  • CMRR falls with frequency. Stray capacitances on the two sides of the circuit do not match, so rejection degrades as frequency rises. Interference at mains frequency is usually fine; switching-regulator noise at hundreds of kilohertz may not be.
  • Input common-mode range. The front-stage outputs carry Vcm plus the amplified signal; at high gain, they can hit a rail before the final output does. Check the internal nodes, not just the output.
  • Reference pin. The output is referred to the difference amplifier's reference terminal. Driving it from a resistor divider adds resistance to Rd and ruins the matching; drive it from a low-impedance buffer.
  • Source imbalance. Even with high input impedance, unbalanced source impedances combined with input capacitance convert common mode to differential at high frequency.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the Monte Carlo script with numpy, then set gain=10 and see how the difference amplifier's own gain changes the CMRR spread.
  2. In ngspice, build the three-op-amp circuit with ideal op-amps (high-gain voltage-controlled sources) and RG = 202 Ω. Apply 1 mV differential on a 2.5 V common level and check the output.
  3. Change one difference-stage resistor by 1 %, drive a pure 1 V common-mode input, and measure the output. Then raise the front-stage gain and confirm that the input-referred common-mode error falls in proportion.
