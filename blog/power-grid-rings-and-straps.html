Designing a power grid: rings, straps and why IR drop starts at the floorplan

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Rings, straps and rails carry supply current from the pads to every cell. Learn how the grid is laid out, why its resistance sets the IR drop, and why it is far easier to plan early than to repair after routing.

Every standard cell needs a solid connection to VDD and ground, and every one of those connections is made of metal with resistance. The power distribution network (PDN) is the structure that carries current from the supply pins to millions of cell rails. It is laid down at the floorplan, before a single signal wire exists, and that timing is the whole point: once routing has filled the metal layers, there is nowhere left to make the grid stronger.

The three layers of the grid

A typical digital block's power grid has three parts, from coarse to fine.

  • Rings run around the core boundary, one for VDD and one for ground, usually on two upper metal layers (one horizontal, one vertical). They collect current from the supply pins or the chip-level grid and spread it around the block's edge.
  • Straps (also called stripes) run across the core at a regular pitch, on upper metal layers, connected to the rings at both ends. A vertical set on one layer and a horizontal set on another form a mesh.
  • Rails (follow-pins) run along every placement row on the lowest metal layer, alternating VDD and ground. Standard cells abut these rails, so each cell connects by simply being placed. Stacks of vias drop current from the straps down to the rails wherever they cross.

Why the grid's resistance becomes a timing problem

Current through resistance drops voltage. A cell at the centre of the block sees a supply lower than the voltage at the pins by the sum of the drops along its path: ring, strap, vias, rail. That loss is IR drop. A cell running on a lower supply switches more slowly, so IR drop eats into timing margin, and in the worst case causes functional failure.

A simple, illustrative calculation shows how layout choices matter. Take one strap 200 µm long and 1.6 µm wide, on a metal with an assumed sheet resistance of 0.05 Ω per square (made up for illustration; take real values from your PDK).

  • Number of squares = 200 / 1.6 = 125.
  • Strap resistance = 125 × 0.05 = 6.25 Ω.

Suppose the cells along this strap draw 10 mA in total, spread evenly along its length.

  • Fed from one end only, the worst drop at the far end is I × R / 2 = 0.010 × 6.25 / 2 ≈ 31 mV.
  • Fed from both ends (connected to the ring at top and bottom), the worst point is the middle and the drop is I × R / 8 ≈ 7.8 mV.

Connecting both ends cuts the worst drop by a factor of four. Halving the strap pitch (twice as many straps, each carrying half the current) halves it again. Doubling the width halves the resistance. These are all floorplan-time decisions, and each costs routing tracks that signals can no longer use.

Planning the grid early

Because the grid competes with signals for metal, sizing it is a trade-off you make at the start:

  1. Estimate the current. From an early power estimate, divide by the supply voltage to get the average current, and allow for higher peak current when many flops switch on the same clock edge.
  2. Pick a target drop. Many teams budget a small fraction of the supply for static IR drop; the exact budget depends on your timing margins and your sign-off rules.
  3. Choose strap layers, width and pitch so the estimated drop meets the target, then check with an IR analysis tool after placement, when cell positions and power are known.
  4. Count the via stacks. A strap is only as good as its connections to the rails. Too few or too small vias add resistance that the strap calculation ignores, and they also carry high current density.
  5. Leave space around macros so their power pins connect directly to straps.

OpenROAD's power grid generator (pdngen) builds this structure from a short description of layers, widths, pitches and offsets, and LibreLane exposes these as configuration variables. The grid is then checked by OpenROAD's power analysis tool (psm) for IR drop.

Why repairs after routing are hard

If IR analysis after routing shows a hot spot, the obvious fix is a wider strap or an extra one through that region. But the tracks are now full of signal wires. Adding a strap means ripping up and rerouting those signals, which changes their parasitics and timing, which may need new buffers, which draw more current. Sometimes the cheaper fix is to spread out the power-hungry cells, which means going back to placement.

At the floorplan, the same strap costs one line of configuration. That asymmetry is why experienced designers make the grid slightly stronger than the first estimate requires: a little routing capacity spent early is cheaper than a loop back from sign-off.

Common mistakes

  • Rings only, no straps. The centre of a large block is then fed only through thin rails, and the drop there is large.
  • Straps not connected to the ring at both ends. As the calculation shows, single-ended feeding quadruples the worst drop.
  • Ignoring macro power pins. A memory with a weak connection can see the worst drop in the block.
  • Checking only average current. Simultaneous switching causes short, deeper dips that static analysis does not show.

Try it yourself

  1. Run a small design through LibreLane on sky130 and open the floorplan in the OpenROAD GUI or KLayout. Identify the ring, straps, rails and via stacks.
  2. Change the PDN strap pitch variables in the configuration (check the LibreLane variable reference for the exact names in your version), rerun, and compare the IR drop report.
  3. Repeat the calculation above in Python for your strap widths and lengths, using sheet resistances from the PDK documentation, and compare with the tool's result.
