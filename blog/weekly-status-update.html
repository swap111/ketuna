Writing a weekly status update that managers and peers can scan

Careers & skills Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A good weekly status update fits in a few lines, done, next, blocked and risks, each backed by a link to evidence rather than adjectives. Here is the format, a worked example and the mistakes to avoid.

Every engineering team asks for some form of weekly status update. Many engineers treat it as a chore and write either too little ("working on FIFO") or a long diary of effort. Both waste the reader's time. A status update is read by people deciding where to help, what to worry about and whether a date is still realistic. Written well, it takes a few minutes to write and under a minute to read, and it is one of the most visible things you produce in your first year.

Who reads it and what they decide

Before writing, think about the readers. Your manager wants to know whether your work is on track and whether anything needs their action. Your peers want to know whether something they depend on is ready or delayed. A lead combining several updates wants lines they can copy into a summary without rewriting.

None of them wants to know how hard you worked. They want to know what changed, what will change next, and what might go wrong. That leads directly to the format.

Four headings, a few lines each

Use the same four headings every week, in the same order, so readers know where to look:

  • Done: what finished this week, each item with evidence
  • Next: what you plan to finish next week, as outcomes
  • Blocked: anything stopping you, with who can unblock it
  • Risks: things that are not blocking yet but could affect a date

Start with a one-line summary above the headings: on track, at risk or blocked, and the single main reason. Many readers will read only that line, so make it accurate. The whole update should usually stay under about 200 words.

Evidence instead of adjectives

The most important habit is to replace adjectives with links to evidence. "Made good progress on verification" cannot be checked and means different things to different readers. "Scoreboard test passes on 20 seeds (link to CI run)" can be checked in one click and means one thing.

Evidence can be a commit or pull request, a CI run, a test result, a coverage report, a review record, a measurement, or a document. If an item has no evidence, ask whether it is really done. "Wrote the RTL" without a passing test is usually not done; it is in progress.

The same applies to plans. "Continue working on UART" is not a plan. "UART TX passes the receiver-model test at 115200 and 9600 baud" is a plan you can be held to, and that you can report as done or not done next week.

A worked example

Here is a weekly update for a student working on a small FIFO block in a team project. The names and links are illustrative.

Week 6 - sync FIFO block
Status: AT RISK - full flag fails one corner case, fix in review.

Done
- RTL lint clean under Verilator -Wall (CI run #41)
- Scoreboard test: 18 of 20 seeds pass (CI run #42)
- Spec v0.3 updated with simultaneous read/write rule (PR #17)

Next
- All 20 seeds pass, including write-when-full (target Wed)
- Coverage report showing full and empty both reached (target Fri)

Blocked
- None

Risks
- Integration with the UART block needs its final interface;
  asked the UART owner for v1.0 by Thursday. If late, integration
  test moves to next week.

Look at what a reader gets in a few seconds. The status line is honest: at risk, with the reason. Every "done" item has a link. The plan is two checkable outcomes with days. The risk names a dependency, the person involved, a date and the consequence if it slips. Nothing describes effort.

Notice also that the student reported the failing corner case openly. Bad news reported early gives the team options: someone can review the fix today, or the integration plan can adjust. Bad news discovered on the deadline leaves no options at all.

Making it a five-minute habit

The update is easy to write if you collect material during the week rather than reconstructing it on Friday afternoon. Keep a short running note: each time a test passes, a review closes or a problem appears, add one line with the link. On Friday, most of the update is already written; you only choose what matters and sort it under the headings.

Compare against last week's "next" list before writing. Each item there is now either done, still in progress, or dropped, and readers should be able to see which. If something you promised did not happen, say so in one line with the reason, and either move it forward or remove it. Quietly dropping items from the plan teaches readers that your plans are not reliable.

Finally, send it at the same time every week. Readers then come to expect it, and a missing update is noticed quickly.

Common mistakes

  • Effort instead of results. "Spent all week debugging" tells the reader nothing about whether the bug is fixed.
  • Hiding problems. A status line that says "on track" until the week of the deadline destroys trust faster than any bug.
  • Vague plans. "Continue verification" cannot be checked next week; name the outcome.
  • No evidence. Every claim the reader cannot check becomes a follow-up question in a meeting.
  • Long paragraphs. Readers scan; bullets under fixed headings are faster to read than prose.
  • Blockers without an owner. "Waiting on interface spec" should say whose spec and by when.

Try it yourself

  1. Write this week's status for a lab or project you are doing, using the four headings, a status line and at most 200 words. Count the words.
  2. For each "done" item, add a link or reference to evidence: a commit in Git, a test log, a report. Remove or move to "next" any item you cannot back up.
  3. Set up a simple make target or script that runs your tests and prints PASS or FAIL, so next week's evidence is one command away.
  4. Ask a teammate to read your update for thirty seconds and tell you the status and the main risk. If they cannot, rewrite the top line.
