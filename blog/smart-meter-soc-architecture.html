Architecture of a smart energy meter SoC: core, metering engine, security and communications

Computer architecture & RISC-V Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Why a smart meter SoC splits its work between a fixed-function metering engine, a small processor core, secure storage and communication interfaces, with a worked energy calculation and the reasoning behind each block.

An electricity meter looks like a simple product: measure energy, show a number, report it. A smart meter adds remote reading, time-of-use tariffs, tamper detection, firmware updates and secure communication, and it must keep measuring correctly for many years, through power cuts and attacks, on very little power. Designing the SoC inside it is a good lesson in architecture, because the right answer is not "a faster processor" but a careful split of responsibilities. This article walks through that split.

The blocks and why each exists

  • Metering engine. Analog-to-digital converters (typically sigma-delta types, which suit high-resolution, low-bandwidth signals) sample the voltage and current of each phase continuously. Fixed-function digital logic filters the samples, multiplies voltage by current, and accumulates active and reactive energy, RMS values and frequency into registers.
  • Processor core. A small 32-bit microcontroller-class core runs everything that is policy rather than physics: tariff schedules, the display, event logs, the communication protocol stack, firmware update and housekeeping.
  • Security block. Hardware cryptography and protected key storage for authenticated, encrypted communication and for verifying firmware images before they run.
  • Non-volatile memory. Stores cumulative energy registers, configuration and event logs so they survive power loss.
  • Real-time clock. Keeps time for time-of-use tariffs and timestamped events, with a backup battery so time is not lost during outages.
  • Communication and user interface. An optical port for local reading, a UART or SPI link to a separate communication module (radio, power-line or cellular, chosen per deployment), and a segment LCD driver.

Why metering gets its own hardware

The arithmetic is not demanding. As an illustration, sampling voltage and current at 4 kHz per phase needs 4000 multiply-accumulates per second per phase, which a small core could do easily. The reasons for a fixed-function engine are elsewhere:

  • It must never stop. Energy is accumulated sample by sample. If the firmware is busy with a protocol message, handling an interrupt storm or being updated, a software meter would miss samples. A hardware engine keeps counting regardless of what the core is doing.
  • Metrology must be isolated from firmware changes. A meter's accuracy is certified. If measurement lives in firmware, every firmware update can affect the certified function and may require recertification. Keeping metrology in a fixed block with a narrow register interface lets communication and tariff firmware evolve without touching it. Check the current rules of the certifying authority for what separation they require.
  • Power and determinism. A small dedicated datapath clocked just fast enough uses less energy than a core running a software loop, and its timing never varies.

A worked energy calculation

Take one phase at 230 V RMS, 5 A RMS and a power factor of 0.9 (illustrative values). Active power is 230 × 5 × 0.9 = 1035 W. At 4000 samples per second, each sample contributes on average 1035 / 4000 = 0.25875 J to the energy accumulator. One kilowatt-hour is 3.6 × 106 J, so at this load the accumulator gains one kWh after about 13.9 million samples, a little under an hour.

Meters often also emit a test pulse (an LED) at a fixed number of pulses per kWh so that test equipment can check accuracy. With an illustrative constant of 3200 pulses per kWh, each pulse represents 3.6 × 106 / 3200 = 1125 J, and at 1035 W the LED blinks 3312 times an hour. The metering engine generates these pulses directly from its accumulator, so the check exercises the same path that bills the customer.

The accumulator must be wide enough never to overflow between the moments when firmware moves its contents into the long-term energy register, and the engine must use fixed-point scaling chosen so that rounding errors stay far below the accuracy class. Both are decisions to make on paper, with numbers, before RTL.

Power failure and tamper

Two events shape the rest of the architecture.

Power failure. When mains drops, the meter has a short time, powered by stored charge, to save its state. A brownout detector interrupts the core early, the core writes the latest energy registers and a timestamped event to non-volatile memory, and the system shuts down cleanly. The hold-up time, the amount to save and the memory's write time must be budgeted together; this is a hardware-software co-design problem, not an afterthought.

Tamper. Meters face deliberate interference: opening the cover, strong magnets near current sensors, reversed connections. Cover switches and magnetic-field sensors feed the SoC as interrupt sources, and the metering engine itself can flag anomalies such as reverse energy or a large imbalance between phase and neutral current. Each event is logged with the RTC timestamp and protected against later erasure.

Standards to look up

Meter data models and communication commonly follow an internationally standardised metering protocol family (IEC 62056, often called DLMS/COSEM), and national requirements are set in the relevant Indian Standard (IS) for smart meters. Read the current editions for accuracy classes, tamper events and communication requirements; they shape the register map and the security design directly.

Common mistakes

  • Putting metrology in firmware to save area. It ties every firmware update to certified accuracy.
  • Under-budgeting power-fail hold-up. If the save does not finish, energy is lost.
  • Sharing key storage with general memory. A firmware bug then becomes a key leak.
  • Choosing one communication technology on-chip. Keeping the radio or modem as an external module lets one SoC serve many deployments.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a Python model that generates sampled voltage and current with a phase shift, accumulates V × I, and checks that the energy matches Vrms × Irms × power factor × time.
  2. Implement the accumulator and pulse generator in Verilog, simulate in Icarus Verilog against the Python model, and check that the pulse count matches.
  3. Write a power-fail budget in a spreadsheet or Python: bytes to save, memory write time, hold-up capacitance and the voltage at which the brownout fires.
