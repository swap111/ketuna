Running a trade study: criteria, weights and honest scoring

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A weighted trade study picks between architectures with criteria and weights agreed before scoring, a sensitivity check on the weights, and a written rationale that says what would change the answer.

Most architecture arguments are not really about the options. They are about criteria nobody has written down: one engineer is optimising for cost, another for power, a third for the schedule, and each is right by their own measure. A trade study makes those measures explicit, scores the options against them in the open and records why the team chose what it chose. Done honestly, it ends the argument; done carelessly, it dresses up a favourite in a spreadsheet.

The parts of a trade study

A trade study has five ingredients, and the order in which you prepare them matters more than the arithmetic:

  1. The decision, stated narrowly: "how to power the field sensor node", not "the architecture".
  2. The options, each described well enough that someone else could estimate its cost and behaviour. Include the boring option; it often wins.
  3. The criteria, each tied to a requirement or a project constraint, with a direction (higher is better or lower is better).
  4. The weights, agreed with the people who own those requirements, before anyone scores.
  5. Scoring anchors: a short description of what a 1, a 3 and a 5 mean for each criterion.

Fixing weights before scoring is the single most important rule. If you score first, it is very hard not to nudge the weights until your preferred option comes out on top, and the result says more about your preference than about the options.

Anchors make scores comparable

A score of "4 for maintenance" means nothing unless everyone agrees what 4 is. Write anchors using real quantities wherever you can. For a field sensor, the maintenance criterion might be anchored on site visits per year for battery work:

Score Maintenance anchor
5 No planned battery visits over the product life
4 One visit every two years or less often
3 One visit a year
2 Two visits a year
1 More than two visits a year

With anchors, two people scoring independently usually land within a point of each other, and when they do not, the disagreement points at a real question ("what discharge rate did you assume?") rather than taste. Where you can, score from a calculation, not an impression: the battery-life estimate behind the maintenance score should be attached to the study.

A worked example

Suppose a team must choose how to power a soil sensor node and has agreed four criteria with weights summing to 1. The options and scores below are made up for illustration.

Criterion Weight A: primary cell B: solar + rechargeable C: larger primary pack
Maintenance 0.35 2 5 4
Unit cost 0.25 4 2 3
Reliability 0.25 5 3 4
Size 0.15 4 2 2
Weighted total 3.55 3.30 3.45

Each total is the sum of weight times score. For option A: 0.35 × 2 + 0.25 × 4 + 0.25 × 5 + 0.15 × 4 = 0.70 + 1.00 + 1.25 + 0.60 = 3.55. Option A wins, but by 0.10 over C and 0.25 over B. On a 1-to-5 scale with judgement in every score, that margin is small. Before anyone celebrates, check how fragile it is.

Sensitivity: what would flip the decision

A sensitivity check varies one weight at a time, rescales the others so they still sum to 1, and watches the winner. This script does it for the maintenance weight.

weights = {"maint": 0.35, "cost": 0.25, "rel": 0.25, "size": 0.15}
scores = {
    "A primary":     {"maint": 2, "cost": 4, "rel": 5, "size": 4},
    "B solar":       {"maint": 5, "cost": 2, "rel": 3, "size": 2},
    "C big primary": {"maint": 4, "cost": 3, "rel": 4, "size": 2},
}

def totals(w):
    return {o: sum(w[c] * s[c] for c in w) for o, s in scores.items()}

others = {c: v for c, v in weights.items() if c != "maint"}
rest_sum = sum(others.values())
for step in range(3, 12):
    wm = step * 0.05
    w = {"maint": wm}
    w.update({c: v * (1 - wm) / rest_sum for c, v in others.items()})
    t = totals(w)
    best = max(t, key=t.get)
    print(f"maint={wm:.2f}  " +
          "  ".join(f"{o[0]}={v:.2f}" for o, v in t.items()) +
          f"  -> {best}")

The output shows A winning up to a maintenance weight of 0.35, C taking over at 0.40, and B winning from 0.45 upwards. That is the real finding of the study: the answer depends on how much the customer values avoiding site visits. It turns an argument about power supplies into a question you can put to the customer, ideally with the cost of a field visit in hand. If they say visits to remote farms are expensive and slow, weight maintenance higher, and B becomes the right answer for a reason everyone can see.

Run the same sweep for each weight. If no reasonable weight changes the winner, the decision is robust and you can say so. If a small change flips it, say that too, and say which fact you would need to settle it.

Writing the rationale

The record of a trade study is worth more than the table. A good one fits on a page:

  • the decision, options and criteria, with links to the requirements behind each criterion
  • the weights and who agreed them, with the date of the meeting
  • the scores, with the calculation or evidence behind each one
  • the result and the sensitivity findings
  • what would reopen the decision, for example "if field visit cost exceeds the price difference of the solar kit, revisit"

Months later, when someone asks why the product uses a primary cell, the answer is in writing, and so is the condition under which the team agreed to change it.

Common mistakes

  • Weights chosen after scoring. The study then confirms a favourite. Fix weights first, with the stakeholders.
  • Too many criteria. Twelve criteria with small weights dilute the ones that matter. Keep four to six and fold the rest into must-pass constraints.
  • Mixing constraints with criteria. If an option fails a hard requirement, such as a safety limit, it is out, not scored low.
  • False precision. Reporting 3.547 against 3.452 implies a confidence the scores cannot support.

Try it yourself

  1. Pick a real choice from a project, such as two microcontrollers or two radio options, write anchors for four criteria and score it alone, then compare scores with a teammate who scored independently.
  2. Run the script with Python 3 on your numbers and sweep each weight in turn. Note the weight at which the winner changes.
  3. Keep the table in LibreOffice Calc or a CSV under version control, and write the one-page rationale next to it, including what would reopen the decision.
