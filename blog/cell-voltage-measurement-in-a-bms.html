Measuring cell voltages in a battery pack: the analog front end of a BMS

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Each cell in a series battery stack sits at a different, often high, common-mode voltage. Learn why simple dividers fail, how level shifting and stacked monitors measure each cell, and what balancing demands.

A battery management system (BMS) protects a pack of lithium cells from overcharge and overdischarge, estimates state of charge and keeps the cells balanced. All of that rests on one measurement: the voltage of every individual cell, accurate to a few millivolts. In a series stack, that measurement is harder than it sounds, because each cell's small voltage sits on top of all the cells below it. This article explains why the obvious approach fails, and the circuit techniques used instead. A safety note first: a large series pack can deliver dangerous voltages and fault currents, and work on real packs needs proper training, insulation and supervision.

The common-mode problem

Take sixteen cells in series, each at up to 4.2 V when full, illustrative values for a common configuration. The bottom of the stack is the system ground and the top is at 16 × 4.2 = 67.2 V. To measure the top cell, you need the difference between 67.2 V and 63.0 V. The signal is 4.2 V; the common-mode voltage is 63 V, and it changes with every cell's state of charge and with load current.

A ground-referenced ADC on a 3.3 V supply cannot connect to either node directly. Every cell-measurement architecture is an answer to the same question: how to move a small differential voltage from a high, shifting common-mode level down to the ADC's ground, without destroying its accuracy on the way.

Why dividers and subtraction fail

The obvious approach divides each tap voltage down to the ADC range and subtracts neighbours in software. Run the numbers for the top cell with a 12-bit, 3.3 V converter.

  • The top tap needs a divider of about 67.2 / 3.3 ≈ 20.4 to fit the ADC range.
  • One ADC LSB is 3.3 V / 4096 ≈ 0.81 mV at the converter, which is about 16.4 mV at the tap. Subtracting two such readings makes the quantization uncertainty worse, not better.
  • Divider gain error is worse still. Resistors matched to 0.1 % give a gain error of 0.1 % on 67.2 V, which is 67 mV, appearing as an error in the top cell's voltage.

The required accuracy is a few millivolts. The ground-referenced divider misses it by more than an order of magnitude, and the error grows for cells higher up the stack. The dividers also draw current continuously from the cells, and from different cells unequally, which itself unbalances the pack.

The fix in every practical design is to measure each cell differentially, close to the cell, so that common-mode voltage never reaches the precision path.

Technique 1: the flying capacitor

A capacitor is connected across one cell through a pair of switches, charges to that cell's voltage, and is then disconnected. A second pair of switches connects the same capacitor across a ground-referenced ADC input. The capacitor carries the differential voltage down to ground and leaves the common mode behind.

It is conceptually clean: one capacitor and one ADC can serve many cells in sequence. The difficulty is in the switches, which must withstand the full stack voltage when open, have low leakage so the capacitor does not droop, and inject little charge when they open. Sequential measurement also means cells are measured at different instants, which matters under load, as discussed below.

Technique 2: current-mode level shifting

A resistor across the cell, in series with a high-voltage transistor, converts the cell voltage into a current. A feedback loop forces the cell voltage across a resistor R1, so the current is Vcell/R1. The current flows down through the high-voltage transistor to a ground-referenced resistor R2, where it becomes Vcell × R2/R1. Current does not care about common-mode voltage; it flows the same whatever potential the cell sits at.

Accuracy depends on the ratio R2/R1, which on chip can be matched and trimmed, and on the transistor passing all the current (its leakage and any base or gate current are errors). Each cell needs its own high-voltage path, which costs area in a high-voltage process.

Technique 3: stacked monitor chips

Large packs typically use monitor ICs that each handle a group of cells, for example a dozen or so, and each chip is powered from and referenced to its own group. Inside a chip, the common-mode voltage is limited to the group's span, so ordinary differential measurement techniques, a multiplexer and a precision ADC, work well.

The chips are stacked, each one higher on the pack than the one below, and pass data down to the system controller through an isolated or capacitively coupled daisy-chain link that tolerates the voltage step between neighbours. The precision analog problem becomes a communication problem, which is easier to make robust.

What balancing and estimation demand

Why are millivolts needed? For lithium chemistries, the open-circuit voltage changes slowly with state of charge across the middle of the range, and for some chemistries, such as lithium iron phosphate, the curve is especially flat. A small voltage error therefore corresponds to a large error in estimated charge. Balancing decisions, which cell to bleed and by how much, inherit that error. Overvoltage protection near full charge also needs accuracy, because the margin between a full cell and an overcharged one is small.

Several practical points follow:

  • Simultaneous sampling. Under load, each cell's terminal voltage includes the load current times its internal resistance. If cells are measured at different instants while the current changes, the comparison between cells is corrupted. Monitors either sample all cells together or synchronise the measurement with a current reading.
  • Filtering. Each cell input has an RC filter against switching noise from motor drives and converters. The filter must be differential and matched, or it converts common-mode noise into differential error.
  • Balancing currents in sense wires. Passive balancing bleeds current through resistors, often sharing the cell's sense wire. That current drops voltage in the wire and connector, so measurements are usually taken with balancing paused.
  • Open-wire detection. A broken sense wire can make a cell read as normal while it is not; monitors inject small test currents to detect it.
  • Temperature. Cell voltage depends on temperature, so a BMS also measures temperatures at several points in the pack.

Try it yourself

  1. In Python, repeat the divider calculation for each tap of a 16-cell stack, adding a random 0.1 % error to each divider, and plot the worst-case error per cell.
  2. In ngspice, build a flying-capacitor measurement with ideal switches, add realistic switch leakage and charge injection, and see how the measured voltage drifts with hold time.
  3. Model a current-mode level shifter with an ideal op-amp, a high-voltage transistor and two resistors, and sweep the common-mode voltage from 0 to 60 V to confirm that the output depends only on the cell voltage.
