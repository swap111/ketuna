Rural IoT node trade-offs: range, battery life, cost and maintenance

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How radio choice, reporting interval, battery and enclosure decisions pull against each other in a sensor node for farms and villages, with a battery-life model that shows which decisions matter most.

A sensor node on a farm, a village water tank or a rural roadside has to do four things at once: reach a gateway or network from far away, run for a long time on a battery, cost little enough to deploy in numbers, and need almost no maintenance, because a site visit may mean hours of travel. These goals pull against each other. Every decision that improves one hurts another, and the hard part of the design is seeing how they connect before you commit to a radio or an enclosure.

The four forces

  • Range. Rural sites are far from infrastructure. Longer range comes from lower radio frequencies, higher transmit power, better antennas mounted higher, and slower data rates that let the receiver pick out weaker signals.
  • Battery life. Energy is spent mostly while the radio is on and while sensors warm up. Slower data rates and higher transmit power, both good for range, keep the radio on longer or drawing more current.
  • Cost. Per-node hardware, gateways or network subscriptions, and installation. A cheap node that needs its own gateway may cost more overall than a dearer node using an existing network.
  • Maintenance. Battery replacement, cleaning sensors, fixing damage. In rural deployments the cost of a visit can exceed the cost of the node.

Radio choice sets the frame

The radio decision constrains everything else, so make it with a trade study rather than by habit. Broadly, the options are:

Option Strengths Weaknesses
Sub-GHz long-range low-power radio to your own gateway Long range at low power; no subscription; works where there is no cellular coverage You install and maintain gateways; low data rates; duty-cycle limits in unlicensed bands
Cellular low-power standards (NB-IoT, LTE-M) No gateway to maintain; wide-area coverage where the network supports it Depends on local coverage and operator support; SIM and subscription cost; higher energy per message
2.4 GHz short-range (IEEE 802.15.4 mesh, Bluetooth Low Energy) Low cost, low energy per bit, mesh can extend coverage Short range per hop; mesh routers need power; vegetation absorbs 2.4 GHz

Frequency matters for range because free-space path loss grows with frequency: moving from about 866 MHz to 2.4 GHz adds 20 log10(2400 / 866) ≈ 8.9 dB of loss at the same distance, before counting the extra absorption by crops and trees at higher frequencies. Unlicensed sub-GHz bands and the power and duty-cycle limits that apply to them are set by national rules; check the current regulations for India before choosing a band and transmit power.

A battery-life model

Battery life is where the trade-offs become numbers. The model below takes a sleep current, a sensor reading and a radio transmission per report, and computes life for different reporting intervals. All values are made up for illustration, and it ignores retransmissions and receive windows, which a real model must include.

CAP_MAH = 2600 * 0.8        # usable after derating for temperature, age
SLEEP_UA = 8.0 + 3.0        # sleep current plus self-discharge equivalent
SENSE_MA, SENSE_S = 5.0, 0.5   # sensor warm-up and read
radios = {                     # (current mA, on-time s) per message
    "sub-GHz LPWAN": (40.0, 0.4),
    "cellular LPWAN": (120.0, 3.0),
}

def life_years(interval_min, tx_ma, tx_s):
    n = 24 * 60 / interval_min                # messages per day
    active_mas = n * (SENSE_MA * SENSE_S + tx_ma * tx_s)
    sleep_mas = SLEEP_UA / 1000 * 86400
    avg_ma = (active_mas + sleep_mas) / 86400
    return CAP_MAH / avg_ma / 24 / 365

for name, (ma, s) in radios.items():
    row = [f"{life_years(t, ma, s):4.1f}" for t in (5, 15, 60)]
    print(f"{name:15s} 5/15/60 min: " + "  ".join(row) + " years")

The output:

sub-GHz LPWAN   5/15/60 min:  3.3   7.5  14.7 years
cellular LPWAN  5/15/60 min:  0.2   0.6   2.1 years

Several lessons fall out of the table:

  1. Reporting interval dominates. Going from 5 to 60 minutes multiplies life by more than four for the sub-GHz case and about ten for the cellular case. Before arguing about batteries, ask how often the user truly needs a reading. Soil moisture changes over hours; a flood-level alarm may need minutes, but only when the level is rising, which suggests reporting on change rather than on a fixed timer.
  2. Energy per message matters more than sleep current at short intervals, and sleep current plus self-discharge matters more at long intervals. At 60 minutes the sub-GHz node's life is set mostly by the 11 µA floor.
  3. Long calculated lives are a warning, not a result. A model that says 14.7 years is telling you that the battery's shelf life, the enclosure seals and the electronics will fail before the charge runs out. Cap your design life at what the weakest part can actually deliver.

Enclosure and installation decisions

The enclosure pulls on range, cost and maintenance at once. A metal box shields the radio, so the antenna must be outside it, which adds a cable, a connector and a seal. Mounting the antenna higher improves range considerably, because it clears crops, walls and the ground, but it costs a pole and makes the node more visible to curious hands. A bigger box fits a bigger battery and longer life but costs more and is harder to mount.

Design for the maintenance visit you cannot avoid. If batteries will need replacing, make it a tool-light, one-person, few-minute job with no recalibration, and schedule it with other site work. If sensors need cleaning, make the probe removable without opening the electronics compartment.

Write the trade-offs into requirements

Each force becomes a requirement with a number, and the numbers must be consistent: a reporting interval, a range at a stated antenna height and terrain, a battery life at a stated temperature, a unit cost at a stated volume and a maximum maintenance time per visit. Then check them together with a model like the one above. If the requirements cannot all be met, that is a conversation to have with the user now, about which one gives way.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the radio first and the reporting interval later. The interval often matters more.
  • Leaving out retransmissions, receive windows and network join energy. Real radios rarely send once and sleep.
  • Ignoring temperature. Battery capacity falls in cold weather and self-discharge rises in heat.
  • Designing for the bench. Range measured across a lab says nothing about a field of tall crops.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the model, then add a retransmission probability and a receive window after each message, and see how life changes.
  2. Measure the real sleep and active current of a development board running Zephyr with a simple current-sense setup, and replace the illustrative values with yours.
  3. Write a one-page trade study for a node at a site you know, with criteria for range, life, cost and maintenance, and run a sensitivity check on the weights.
