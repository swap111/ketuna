Skilling paths into chip design in India: degrees, short programs and self-study

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

What a degree, a short training program and self-study each give you on the way into chip design, what an employer can actually check, and how open-source projects tie the three together into evidence.

In this article

If you want to work on chips, you will hear conflicting advice: do a master's degree, take a short course, just build things on your own. Each route has real strengths and real gaps, and most people who get into the field use more than one. The useful question is not which route is best, but what each one gives you, what it leaves out and how you will show an employer what you can do. This article lays that out.

What the job asks for

Chip design is several jobs, not one. RTL design, verification, physical design, design-for-test, analog and mixed-signal design, and post-silicon validation each need different daily skills. What they share is a foundation:

  • Digital logic and timing: combinational and sequential logic, finite state machines, setup and hold, clock domains
  • A hardware language: Verilog or SystemVerilog, written as hardware, not as software
  • Scripting: Python, Tcl and shell, because every flow is automated
  • Linux comfort: most tools run there
  • Reading reports and debugging: waveforms, timing reports, log files

The track you aim for adds its own layer: verification methodology for verification, synthesis and timing for physical design, device physics and circuit design for analog. Decide roughly which track interests you, because it changes what you practise.

Degrees: depth and breadth

A bachelor's degree in electronics or a related branch gives you the theory under all of this: circuits, signals, devices, digital design and computer architecture. A master's degree with a VLSI focus adds depth, often a thesis on a real design problem and access to labs and faculty who work in the area.

What degrees do well is foundations and long, unhurried projects. What they often do less well is current tool flows and industry practice, because syllabi change slowly and lab tool access varies widely between colleges. A degree also does not by itself show an employer that you can debug a failing testbench at the end of a long day. Treat the degree as the base, not the proof.

Short programs: focus and feedback

Short, focused programs concentrate on one track and the tools and flows used in it. The good ones give you structured practice, review of your work by experienced engineers and a project with a beginning, middle and end. That review is the part hardest to get on your own: someone telling you why your testbench is weak or your constraints are wrong.

When you compare programs, look at what you will actually do, not the list of topics. Ask how many hours you will spend writing and debugging designs yourself, whether someone will review your code, what you will have finished at the end, and which tools you will learn and whether you can keep using them afterwards. Be wary of any program that promises outcomes rather than describing work.

Self-study: free, flexible and hard to sustain

Open-source tools, open PDKs, public courses and textbooks make serious self-study possible. You can simulate with Icarus Verilog or Verilator, synthesise with Yosys, run a full flow with LibreLane on sky130 and even send a small design to an open shuttle.

The difficulty is structure and feedback. Without deadlines it is easy to stall; without review it is easy to practise a mistake for months. Self-study works best with a clear plan, a public record of progress and some way of getting others to look at your work, such as forums or study groups.

What employers can check

An interviewer has a short time to decide whether you can do the work. They can check:

Evidence What it shows
Fundamentals in a conversation Whether you understand timing, logic and architecture, not just definitions
Live problem solving How you reason about a small design or a bug
A repository of your work Real code, testbenches, scripts and reports, with history
A clear write-up of one project Whether you can explain choices, trade-offs and what went wrong

Certificates tell an employer you attended something. A repository with a design, a self-checking testbench, synthesis and timing reports and a README explaining your decisions tells them you can do the job. Make sure your evidence is your own work and that you can explain every line of it, because a good interviewer will ask.

Combining the routes with open-source practice

The strongest profiles use open-source projects to connect whatever routes they took. A sensible plan:

  1. Foundations from your degree, filled in with textbooks where your course was thin
  2. One focused project per track you are considering, each in a public repository: for example a UART with a cocotb testbench for verification, or the same block hardened on sky130 for physical design
  3. Review by someone else, whether a mentor, a program or a community forum
  4. A write-up for each project explaining what you built, how you checked it and what you would change

Draw it out by hand first, then use AI tools to speed up the routine parts, and check what they produce against your own understanding. Interviewers can tell quickly whether you know why your design works.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a small design, such as a FIFO, in Verilog and simulate it with Icarus Verilog.
  2. Add a self-checking cocotb testbench that compares against a Python model.
  3. Synthesise it with Yosys and read the cell count and any warnings.
  4. Put it all in a public repository with a README that explains your design choices in one page.
