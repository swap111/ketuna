Designing a biopotential front end for a health wearable: gain, CMRR and input protection

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Body signals are millivolts riding on large electrode offsets and mains interference. See how input impedance, CMRR, a driven reference and staged gain recover them, and how input protection keeps the wearer safe.

In this article

  2. Input impedance and CMRR work together
  3. Reduce the common mode at its source
  4. Staging the gain around the offset

A wearable that records heart or muscle activity has to measure signals of around a millivolt or less through electrodes on skin, while the wearer's body acts as an antenna for mains interference and the electrodes themselves add DC offsets hundreds of times larger than the signal. The analog front end has to extract the signal from all of that, and it is wired directly to a person, so it must also be safe under faults. This article walks through the design decisions in order. A note before starting: a wearable's signal processing can flag patterns, but it is not a medical diagnosis, and any device sold for medical use goes through formal safety and performance standards and approval.

What the front end is up against

Three unwanted quantities arrive with the wanted signal.

  • Electrode offset. Each electrode-skin interface behaves like a small battery plus a resistance and capacitance. The difference between two electrodes can be a few hundred millivolts of slowly drifting DC. It is far larger than the signal, and it is differential, so common-mode rejection does nothing about it.
  • Common-mode interference. The body couples capacitively to nearby mains wiring and to ground. The whole body, and so both electrodes, moves up and down together at 50 Hz (the mains frequency in India) by an amount that can be far larger than the signal.
  • Electrode impedance imbalance. The two electrode contacts never have the same impedance, and the values change with skin condition, movement and time.

The third item quietly connects the first two, as the next section shows.

Input impedance and CMRR work together

The amplifier's job is to reject the common-mode voltage Vcm. Its own common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) sets how much leaks through. With 1 V of common-mode interference and a CMRR of 100 dB (a factor of 105), the leakage referred to the input is 1 V / 105 = 10 µV. That is small compared with a millivolt signal. These numbers are illustrative.

But the electrodes and the amplifier's input impedance form two voltage dividers, one per input. If the electrode impedances differ by ΔZ and the amplifier's common-mode input impedance is Zin, the two inputs see slightly different fractions of Vcm. That difference is a genuine differential signal, which no amplifier CMRR can reject:

Verror ≈ Vcm × ΔZ / Zin

With 1 V of common mode, a 50 kΩ imbalance and Zin = 100 MΩ, the error is 1 × 50 kΩ / 100 MΩ = 0.5 mV, comparable to the signal. Raise Zin to 10 GΩ and it drops to 5 µV. That is why biopotential inputs need very high common-mode input impedance, why input bias currents must be tiny (they also flow through the electrode impedance and add offset), and why any input filter capacitors to ground must be matched.

Reduce the common mode at its source

Rather than only rejecting common-mode voltage, a front end can shrink it. A driven reference (in ECG systems, the right-leg drive) senses the average of the input electrodes, inverts and amplifies it, and drives it back into the body through a third electrode. It is a feedback loop around the body: the common-mode interference is reduced by the loop gain, which relaxes both the CMRR and the imbalance requirements. The loop must be stable with the body's capacitances in it, and its output current must be limited for safety.

Staging the gain around the offset

The electrode offset dictates the gain plan. Suppose the analog supply is 3.3 V, the signal path is centred at 1.65 V, and the electrode offset can reach ±300 mV. A first stage with gain 5 turns that offset into ±1.5 V, which just fits inside the rails with some margin for the signal. A gain of 50 would saturate the stage, and the signal would be lost.

So the classic structure is:

  1. A low-gain instrumentation amplifier with high input impedance and high CMRR. Low gain keeps the offset in range; being the first stage, it should still have low noise, since its noise is not divided by anything.
  2. A high-pass filter that removes the DC offset and slow drift. Its corner must be low enough to preserve the slow parts of the waveform you care about; diagnostic-quality ECG, for instance, needs a much lower corner than a simple heart-rate monitor. Some designs instead feed the output back through an integrator to cancel the offset at the input stage, which allows more gain up front.
  3. A second gain stage, here 100, for a total of 500. A 1 mV signal becomes 0.5 V, using a good fraction of the ADC range.
  4. A low-pass anti-alias filter before the ADC.

Many modern designs digitise early instead, using a high-resolution sigma-delta ADC with enough dynamic range to accept the offset directly and remove it in software. The trade is a harder converter for a simpler analog chain.

Protecting the wearer and the circuit

The front end connects through electrodes to a person, so protection runs in both directions.

  • Current into the body must stay very small, both in normal operation and under a single fault such as a failed component. Series resistors in each electrode line limit the current that any fault inside the device could drive into the wearer. The applicable limits are set by the medical electrical equipment safety standards (the IEC 60601 family and their national equivalents); check the current edition for your device class rather than relying on remembered numbers.
  • The circuit must survive what the body brings: electrostatic discharge when someone touches an electrode, and in clinical settings much larger pulses. The same series resistors limit that current, and clamp diodes to the supply rails steer it away from the amplifier inputs. The resistors add thermal noise and, with input capacitance, form a filter, so their value is a compromise.
  • Isolation from mains-powered equipment. A wearable running from its own battery is inherently isolated. Charging and wired data connections are where isolation can be lost; many designs simply disable measurement while connected to a charger.

Safety work like this needs review by people qualified in medical device standards. Treat this section as the reasoning behind the requirements, not the requirements themselves.

Try it yourself

  1. In ngspice, model two electrodes as voltage sources with different series resistances, add a 1 V, 50 Hz common-mode source, and measure the differential error at an ideal amplifier's input for input impedances of 100 MΩ and 10 GΩ.
  2. Build the three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier with ideal op-amps, set its gain to 5, and add a ±300 mV differential offset to check that the output stays in range.
  3. Design a simple driven-reference loop around a model of body capacitance and check its stability with an ac analysis before trusting it in a transient.
