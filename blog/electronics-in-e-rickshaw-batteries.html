The electronics inside e-rickshaw battery systems, and where a BMS chip fits

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How a small traction battery pack is built from cells, and how its battery management system measures, protects, balances and reports, with a worked balancing calculation and what a dedicated chip could integrate.

Electric three-wheelers carry passengers and goods through traffic, heat and rain every day, often driven hard and charged wherever a socket is available. Their battery packs live a rough life. Whether the battery is a lead-acid bank or a lithium-ion pack, the electronics around it decide how long it lasts, how far the vehicle goes on a charge and, for lithium-ion, whether a fault ends in a warning light or a fire. This article walks through those electronics and shows where a dedicated battery management chip fits.

From cells to a pack

A lithium-ion cell has a nominal voltage of a few volts, far too low to drive a traction motor. So cells are connected in series to raise the voltage and in parallel to raise the capacity. A pack described as, say, 16S2P has 16 series groups, each made of 2 cells in parallel.

The series connection is what makes management necessary. The same current flows through every series group, but no two groups are identical. They differ slightly in capacity, internal resistance and self-discharge, and those differences grow with age and temperature. Over many cycles, one group ends up fuller or emptier than the rest. Because charging must stop when the highest group reaches its limit, and discharging when the lowest reaches its limit, one weak group limits the whole pack.

What the battery management system does

The battery management system (BMS) has five jobs:

  • Measure every series group's voltage, the pack current and several temperatures
  • Protect by opening switches, usually back-to-back MOSFETs or a contactor, on over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current, short circuit and over- or under-temperature
  • Balance the groups so they stay at similar states of charge
  • Estimate state of charge and state of health from those measurements
  • Communicate with the charger, the motor controller and the driver's display

Protection must work even if the software hangs. That is why front-end chips usually include hardware comparators for short circuit and over-current that act within microseconds, independently of the microcontroller.

Balancing, with a worked example

The simplest and most common method is passive balancing: a resistor and a switch across each group bleed charge from the fullest groups, usually near the end of charge, until the others catch up. The energy is wasted as heat, but the circuit is cheap and simple. Active balancing moves charge from full groups to empty ones with inductors, capacitors or transformers. It wastes less energy but costs more and adds complexity.

An illustrative calculation, with made-up numbers: one group is 100 mAh fuller than the rest, the bleed resistor is 33 Ω and the group sits at about 3.6 V.

I = 3.6 V ÷ 33 Ω ≈ 109 mA, so t ≈ 100 mAh ÷ 109 mA ≈ 0.92 h, about 55 minutes

P = 3.6 V × 109 mA ≈ 0.39 W per resistor

That tells you two things. If the vehicle is typically charged for a short time, a small balancing current may never catch up with a growing imbalance. And if many resistors bleed at once on a small board inside a hot pack, the heat matters, so the firmware should limit how many balance at the same time and watch temperature.

Charging and monitoring

Lithium-ion cells are charged with a constant current until the highest group reaches its maximum voltage, then at constant voltage while the current tapers. The BMS tells the charger what it may do and stops the charge if anything goes out of range. A pack that may be plugged into many different chargers has to protect itself on its own, without trusting the charger.

State of charge is commonly estimated by coulomb counting, integrating current over time, corrected against the open-circuit voltage when the pack rests. Accurate current sensing, including near zero, matters because small offset errors integrate into large errors over days. State of health tracks how capacity and internal resistance change as the pack ages, which is valuable information for owners, fleet operators and battery-swapping services.

Where a dedicated chip fits

Today a typical BMS board combines a multi-cell analog front end chip, a separate microcontroller, a current-sense amplifier, an isolated communication interface and discrete protection drivers. A dedicated chip designed for small traction packs could integrate:

  • precise multi-channel cell voltage measurement with a high-resolution ADC
  • current measurement and coulomb counting in hardware
  • hardware protection comparators and gate drivers for the switches
  • balancing switches and their control
  • a small processor core with secure firmware and a communication interface

Integration lowers part count and cost and can improve accuracy, because the measurement path is designed as one system. It is also a good example of a mixed-signal SoC problem: precise analog, robust high-voltage interfaces and careful digital control on one die. Battery safety, though, is never just the chip: cell quality, mechanical design, thermal design and the relevant safety standards all matter. Check the current safety requirements for traction batteries before building a pack, and work with qualified people when handling lithium-ion cells.

Try it yourself

  1. Repeat the balancing calculation for your own resistor value and imbalance, and estimate how much charging time is needed each day.
  2. Write a coulomb counter in Python, add a small current offset and see how quickly the state-of-charge estimate drifts over a simulated week.
  3. Simulate one balancing channel, a cell model, resistor and MOSFET switch, in ngspice and check the bleed current and power.
