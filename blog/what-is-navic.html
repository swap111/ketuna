What NavIC is, and what it takes to build a receiver for it

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

NavIC is India's regional satellite navigation system. This article explains how it differs from global systems, how any satellite receiver finds its position, and what a NavIC receiver chip has to do.

When your phone shows your position on a map, it is listening to satellites. Most people say "GPS", but GPS is only one of several satellite navigation systems. India operates its own: NavIC, short for Navigation with Indian Constellation. It was developed by the Indian Space Research Organisation and was earlier known as IRNSS, the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System. This article explains what makes NavIC different, how satellite positioning works in general, and what an engineer has to build to receive it.

Regional, not global

GPS, Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou are global systems: their satellites circle the Earth in medium orbits so that enough of them are visible from anywhere. NavIC is regional. It is designed to serve India and a region around it, and its satellites sit in geostationary and inclined geosynchronous orbits. Seen from the ground, such satellites stay over roughly the same part of the sky instead of rising and setting, so across the service area they are visible all day, often high above the horizon where buildings and terrain block them less.

The trade-off is coverage: far outside the region, NavIC satellites are low on the horizon or not visible. That is why receivers usually combine NavIC with one or more global systems rather than using it alone.

NavIC provides an open Standard Positioning Service for everyone and a Restricted Service for authorised users. Its original signals are in two bands: L5, centred at 1176.45 MHz, and S band, centred at 2492.028 MHz. Newer satellites also add a signal in the L1 band, the band most consumer receivers already listen to, which makes it easier to add NavIC support to existing designs.

How any satellite receiver finds its position

Every navigation satellite broadcasts a signal that carries precise timing plus data describing its own orbit. A receiver measures when each signal arrived. Multiplying the travel time by the speed of light gives a distance to that satellite.

There is a catch: the receiver's own clock is cheap and not synchronised to the satellites, so every measured distance is off by the same unknown clock error. These biased distances are called pseudoranges. The receiver therefore has four unknowns, its three position coordinates plus its clock error, and needs at least four satellites to solve for them. More satellites make the solution more robust, which is another reason to combine systems.

Using two frequencies helps too. The ionosphere delays radio signals by an amount that depends on frequency, so a receiver that measures the same satellite on two bands, such as NavIC's L5 and S, can estimate that delay and remove much of it.

From antenna to position: the receiver chain

A NavIC receiver, whether on a dedicated chip or inside a larger system-on-chip, goes through the same stages as any satellite receiver:

Stage What it does Usually built as
Antenna and low-noise amplifier Picks up signals that arrive far below the noise floor and amplifies them with minimal added noise Analog RF
Filtering and down-conversion Selects each band and shifts it to a low intermediate frequency Analog RF
Analog-to-digital converter Samples the signal, often with only a few bits Mixed-signal
Acquisition Searches for each satellite's code timing and Doppler shift Digital hardware
Tracking Keeps code and carrier locked as the satellite and receiver move Hardware correlators plus firmware loops
Data decoding and solution Reads orbit and time data, then computes position, velocity and time Firmware on a processor

The signal is buried in noise when it arrives. What makes reception possible is that each satellite spreads its signal with a known pseudo-random ranging code. Multiplying the incoming samples by a local copy of the code and summing, which is called correlation, adds up the tiny signal coherently while the noise averages out. The correlation peaks only when the local copy lines up with the received code, and that alignment gives the timing measurement.

You can see the effect in a few lines of Python. The code below uses a random ±1 sequence as a stand-in for a ranging code; it is not a real NavIC code.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N = 1023                                   # chips in one code period
code = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], N)          # stand-in code, not a real NavIC code
true_shift = 357
rx = np.roll(code, true_shift) + rng.normal(0.0, 3.0, N)   # signal weaker than the noise

# circular correlation against every code phase at once, using the FFT
corr = np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(rx) * np.conj(np.fft.fft(code))).real
peak = int(np.argmax(corr))
print(peak, round(corr[peak] / np.std(np.delete(corr, peak)), 1))

Each received sample is mostly noise, yet the script prints 357 9.6: it finds the right code phase, with a peak about ten times the spread of the other correlation values. A real receiver must also search over Doppler frequency, because satellite motion shifts the carrier, so acquisition is a two-dimensional search over code phase and frequency.

What building a NavIC receiver chip involves

Designing such a chip touches almost every discipline in electronics:

  • RF and analog: low-noise front ends for L5 and S band, and L1 if supported, with filters, mixers, a frequency synthesiser and ADCs.
  • Digital design: many parallel correlator channels running at the sample rate, plus acquisition engines. These are classic RTL blocks that need careful verification.
  • Firmware: tracking loops, navigation message decoding and the position solution, often on a small embedded processor such as a RISC-V core.
  • Systems work: power budgets for battery devices, time to first fix, and testing against recorded or simulated signals.

That breadth is why a NavIC receiver chip is one of the ten projects on Ketuna's projects page. Like the others it is a work-in-progress concept that starts on a board, moves to an FPGA and points towards a chip.

Why it matters

A regional system run from within the country gives India a navigation and timing service under its own control, tuned for its region. Timing is as important as position: power grids, telecom networks and financial systems all depend on accurate time, and satellite receivers are a common way to get it. For engineers, NavIC is also a reason to learn RF, signal processing, digital design and firmware together, on a problem with a clear national context.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script above, then add a carrier frequency offset to rx and extend the search over a grid of trial frequencies. Watch the peak vanish when the trial frequency is wrong.
  2. Read the source of an open-source software receiver such as GNSS-SDR. It implements acquisition, tracking and the position solution for global systems, and the structure maps directly onto a NavIC receiver.
  3. If you have a software-defined radio, record raw samples and experiment with GNU Radio. Check that your antenna and front end actually cover the band you want before drawing conclusions from silence.
