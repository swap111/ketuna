Analog layout for matching: common centroid, interdigitation and dummies

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Process parameters drift across a die, so two identical transistors placed side by side are not identical. See how interdigitated and common-centroid patterns cancel gradients, and why dummies and orientation matter.

A differential pair, a current mirror or a capacitor array works only if its devices match. The schematic says the two transistors are identical, but on silicon they are drawn at different places, and the wafer is not uniform. Oxide thickness, doping, stress and temperature all drift slowly across a die. Analog layout has a small set of patterns that turn those drifts from a systematic error into nothing at all, and knowing why each pattern works lets you choose the right one.

Two kinds of mismatch

Mismatch between nominally identical devices has two components, and layout treats them differently.

  • Random mismatch comes from atomic-scale randomness: the exact number of dopant atoms under a gate, roughness along its edges. It differs from device to device with no pattern, and it shrinks with area, roughly as one over the square root of gate area. Layout cannot cancel it; only larger devices reduce it.
  • Systematic mismatch comes from smooth variations across the die: a gradient in oxide thickness, a temperature gradient from a hot output stage nearby, mechanical stress near the package edge. It depends on where each device sits. Layout patterns can cancel it.

A simple model is enough to reason about the systematic part. Suppose the threshold voltage drifts linearly across the die by g millivolts per unit of distance. A device's effective shift is the average of the shift at each of its parts. The trick is to arrange the parts so that both devices have the same average.

Split, then arrange

The first step is to split each device into identical unit transistors (fingers) wired in parallel. A pair of transistors A and B, each four units, becomes eight units in a row. Now the order matters:

  • AAAABBBB puts A's centre at position 1.5 and B's at 5.5 (counting from 0). A linear gradient shifts them differently by four unit pitches' worth.
  • ABABABAB, called interdigitation, alternates the units. The centres are now only one pitch apart, so the error falls by a factor of four, but it is not zero.
  • ABBAABBA places A at 0, 3, 4, 7 and B at 1, 2, 5, 6. Both centres sit at 3.5. This is a common-centroid arrangement: the geometric centre of each device's units coincides, so any linear gradient affects both equally and cancels.

Checking patterns with numbers

The script below evaluates each pattern under a linear gradient of 1 mV per unit pitch and under a curved, bowl-shaped variation centred on the row. The values are made up for illustration; the comparison between patterns is the point.

# Threshold shift of each unit = gradient evaluated at its position.
# Compare the average shift of device A with device B for a few layouts.
def mismatch(pattern, shift):
    a = [shift(i) for i, u in enumerate(pattern) if u == "A"]
    b = [shift(i) for i, u in enumerate(pattern) if u == "B"]
    return sum(a) / len(a) - sum(b) / len(b)

linear = lambda x: 1.0 * x                 # 1 mV per unit pitch
bowl = lambda x: 0.5 * (x - 3.5) ** 2      # curved, centred on the row

for p in ("AAAABBBB", "ABABABAB", "ABBAABBA", "AABBBBAA", "ABBABAAB"):
    print(f"{p}: linear {mismatch(p, linear):+.2f} mV, "
          f"curved {mismatch(p, bowl):+.2f} mV")

Two lessons come out of the table. Common centroid cancels linear gradients, but a pattern that is symmetric about the centre can be badly hit by a curved variation, as AABBBBAA shows. Dispersing the units more finely (ABBAABBA versus AABBBBAA) shrinks the residual, and a carefully chosen sequence such as ABBABAAB cancels both terms in this model. Real gradients are not known exactly, so the practical goal is a pattern that is common-centroid and well dispersed.

In two dimensions, the classic structure is the cross-coupled quad: A on one diagonal, B on the other. It cancels linear gradients in both x and y and is the default for a critical differential pair.

Dummies, orientation and surroundings

Common centroid handles smooth gradients. Several local effects need other measures.

  • Dummies. A unit at the end of a row has a neighbour on one side only. Etching, polysilicon line width and stress depend on what is nearby, so end units come out slightly different. Adding non-functional dummy units at both ends, typically with their gates tied off, makes every active unit see the same neighbourhood.
  • Orientation. Matched transistors should have current flowing in the same direction. Mobility depends on crystal direction and stress, and some implant steps are tilted, so a transistor rotated by 90° or mirrored is not equivalent.
  • Well and isolation proximity. Devices close to the edge of a well or to shallow trench isolation see threshold and mobility shifts. Keep matched devices away from well edges and give them identical spacing.
  • Symmetric wiring. A few ohms of extra wire in one source shifts its effective gate-source voltage. Route the two halves of a pair as mirror images, and keep heavy currents out of shared source lines.
  • Thermal symmetry. Place the matched structure on an axis of symmetry relative to any hot block, so both halves sit at the same temperature.

Common mistakes

  • Matching the transistors but routing the drain wires of a differential pair with different lengths and couplings
  • Forgetting dummies on capacitor arrays, where edge effects are as strong as for transistors
  • Assuming common centroid fixes random mismatch; only area does that
  • Ratioing devices with different unit sizes, which breaks the identical-units principle

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script above, add your own patterns for eight or twelve units, and find one that cancels both terms.
  2. In Magic with the sky130 technology, lay out a cross-coupled quad of four identical NMOS units with dummies on each side, and run DRC.
  3. Extract it with extract all and ext2spice, compare against a schematic netlist with Netgen for LVS, and simulate the extracted pair in ngspice to check that the routing has not added asymmetric parasitics.
