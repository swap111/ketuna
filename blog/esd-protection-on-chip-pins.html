ESD protection on chip pins: why every pad has diodes

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A touch from a charged hand can put amperes into a pin and kilovolts across oxide that fails at a few volts. Learn what ESD does to a chip, and how pad diodes and rail clamps steer the current safely away.

Walk across a floor, pick up a chip, and you may deliver a discharge of thousands of volts to one of its pins. You will not feel it, but the transistors inside are built around gate oxides a few nanometres thick that break down at a handful of volts. Without protection, a single careless touch during assembly would destroy most chips. That is why every pad on a chip, including the simplest digital input, sits inside a protection structure, and why the I/O ring is a design discipline of its own.

What a discharge looks like

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) is described by test models that mimic how charge reaches a chip. The two most used are:

  • Human body model (HBM): a charged person touches a pin. The model is a 100 pF capacitor charged to the test voltage and discharged through a 1.5 kΩ resistor into the pin.
  • Charged device model (CDM): the chip itself becomes charged, for example by sliding along a tube in assembly, and then discharges through one pin when it touches a grounded surface. The pulse is much shorter and its peak current higher, because there is no 1.5 kΩ resistor to limit it.

Run the HBM numbers for a 2 kV test. The peak current is 2,000 V / 1,500 Ω ≈ 1.33 A, and it decays with a time constant of 1.5 kΩ × 100 pF = 150 ns. The stored energy, ½CV2, is ½ × 100 pF × (2 kV)2 = 200 µJ. The protection network must carry more than an ampere for a few hundred nanoseconds while keeping the voltage across every sensitive device below its breakdown.

Two things fail if it does not. Thin gate oxides break down when too much voltage appears across them, leaving a permanent leakage path or a short. Junctions and metal melt when too much current is forced through too small a region. The first is a voltage problem, the second a current-density problem, and protection design has to address both.

The standard protection network

The usual arrangement has three parts.

  • Two diodes at the pad. D1 runs from the pad up to VDD; D2 runs from VSS up to the pad. In normal operation the pad stays between the rails, so both are reverse-biased and invisible apart from their capacitance and leakage. If the pad is driven above VDD, D1 conducts; if below VSS, D2 conducts.
  • A rail clamp between VDD and VSS. A large NMOS transistor connected across the supply rails, normally off, turned on by a detector when a fast voltage rise appears on VDD. A common detector is an RC network: an ESD pulse rises in nanoseconds, while normal power-up takes microseconds or longer, so the RC time constant is chosen to distinguish the two and to keep the clamp on for the duration of the pulse.
  • A series resistor and secondary clamp in front of sensitive inputs, especially those that connect directly to a gate. The resistor limits current into the inner circuit, and small diodes after it clamp what remains, which matters for the fast CDM pulse.

Following the current

The value of the network is that there is a low-impedance path for every polarity and pin combination a test applies.

  • Positive zap on the pad, VSS grounded: current flows through D1 onto the VDD rail, then through the rail clamp down to VSS.
  • Negative zap on the pad, VSS grounded: D2 conducts directly from VSS to the pad.
  • Positive zap on the pad, VDD grounded: D1 conducts directly to VDD.
  • Pin to pin: the current enters one pad, flows through its D1 to VDD, through the clamp to VSS, and out through the other pad's D2.

The voltage the internal circuit sees is the sum of the drops along the path: diode forward voltage, rail resistance and the clamp's on-voltage, all at more than an ampere. Bus resistance matters more than intuition suggests. If the VDD rail between a pad and the nearest clamp has 2 Ω of resistance, 1.33 A adds about 2.7 V to the voltage at the pad. That is why clamps are distributed around the I/O ring, rails are wide, and diodes sit right next to the pad.

The design window

Every protection element must respect a window.

  • Above the operating range: it must not conduct during normal operation, including supply ramps, overshoot on signals and any allowed overvoltage. A rail clamp that triggers on a fast power-up is a real failure mode.
  • Below the failure point: it must turn on and hold the voltage below the breakdown of the gate oxides and junctions it protects, with margin, while carrying the full pulse current.

As processes shrink, oxides get thinner and the window narrows from above while operating voltages fall less, which makes protection harder. It also costs: pad diodes add capacitance and leakage to every pin, which hurts high-speed and very low-leakage inputs, so those pins need carefully optimised structures.

Common mistakes:

  • Treating protection as a library cell to drop in, without checking the rail resistance between pads and clamps
  • Forgetting pins that connect to separate supply domains; each domain needs its own clamps and a path to the others
  • Connecting a pad directly to a gate with no series resistance or secondary clamp
  • Overlooking latch-up: large ESD diodes inject carriers into the substrate, so they need guard rings

Try it yourself

  1. In ngspice, build the HBM source as a 100 pF capacitor charged to 2 kV discharging through 1.5 kΩ via a switch, and confirm the 1.33 A peak and 150 ns decay with a resistive load.
  2. Add the dual-diode network with sky130 diode models and an ideal clamp (a voltage-controlled switch with some on-resistance), and plot the pad voltage during the pulse. Add 2 Ω of rail resistance and compare.
  3. Open the I/O cells of an open PDK in KLayout and find the diodes and clamp transistors; note how wide their metal connections are.
