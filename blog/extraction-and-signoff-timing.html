Parasitic extraction and sign-off timing: why post-route numbers change

Physical design & tape-out Advanced 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Before routing, wire delay is a guess. After routing, extraction measures real resistance, capacitance and coupling. See with a worked RC example why a path that passed after placement can fail at sign-off.

Timing that is clean after placement and fails after routing is one of the most common surprises in a first physical design run. Nothing is broken. The earlier number was an estimate based on where cells sat, and the later number is based on the wires that were actually drawn, with their real lengths, layers, vias and neighbours. Understanding what changes between the two tells you how much margin to keep early and where to look when the post-route report turns red.

What placement-stage timing assumes

Before routing, the timing engine needs a delay for every net that does not exist yet. It estimates the wire from cell positions, typically using a Steiner tree or a bounding-box length, and multiplies by per-unit resistance and capacitance for an assumed layer. Some flows run a global route and estimate parasitics from its guides, which is better but still not exact.

These estimates miss several things:

  • Detours. Congestion forces some nets around obstacles, making them longer than the ideal tree.
  • Layer choice. Lower metals are thinner and more resistive than upper ones. A net estimated on a mid layer but routed on a low one is slower.
  • Vias. Each via adds resistance, and a net that changes layers often accumulates it.
  • Coupling. The estimate usually lumps all capacitance to ground. In reality a large part of a wire's capacitance is to its neighbours on the same layer.

What extraction measures

Parasitic extraction reads the routed layout and the PDK's process data (layer thicknesses, dielectric constants, sheet resistances) and computes, for each net, a network of resistors and capacitors. It separates ground capacitance (to the substrate and to power shapes) from coupling capacitance (to specific neighbouring signal nets). The result is written in SPEF, a standard parasitic exchange format that timing tools read. OpenROAD's openRCX is one open extractor; Magic can also extract parasitics.

The timing tool then computes delays with these RC networks, using a model such as Elmore delay for a first-order view or more accurate waveform-based methods at sign-off.

A worked example: one net, three views

Take one net with illustrative numbers. A driver with an effective output resistance Rd = 2 kΩ drives a routed wire with Rw = 400 Ω and total capacitance Cw = 60 fF, into a load pin of CL = 10 fF. With the wire as a distributed RC line, the Elmore delay is:

t ≈ Rd(Cw + CL) + Rw(Cw/2 + CL)

  • Placement estimate: suppose the estimate assumed 30 fF of wire capacitance and ignored wire resistance. Then t ≈ 2000 × 40 fF = 80 ps.
  • Extracted, neighbours quiet: 2000 × 70 fF + 400 × (30 + 10) fF = 140 + 16 = 156 ps.

The routed net is nearly twice as slow as the estimate. On a path of ten gates, a few nets like this can turn positive slack negative.

Now split the 60 fF into 40 fF to ground and 20 fF coupling to one neighbour. When the neighbour switches in the opposite direction at the same time, the voltage across the coupling capacitor changes twice as much, and the effective coupling roughly doubles. When it switches in the same direction, the effective coupling falls towards zero.

Neighbour behaviour Effective Cw Elmore delay
Same direction 40 fF 112 ps
Quiet 60 fF 156 ps
Opposite direction 80 fF 200 ps

The same wire can be anywhere from 112 ps to 200 ps depending on what its neighbour does. Sign-off timing with signal integrity analysis considers this window: it uses the slow case for setup checks and the fast case for hold checks, and also checks crosstalk noise, where a switching neighbour couples a glitch onto a quiet net that could be captured as a wrong value.

Corners multiply the picture

Extraction itself has corners. Metal thickness and width, and dielectric thickness, vary across wafers, so PDKs provide RC corners, such as a maximum-capacitance case and a minimum-capacitance case. Sign-off runs timing at combinations of transistor corners (slow, typical, fast with voltage and temperature) and RC corners. A path that is critical at one combination may be comfortable at another. Most flows sign off setup at slow transistors with high RC and hold at fast transistors with low RC, plus any other combinations the foundry requires.

Keeping the surprise small

  • Keep margin early. Close timing after placement with some slack held back, because routing will take some of it.
  • Compare reports on the same path. When a path fails post-route, find it in the placement report and see which nets grew. A single large jump points to a detour or a low-layer route.
  • Use non-default rules for critical nets. Wider spacing or upper layers for clocks and the worst nets reduce coupling and resistance.
  • Do not hand-tune to the estimate. Optimising hard against placement parasitics can fit noise in the estimate rather than the real design.

Try it yourself

  1. Harden a small design with LibreLane on sky130. Find the worst setup path in the placement-stage timing report and in the post-route sign-off report.
  2. Locate the SPEF file in the run directory and find one net from that path. Read its total capacitance and coupling entries.
  3. Load the netlist, Liberty and SPEF into OpenSTA with read_spef, then compare report_checks for that path with and without the SPEF loaded.
  4. Recompute the table above in Python for one of your real nets, using the resistances and capacitances from the SPEF.
