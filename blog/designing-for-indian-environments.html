Designing for dust, heat and moisture: environmental requirements for Indian conditions

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

How to write environmental requirements for products deployed in hot, dusty, humid and monsoon-affected sites, and how they drive enclosure ratings, venting, coatings, part choices and tests.

In this article

  2. Turn conditions into testable requirements
  3. Dust and water: the IP code
  4. Condensation: the monsoon problem
  5. Materials and testing

A board that runs for months on a lab bench can fail in weeks on a pole beside a field, a rooftop in a coastal city, or inside a pump house. The electronics are the same; the environment is not. Across India a single product may meet dry heat and fine dust in one season and saturated monsoon air in the next, with large swings between day and night. Those conditions have to be written down as requirements at the start, because they decide the enclosure, the coating, the parts and the tests, and none of those can be added cheaply at the end.

Start from the site, not from a template

Environmental requirements copied from another product's specification describe somebody else's site. Instead, describe where your product will actually live, then turn each condition into a number:

  • Location and mounting: indoors, sheltered outdoors or fully exposed; on a wall, pole, vehicle or in the ground.
  • Temperature: the range of air temperature at the site, from historical weather records for the region, plus the effect of the installation. A sealed box in direct sun gets considerably hotter inside than the surrounding air; how much depends on its colour, size and venting, so estimate it or measure a sample box.
  • Humidity and water: humidity range, rain, wind-driven rain, hose-down cleaning, flooding or temporary immersion.
  • Dust and particles: fine soil dust, conductive dust near industrial sites, salt-laden air near the coast.
  • Biological: insects nesting in vents, rodents chewing cables, mould on organic materials.
  • Solar radiation: ultraviolet light degrades many plastics and cable jackets over time.
  • Mechanical: vibration on vehicles and pumps, handling and transport shocks.

Use national meteorological data for the regions you sell into rather than guesses. If you cannot get site data, state the assumption in the requirement so it can be reviewed and tested.

Turn conditions into testable requirements

Each condition becomes one requirement with a number, a condition and a test method. The values below are made up for illustration:

ID Requirement Verification
ENV-1 The unit shall operate to specification at enclosure-internal temperatures from 0 °C to 70 °C Test in a thermal chamber
ENV-2 The unit shall survive storage from −10 °C to 85 °C, unpowered Test
ENV-3 The enclosure shall meet IP65 Test to the relevant IEC method
ENV-4 The unit shall operate after 10 days of cyclic damp heat with condensation Test
ENV-5 Exterior plastics shall retain function after UV exposure equivalent to the design life Analysis of material data plus test

Notice that ENV-1 is written in terms of internal temperature. You then need a separate analysis, or a measurement, linking the worst site conditions to that internal figure. Keeping the two apart makes each one testable.

Dust and water: the IP code

The international standard IEC 60529 defines IP codes: two digits rating protection against solids and against water. The first digit runs up to 6, where 5 means dust-protected (some dust may enter but not enough to interfere) and 6 means dust-tight. The second digit runs up to 8 in the base scale, with, for example, 4 for splashing water, 5 for water jets, 6 for powerful jets and 7 for temporary immersion. So IP65 is dust-tight and protected against water jets. Check the current standard for the exact test conditions behind each digit.

Two practical points. An IP rating applies to the enclosure as installed, with its glands, cables and seals, so a rated box with an unsealed cable entry is not rated. And higher is not automatically better: a fully sealed box traps heat and can trap moisture, which brings us to the problem that catches many outdoor designs.

Condensation: the monsoon problem

Air holds more water when warm. When humid air cools below its dew point, water condenses on the coolest surface, which may be your circuit board. A sealed enclosure is not perfectly sealed: as it heats by day and cools at night, it breathes, drawing in moist air through seals and cable glands. Over many cycles, water accumulates inside a box that would pass a spray test.

A quick dew-point estimate shows how easily this happens. This uses the common Magnus approximation:

import math

def dew_point_c(temp_c, rh_percent):
    a, b = 17.62, 243.12
    g = math.log(rh_percent / 100) + a * temp_c / (b + temp_c)
    return b * g / (a - g)

print(round(dew_point_c(30, 80), 1))   # warm, humid evening air

It prints about 26.2 °C. If air at 30 °C and 80 % relative humidity enters the box in the evening, and the box cools to 24 °C overnight, any surface below 26.2 °C will collect water. That is a small temperature drop.

Design responses, often combined:

  • Pressure-equalising vents with a breathable membrane, which let air move without pumping liquid water in, and let moisture out again.
  • Conformal coating on the board, a thin protective film (acrylic, silicone and polyurethane types are common) that keeps condensed water and dust off conductors. Mask connectors and test points before coating.
  • A small heater or keeping electronics warm, so the board stays above the dew point.
  • Drain holes at the lowest point, oriented downwards when installed.
  • Desiccant, useful for sealed boxes that are rarely opened, but it saturates eventually.

Materials and testing

Heat, parts and materials

Heat requirements flow into part selection. Choose parts rated for the internal temperature with margin: capacitors, especially electrolytics, wear out faster when hot, and batteries degrade and can become unsafe outside their temperature limits. Light-coloured enclosures and sun shields reduce solar heating. Plastics and cable jackets for outdoor use should be specified as UV-stabilised. Keep dissimilar metals apart in salty air, where galvanic corrosion is faster.

Testing what you specified

Environmental requirements are verified in chambers and test rigs: thermal cycling, damp heat with and without condensation, dust chambers, water spray and immersion, salt mist, UV exposure, vibration. The IEC 60068 family of standards describes many of these environmental test methods; check the current editions and any product standard that applies to your market. Do a cheap version early, such as a box with a temperature logger left on a rooftop through a few weeks of weather, and a full test at design validation.

Common mistakes

  • Specifying air temperature when the electronics see enclosure temperature. The difference can be large in sun.
  • Sealing everything. Breathing enclosures accumulate water; vent them properly.
  • Coating over connectors and test points. It ruins contact and blocks production test.
  • Testing only new units. Seals, gaskets and plastics age; consider what the product looks like after years outdoors.

Try it yourself

  1. Pick a product and a real deployment site. Write five environmental requirements with numbers, citing the weather data source you used for each temperature or humidity value.
  2. Run the dew-point function for a range of evening temperatures and humidities and find how far an enclosure must cool overnight before condensation starts.
  3. Put a small temperature and humidity logger, built from a microcontroller board running Zephyr or plain C, inside a sealed plastic box on a sunny rooftop for a week, and compare the internal and external readings.
