Clock gating without glitches: the latch-based integrated clock gate

Digital & VLSI design Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

ANDing a clock with an enable can chop clock pulses and clock flops at the wrong time. See how a latch that is transparent while the clock is low keeps the gated clock clean, and how to use the cell properly.

In this article
  1. Why gating the clock saves power
  2. The problem with a plain AND gate
  3. The latch-plus-AND cell
  4. Timing the enable
  5. Common mistakes
  6. Try it yourself

A significant part of a digital chip's dynamic power goes into the clock network, because the clock toggles every cycle at every flop, whether the data changes or not. Turning the clock off for registers that have nothing to do is one of the most effective power savings available to an RTL designer. The obvious way to do it, an AND gate between the clock and an enable, is also one of the most dangerous circuits you can put on a chip. The standard fix is a small cell built around a latch, and it is worth understanding exactly why it works.

Why gating the clock saves power

A register with an enable is usually built as a flop with a mux in front: when en is 0, the mux feeds Q back to D. The flop still receives every clock edge, so its internal clock nodes, and the clock buffers that drive it, still switch every cycle. If you stop the clock to a group of 32 such flops instead, the mux disappears and the clock nodes of all 32 flops, plus the buffers in that branch of the clock tree, stay quiet whenever the enable is low.

Synthesis tools can do this automatically: they look for groups of flops that share an enable and replace the feedback muxes with one gating cell. To use it well, and to review its results, you need to know what the cell does.

The problem with a plain AND gate

In a typical design, the enable comes from logic clocked by the same rising edge. So the enable changes shortly after a rising edge, while the clock is still high. Feed that straight into an AND gate with the clock and look at what happens:

  • When en rises in the middle of a high clock phase, the AND output rises immediately. The downstream flops see a rising edge at a random time, not at the clock edge. That edge is not timed by anything, so the flops capture whatever their D inputs happen to hold.
  • When en falls in the middle of a high phase, the AND output gives a shortened pulse. A pulse narrower than the flops' minimum pulse width can put them into an undefined state.
  • Any glitch on en from the combinational logic that produces it passes straight onto the clock.

On a data path a glitch is harmless, because the flop only samples at the clock edge. On a clock, every glitch is an edge.

The latch-plus-AND cell

The fix is to make sure the enable seen by the AND gate can only change while the clock is low. When the clock is low, the AND output is 0 regardless of the enable, so a change there cannot produce a pulse. A latch that is transparent while the clock is low does exactly this:

  • While clk is low, the latch passes en through. The enable has the whole low phase to settle.
  • When clk rises, the latch closes and holds the value it had just before the edge. During the entire high phase, the AND gate sees a stable enable.

So the gated clock contains only complete pulses that start at a real clock edge. Look again at the waveform: the latched en_l rises at the falling clock edge after en rose, and the first gated pulse is the next full one.

// Behavioural model of a latch-based clock gate, for simulation only.
// In a real design, instantiate the library's integrated clock gate cell.
module icg_model (
  input  logic clk,
  input  logic en,
  input  logic test_en,      // forces the clock on during scan test
  output logic gclk
);
  logic en_l;
  always_latch
    if (!clk) en_l = en | test_en;   // transparent while clk is low
  assign gclk = clk & en_l;
endmodule

Standard-cell libraries provide this as a single integrated clock gate (ICG) cell. Building it from a separate latch and AND gate is discouraged: the cell's internal timing between the latch and the gate is characterised as one unit, and the tools know how to time and place it as a clock element.

Timing the enable

The enable now has a real timing requirement: it must settle before the rising clock edge at the ICG, because that is when the latch closes. Static timing analysis checks this as a clock gating check, similar to a setup check on the enable pin. Two practical consequences:

  • The ICG sits at the start of a clock-tree branch, so its clock arrives earlier than the clock at the flops it drives. The enable path is therefore tighter than an ordinary flop-to-flop path. Keep enable logic shallow.
  • Place the ICG near its flops so that its output is the root of a compact sub-tree, which keeps skew under control.

Common mistakes

  • Writing assign gclk = clk & en; in RTL and hoping synthesis will fix it
  • Generating the enable with logic triggered on the falling edge, which breaks the low-phase assumption
  • Gating very small groups of flops, where the ICG's own power and area outweigh the savings
  • Forgetting the test enable, so scan test cannot clock the gated flops
  • Using a clock gate to stop a clock that crosses into another domain without thinking about the CDC consequences

Try it yourself

  1. Simulate a plain AND gate and icg_model side by side in Icarus Verilog (iverilog -g2012), with an enable that changes 1 ns after each rising edge. Find the chopped pulse in GTKWave.
  2. Write a 32-bit register with an enable, synthesise it with Yosys, and note the mux in front of each flop. Then add an ICG instance from the sky130 standard-cell library and compare the flop and mux counts.
  3. In OpenSTA, load a netlist containing the ICG and run report_checks to find the clock gating check on its enable pin.
