Fabless, foundry, OSAT: who does what in the semiconductor chain

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

The roles of chip design companies, wafer foundries, assembly and test houses, and EDA and IP providers, what each one hands to the next, and why the handoff files matter as much as the work.

News about semiconductors in India is full of words like fabless, foundry and OSAT, often in the same sentence, and it is easy to come away thinking they are all the same kind of company. They are not. Making a chip is split across several kinds of organisation, each with very different skills, equipment and economics, and they cooperate through a small number of well-defined handoffs. Knowing who does what tells you where different engineering jobs sit and what each one needs to learn.

Why the work is split

In the early decades of the industry, one company often did everything: designed the chip, ran its own fab, packaged and tested the parts. That model still exists, and such firms are called integrated device manufacturers. But a modern wafer fab is enormously expensive to build and must run close to full capacity to pay for itself. Few design teams can fill a fab on their own.

The industry's answer was specialisation. Some companies run fabs and sell manufacturing capacity to many customers. Others only design. Still others package and test. Specialists in software tools and reusable circuit blocks serve all of them. Each can focus on what it is good at and spread its fixed costs across many customers.

The design company

A fabless company designs chips but owns no fab. Its engineers write the specification, design digital and analog blocks, verify them, do physical design and produce the final layout, usually as a GDS or OASIS file. It also writes test programs, works out how the part will be packaged, characterises samples and supports customers.

What it receives: tools from EDA providers, reusable blocks from IP providers, and from the foundry a process design kit (PDK) describing the transistors, design rules and standard cells for one process. What it delivers: the layout to the foundry, and test specifications and package requirements to the assembly and test house.

India has long had a large community of design engineers working on chips, whether in design companies or in design centres of larger firms. Most VLSI jobs that students see advertised, in RTL design, verification, physical design and analog layout, sit in this part of the chain.

The foundry

A foundry manufactures wafers for other companies. It develops and maintains processes, publishes PDKs, runs mask making (directly or through partners) and processes wafers through hundreds of steps. It checks each lot with test structures on the wafer and gives customers data showing the process stayed within its limits.

The foundry's skills are materials science, process engineering, equipment and yield. It does not know or care what your chip does; it guarantees that if your layout obeys its rules, the transistors will behave as its models say, within stated variation. That guarantee is why design rule checks are non-negotiable.

Assembly and test

OSAT stands for outsourced semiconductor assembly and test. These facilities receive finished wafers, test each die on the wafer, cut the wafer into dies, mount each die in a package, connect it, seal it, and test the packaged part again before shipping. Their skills are packaging technology, high-volume test, handling equipment and process control.

Packaging is not just a box around the die. It carries heat away, sets electrical parasitics on every pin, and determines how the chip is soldered onto a board. Advanced packages that stack dies or put several chiplets side by side are becoming as important to performance as the chip itself, which makes this part of the chain increasingly design-heavy.

EDA and IP providers

EDA companies make the software that every other part of the chain depends on: simulators, synthesis, place-and-route, timing analysis, physical verification and test generation. IP providers sell or license pre-designed blocks such as processor cores, memory compilers, interface controllers and analog blocks like PLLs and data converters, so a design company does not design every block from scratch.

Open-source tools and open PDKs now offer a parallel path for learning and small designs. Yosys, OpenROAD, Magic, KLayout and Netgen, with PDKs such as sky130 and gf180, let you see every file that passes between stages, which makes the handoffs easy to study.

What passes between them

From To What is handed over
Foundry Design company PDK: models, rules, cell libraries
Design company Foundry Layout file, mask data, process options
Foundry Assembly and test Wafers, wafer maps, process data
Design company Assembly and test Package drawing, bond diagram, test program

Most painful delays in real projects happen at these boundaries: a test program not ready when wafers arrive, a bond diagram that does not match the pad ring, a PDK version mismatch. Engineers who understand both sides of a handoff are valuable for exactly that reason.

Try it yourself

  1. Install the sky130 PDK and open its documentation; list what the foundry is telling designers (rules, models, cells).
  2. Open a sample GDS in KLayout and look at the layers: this file is what a design company sends a foundry.
  3. Pick a chip you use, read its datasheet's package section, and sketch which parts of the chain produced each piece of information on that page.
