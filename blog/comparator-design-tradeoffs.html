Comparators: speed, offset, hysteresis and kickback

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A comparator turns a tiny voltage difference into a clean digital decision. Compare static and latched designs, see where input offset and kickback come from, and size hysteresis that stops chattering.

In this article
  1. Static and latched comparators
  2. Offset: the error you cannot average away
  3. Hysteresis against chattering
  4. Kickback: the comparator disturbs its input
  5. Try it yourself

A comparator answers one question: is input A higher than input B? Every ADC, every brown-out detector, every zero-crossing detector and many sensor wake-up circuits rely on it. It looks like an op-amp without feedback, but its design goals are different: it must make a decision quickly, make it correctly for inputs of a millivolt or less, and not disturb the signal it is measuring. Four properties capture those goals, and they pull against each other.

Static and latched comparators

A static (continuous-time) comparator is a chain of high-gain amplifier stages running open loop. Its output follows the input continuously, and the decision is reached when the input difference, multiplied by the total gain, is large enough to drive the output to a rail. It needs no clock and suits slowly varying signals such as a battery voltage. Its weakness is speed for small inputs: a stage with large gain has limited bandwidth, so a tiny difference takes a long time to grow.

A latched (dynamic) comparator uses positive feedback. A common form is the StrongARM latch: a differential input pair sits under two cross-coupled inverters. In the reset phase of the clock, the internal nodes are precharged and no static current flows. When the clock edge arrives, the input pair starts to unbalance the two sides, and the cross-coupled inverters amplify whatever imbalance exists. Positive feedback makes the difference grow exponentially with a time constant τ set by the inverters' transconductance and capacitance:

v(t) ≈ v0 × et/τ, so the decision time is t ≈ τ × ln(Vlogic / v0)

The logarithm is the reason latches are fast. With an illustrative τ of 20 ps, resolving an initial 1 mV imbalance to 1 V takes 20 ps × ln(1000) ≈ 138 ps. A thousand times smaller input, 1 µV, takes only twice as long, about 276 ps. A latched comparator draws current only while deciding, which is why SAR ADCs use them almost universally.

The logarithm also hides a trap. As the input approaches zero, the decision time grows without bound. If the clock period ends before the latch has resolved, the output is neither 0 nor 1. That is metastability, the same phenomenon that troubles flip-flops, and designers budget for it with enough time per decision and a small error probability.

Offset: the error you cannot average away

Two input transistors drawn identically still differ slightly in threshold voltage and current factor. The comparator then switches not at zero difference but at some input offset voltage Vos, which is different on every die. In a latch, mismatch in the cross-coupled pair adds more offset, although its effect is divided by the gain of the input pair in front of it.

Three standard remedies:

  • Larger devices. Threshold mismatch falls roughly as one over the square root of gate area, so four times the area halves the offset. The cost is capacitance, which slows the comparator and increases kickback.
  • A preamplifier. A modest-gain differential stage before the latch makes the latch's own offset look smaller when referred to the input.
  • Offset cancellation. In an auto-zero scheme, the comparator periodically shorts its inputs, stores the resulting offset on a capacitor and subtracts it during the measurement phase. Digital calibration does the same by trimming a small current or capacitor until the output toggles at zero input.

Offset matters relative to the application. For an ADC, it should be well below half an LSB of the converter, or calibrated out. For a battery-low detector, a few millivolts may be acceptable.

Hysteresis against chattering

A slow, noisy signal crossing a threshold causes trouble. Near the crossing, noise carries the input back and forth across the threshold, and a fast comparator faithfully reports every crossing as a burst of transitions. Downstream logic sees many edges where one was meant.

The fix is hysteresis: two thresholds instead of one. Once the output goes high, the threshold for going low again is set lower, and vice versa. Noise smaller than the gap between thresholds can no longer cause a second transition.

Here is a worked example of the classic way to add hysteresis around a comparator with a rail-to-rail push-pull output (0 V to 3.3 V). The signal drives the inverting input. The non-inverting input connects to a 1.65 V reference through R1 = 10 kΩ and to the output through R2 = 100 kΩ. By superposition, the non-inverting input sits at:

V+ = Vref × R2/(R1 + R2) + Vout × R1/(R1 + R2) = 1.5 V + 0.0909 × Vout

With the output high (3.3 V), the threshold is 1.5 + 0.30 = 1.8 V. With the output low, it is 1.5 V. The hysteresis is 0.3 V, centred at 1.65 V. Choose the gap larger than the peak-to-peak noise you expect, but small enough that the shifted thresholds still meet your accuracy needs. Inside a chip, the same effect is often built into the circuit itself, for example by a small positive-feedback pair in the preamplifier.

Kickback: the comparator disturbs its input

When a latch fires, its internal nodes swing by the full supply in tens of picoseconds. That swing couples back through the gate-drain capacitance of the input transistors onto the inputs. This kickback injects a small charge into whatever drives the comparator. If the source has low impedance, it absorbs the charge harmlessly. If the source is a high-impedance node, such as the capacitor array of a SAR ADC or a resistor ladder in a flash ADC, the voltage on it is disturbed, possibly enough to corrupt the next decision.

Remedies include a preamplifier that isolates the latch from the input, switches that disconnect the latch at the decision instant, and smaller input devices. That last one conflicts with low offset, which needs larger devices. This tension sits at the heart of comparator design: low offset wants big transistors, speed and low kickback want small ones.

Try it yourself

  1. In ngspice, model the external hysteresis circuit above using an ideal comparator made from a behavioural source (a B source with a tanh of large gain works), feed it a slow ramp plus noise, and confirm the 1.5 V and 1.8 V thresholds.
  2. Remove R2 and watch the output chatter on the same noisy ramp.
  3. Build a StrongARM latch with sky130 devices, apply input differences of 10 mV, 1 mV and 0.1 mV, and plot the decision time against the logarithm of the input. Check that the points fall on a straight line, and estimate τ from its slope.
