Op-amp stability and phase margin without the heavy maths

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Why an op-amp in feedback can ring or oscillate, explained with poles, loop gain and a Bode plot. Learn to read phase margin, why unity gain is the hardest case, and how compensation trades speed for stability.

Negative feedback is what makes op-amp circuits precise: the gain is set by two resistors instead of by transistors that vary with process and temperature. But feedback has a dark side. If the signal coming back around the loop is delayed enough, negative feedback turns into positive feedback and the circuit rings or oscillates. Phase margin is the number that tells you how far you are from that edge. You can understand it fully with a sketch and a little arithmetic.

The loop and the condition for trouble

An op-amp with open-loop gain A(f) drives a feedback network that returns a fraction β of the output to the inverting input. For a non-inverting amplifier with resistors R1 to ground and R2 to the output, β = R1 / (R1 + R2). A unity-gain buffer has β = 1.

The quantity that matters is the loop gain T = A·β: what a signal experiences going once around the loop. While |T| is large, the closed-loop gain is close to 1/β, which is why feedback works.

Trouble comes from phase shift. Negative feedback means the returned signal is inverted, a phase of 180°. Each pole in the amplifier adds up to another 90° of lag at high frequency. If the extra lag reaches 180° at a frequency where |T| is still 1 or more, the returned signal arrives in phase with the input, adds to it, and sustains itself. That is an oscillator.

So check one frequency: the crossover frequency fc, where |T| = 1. Look at the phase of T there.

Phase margin = 180° + phase of T at fc

A phase margin of 0° is an oscillator. A small positive margin is a circuit that rings badly on every step. Around 60° gives a clean step response with little overshoot, which is why it is a common design target.

Poles on a Bode plot

A pole at frequency p does two things to the gain beyond it: it bends the magnitude down by 20 dB per decade and it adds a lag that grows to 90°, reaching 45° exactly at p. Op-amps have several poles. The first, or dominant, pole p1 sits at a low frequency; the second p2 sits much higher.

With only the dominant pole, the phase settles at −90° and the margin is a comfortable 90°. The second pole causes the danger. If p2 sits well above the crossover, it adds only a little lag at fc. If it sits near or below fc, it adds 45° or more and the margin collapses.

A worked example

Take an amplifier with DC gain A0 = 10,000 (80 dB), a dominant pole at 1 kHz, and connect it as a unity-gain buffer, β = 1. These numbers are illustrative. With one pole, the gain would cross 1 at about A0 × p1 = 10 MHz, the unity-gain frequency. Now vary the second pole and compute the actual crossover and margin:

Second pole p2 Crossover fc Phase margin
3 MHz 5.1 MHz 30.6°
10 MHz 7.9 MHz 51.8°
22 MHz 9.2 MHz 67.3°
30 MHz 9.5 MHz 72.4°

The rule that falls out: keep the second pole comfortably above the unity-gain frequency, by a factor of about two or more for a healthy margin. The table comes from a few lines of Python that you can extend with more poles:

from math import atan, degrees, hypot, sqrt

def loop_gain_mag(f, a0, beta, p1, p2):
    return a0 * beta / (hypot(1, f / p1) * hypot(1, f / p2))

def phase_margin(a0, beta, p1, p2):
    lo, hi = 1.0, 1e12             # bisection for |A*beta| = 1
    for _ in range(200):
        f = sqrt(lo * hi)
        if loop_gain_mag(f, a0, beta, p1, p2) > 1:
            lo = f
        else:
            hi = f
    phase = -degrees(atan(f / p1)) - degrees(atan(f / p2))
    return f, 180 + phase

for p2 in (3e6, 10e6, 22e6, 30e6):
    fc, pm = phase_margin(a0=1e4, beta=1.0, p1=1e3, p2=p2)
    print(f"p2 = {p2/1e6:4.0f} MHz: crossover {fc/1e6:4.1f} MHz, "
          f"PM {pm:4.1f} deg")

Why unity gain is the hardest case

Feedback with β = 0.1 (a gain-of-10 amplifier) lowers the loop gain by 20 dB, so the magnitude line crosses 0 dB a decade earlier. Run the code with β = 0.1 and p2 = 10 MHz: crossover drops to about 1 MHz and the margin rises to about 84°. A unity-gain buffer has the largest loop gain of all and therefore the highest crossover. That is why an op-amp is usually specified as "unity-gain stable" or not, and why a design must be checked in the feedback configuration it will actually be used in.

Compensation: buying margin with speed

If the second pole is fixed by the circuit, the remaining lever is the dominant pole. Moving p1 lower pulls the whole magnitude line down, so crossover happens earlier, further below p2. In the example with p2 = 10 MHz, lowering p1 from 1 kHz to 700 Hz moves crossover to about 6.0 MHz and lifts the margin to about 59°.

That is compensation, and its price is visible in the numbers: the amplifier now has less bandwidth. In a two-stage op-amp the standard technique is Miller compensation, a capacitor across the second stage. It pushes the dominant pole down and, as a bonus, pushes the second pole up, so you lose less speed than by lowering p1 alone. It also creates a right-half-plane zero that adds lag, which designers tame with a resistor in series with the capacitor.

Pitfalls to avoid:

  • Checking stability with no load capacitance; a capacitive load lowers the output pole and can erase the margin
  • Simulating closed-loop gain only; a peaky frequency response is a symptom, but phase margin is measured on the open loop
  • Testing at one corner; poles move with bias current, temperature and process

Try it yourself

  1. Run the Python above and add a third pole. Find how high it must be before it stops mattering.
  2. In ngspice, model a two-pole amplifier with two voltage-controlled sources and RC sections, run ac dec 50 1 1G, and find the frequency where the magnitude crosses 0 dB with meas ac and its phase there.
  3. Close the loop as a unity-gain buffer, run a tran with a small step, and compare the overshoot for margins near 30° and near 60°.
