From schematic to Gerbers: designing a first board in KiCad

Systems & product engineering Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

The full path of a first circuit board in KiCad, using a small 3.3 V regulator board as the example, from symbols and footprints through layout and rule checks to the Gerber and drill files a fabricator needs.

Designing your first circuit board feels like a large leap from breadboards, but the process is a fixed sequence of steps, and KiCad, a free and open-source design suite, supports every one of them. The trick is to treat each step as a checkpoint with its own check, so mistakes are caught where they are made rather than when the boards arrive. This article walks through the sequence with a deliberately small example: a board that takes 5 V from a USB connector and provides 3.3 V with a power indicator LED.

The flow at a glance

Schematic: symbols, values and the rules check

Start a new project and open the schematic editor. Place a symbol for each part: a USB connector, a 3.3 V low-dropout regulator, its input and output capacitors, an LED and its resistor. A symbol is the logical view of a part: its pins and their names, with no physical size.

Wire the symbols together, or use labels for nets that run across the sheet. Give every part a value and a meaningful reference (U1, C1, R1, D1). Work out values by hand first. For the LED, if it drops about 2.0 V at the current you want, a resistor from the 3.3 V rail sets the current:

R = (3.3 V − 2.0 V) / 2 mA = 650 Ω

The nearest common value above is 680 Ω, giving (3.3 − 2.0) / 680 ≈ 1.9 mA, plenty for an indicator. The capacitor values come from the regulator's datasheet, which also tells you whether it needs a particular capacitor type to be stable. Read that section carefully; it is a common cause of a regulator that oscillates.

Then run the electrical rules check (ERC). It flags unconnected pins, outputs driving outputs, and power pins with nothing driving them. That last warning often appears on power nets that come from a connector; KiCad expects a "power flag" symbol to mark where power enters. Understand each warning before silencing it.

Footprints: the physical view

Each symbol needs a footprint: the copper pads, outline and markings the part will be soldered to. Use the footprint assignment tool to pick one for every symbol. This is where many first boards go wrong, because the same symbol can come in different packages. A regulator in one package may have its pins in a different order from the same regulator in another. Open the part's datasheet, find the exact package drawing, and check that the footprint's pad count, pitch and pin 1 match. KiCad's standard libraries cover most common packages; prefer them to drawing your own.

Layout and the design rules check

Placing and routing

Update the board from the schematic and the parts appear, joined by thin lines called the ratsnest, showing which pads must connect. Then:

  1. Draw the board outline on the edge-cuts layer, sized for its enclosure or mounting.
  2. Place parts before routing. Connectors at the edges where cables will reach. The regulator's input and output capacitors right beside its pins. The LED where it can be seen.
  3. Set track widths in the board setup. Power tracks carry more current than signal tracks and should be wider; your fabricator's minimum track and gap set the floor.
  4. Route, keeping power paths short and direct. On a two-layer board, a ground pour on the bottom layer, connected with vias to every ground pad, gives every signal a short return path.
  5. Add mounting holes, text and markings: board name, revision and pin 1 or polarity marks visible after assembly.

Design rules check

Load your fabricator's published limits (minimum track width, spacing, drill size and annular ring) into the board setup, then run the design rules check (DRC). It reports tracks too close together, unrouted connections, pads overlapping and copper too near the board edge. Fix every error. For each warning, either fix it or be able to explain why it is safe. A clean DRC is the minimum, not a guarantee: it cannot tell you that a footprint matches the real part, so review that separately.

Fabrication files

A fabricator needs Gerber files, one per copper, mask, silkscreen and outline layer, plus a drill file listing hole positions and sizes. KiCad generates both from the plot and drill dialogs in the board editor, or from the command line with kicad-cli (the rule-check commands need KiCad 8 or later):

kicad-cli sch erc power_board.kicad_sch
kicad-cli pcb drc power_board.kicad_pcb
mkdir -p fab
kicad-cli pcb export gerbers --output fab/ power_board.kicad_pcb
kicad-cli pcb export drill --output fab/ power_board.kicad_pcb

Before uploading, open the output in KiCad's own Gerber viewer and look at each layer. Check that the outline is closed, the drill holes line up with pads, and the silkscreen is not printed over pads. Zip the folder and keep it, with the project files, under version control, tagged with the board revision.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong footprint for the actual package. The most common first-board failure; check every one against the datasheet drawing.
  • Silencing ERC or DRC warnings without understanding them.
  • Routing before placing. Placement decides most of a board's quality.
  • Forgetting the revision number. Once two versions exist, unmarked boards cause confusion.

Try it yourself

  1. Install KiCad, build the 3.3 V board described here, and run ERC until it is clean.
  2. Lay it out on a two-layer board no larger than 30 × 20 mm, run DRC with a fabricator's published rules, and fix every error.
  3. Export Gerbers and drill files, open them in KiCad's Gerber viewer, and ask a classmate to review the layers against the checklist in the PCB design review article before you order anything.
