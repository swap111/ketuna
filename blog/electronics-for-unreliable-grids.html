Power cuts, surges and voltage swings: designing electronics for real-world grids

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How to design electronics that survive power cuts, surges and wide voltage swings, using brown-out detection with hysteresis, layered surge protection, wide-input supplies and a hold-up budget for saving state.

In this article

  2. Brown-out detection with hysteresis

  5. Wide-input supplies and safe restart
  6. Try it yourself

A product designed against a clean, steady bench supply often fails in its first week in a real home, shop or farm. The mains voltage sags in the evening and rises at night, the supply cuts out and comes back several times in a minute, a pump motor or lightning strike sends a spike down the line, and a generator or inverter takes over with its own idea of a sine wave. Electronics that work in these conditions are not exotic. They follow a handful of principles, applied from the first schematic.

What the device actually sees

It helps to name the disturbances separately, because each needs a different defence:

Disturbance What happens Main defence
Sustained under- or over-voltage Mains sits well away from nominal for minutes or hours Wide-input power supply
Brown-out Supply sags slowly, or dips briefly, then recovers Brown-out detection with hysteresis
Outage Supply disappears, sometimes repeatedly Hold-up energy and safe state saving
Surge and transient Short high-energy spike from switching or lightning Layered surge protection
Inrush on restore Large current when capacitors charge at power-up Inrush limiting

The worst moments are often not the outage itself but the transitions: the slow fall as the supply dies, and the bouncy return when it comes back.

Brown-out detection with hysteresis

A microcontroller running below its minimum voltage can execute garbage. It may corrupt flash, drive outputs to random states or lock up. Most microcontrollers have a built-in brown-out reset that holds the chip in reset below a threshold. Enable it, always.

For product behaviour you also want an earlier warning, measured on a higher rail or on the rectified input, that gives firmware time to act. The key is hysteresis: use a lower threshold to declare power failing and a higher one to declare it good again. With a single threshold, a supply hovering near it will flip the state hundreds of times a second.

#include <stdbool.h>
#include <stdint.h>

#define V_FAIL_MV   9000u   /* illustrative thresholds on a 12 V rail */
#define V_GOOD_MV  10500u
#define GOOD_COUNT    50u   /* consecutive good samples before restart */

static bool power_ok = false;
static uint16_t good_samples = 0;

void power_monitor_sample(uint32_t rail_mv)  /* call every 1 ms */
{
    if (power_ok) {
        if (rail_mv < V_FAIL_MV) {
            power_ok = false;
            good_samples = 0;
            /* stop motors, save state, stop writing flash */
        }
    } else if (rail_mv > V_GOOD_MV) {
        if (++good_samples >= GOOD_COUNT) {
            power_ok = true;
            /* restart loads in a controlled order */
        }
    } else {
        good_samples = 0;
    }
}

The counter adds a second form of hysteresis, in time: power must stay good for 50 ms before loads restart, so a bouncing supply does not start and stop a motor repeatedly.

The hold-up budget

When power fails, the bulk capacitors keep the electronics running briefly. Whether that is long enough to save state is a calculation, not a hope. The energy available between the rail voltage at failure and the lowest voltage at which your regulator still works is:

E = ½ C (V12 − V22)

An illustrative example: a 470 µF capacitor on a 12 V rail, a regulator that keeps working down to 6 V, and a load of 1 W.

E = 0.5 × 470 µF × (144 − 36) V2 ≈ 25.4 mJ, so t ≈ 25.4 mJ ÷ 1 W ≈ 25 ms

If your flash write and shutdown take 10 ms after the warning fires, you have margin. If a last-minute firmware change makes the save take 40 ms, you do not, and only a measurement on the bench will tell you. Note that the warning threshold eats into this energy too: if you only detect failure at 9 V, recompute with V1 = 9 V, which gives about 10.6 mJ and roughly 10 ms. That is why detection on the rectified input, before the main rail, is often worth the extra parts.

Turn off heavy loads, such as relays, LEDs and radios, the moment power-fail is detected, so the stored energy goes to the processor.

Layered surge protection

Surges are absorbed in layers, from the most robust part at the input to the most sensitive part at the chip:

  1. Fuse at the input, so a failed protection part opens the circuit instead of catching fire
  2. Metal oxide varistor across the line, which clamps large, high-energy surges
  3. Inductance or resistance, such as a common-mode choke or the supply's input filter, which slows the edge and shares the energy
  4. TVS diodes on the low-voltage side and on every external connection, such as sensor lines, data ports and antenna feeds

Two layout rules matter as much as the parts. Keep surge current paths short and wide, straight from the protection device to the return, and do not route surge return currents under sensitive analog circuits. Remember that cables to remote sensors are antennas and surge paths too; protect them where they enter the box.

Varistors wear with each large surge they absorb, so a design that takes frequent surges should be inspected or designed for replacement. For the formal requirements, the IEC standard series on electromagnetic compatibility includes surge immunity tests; check the current edition that applies to your product.

Wide-input supplies and safe restart

A switching supply designed for a wide input range rides through sags and swells that would push a narrow-range design out of regulation or overheat a linear regulator. Check its behaviour at both ends of the range at full load and at high ambient temperature, not just at nominal.

On restore, limit inrush with an NTC thermistor or an active circuit, so the charging capacitors do not blow the fuse or weld relay contacts. Then bring loads back in a defined order, after the hysteresis delay. A product that comes back from an outage the same way every time is far easier to support than one that sometimes does not come back at all.

Mains work is dangerous. Build and test mains circuits only with qualified supervision and isolated test equipment.

Try it yourself

  1. Recompute the hold-up example for your own design and measure it: trigger an oscilloscope on the power-fail signal and see how long the rail stays above your regulator's minimum.
  2. Simulate a slowly falling supply driving your brown-out logic in ngspice, or feed recorded voltage samples into the C function above compiled with GCC on your PC.
  3. In KiCad, review your board's surge path from connector to protection device to return, and shorten it.
