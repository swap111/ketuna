Satellite navigation for timing: how receivers discipline clocks for grids and networks

India's electronics ecosystem Advanced 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How a timing receiver uses satellite time to steer a local oscillator, why the loop trades short-term and long-term stability, what happens in holdover when signals are lost, and why grids and networks depend on it.

Most people think of satellite navigation as a way to find position. For a large class of infrastructure, position is a by-product and time is the product. Mobile networks need base stations aligned in time so their transmissions do not collide; power grids compare the phase of voltages measured hundreds of kilometres apart, which only works if every measurement carries an accurate timestamp; data centres and financial systems order events by time. A satellite receiver at a fixed site can deliver time traceable to a national or international reference, anywhere with sky view. This article explains how a receiver turns satellite signals into a stable local clock and what happens when the signals disappear.

Why a receiver alone is not a good clock

A navigation receiver solves for its clock bias along with its position, so after each fix it knows the offset between its own clock and the system time. Most timing receivers output this as a one-pulse-per-second (1PPS) signal, whose edge is aligned to the start of each second, plus a message saying which second it was.

But the 1PPS edge from a receiver is noisy. Each fix carries measurement noise, multipath and the effects of changing satellite geometry, so the edge jitters by some tens of nanoseconds from one second to the next. It is accurate on average over long periods but not stable in the short term. A local quartz or atomic oscillator has the opposite character: very smooth from second to second, but it drifts over hours and days as it ages and as temperature changes.

The solution is to combine them, letting the satellite time correct the oscillator's long-term drift while the oscillator provides short-term smoothness. This is called disciplining the oscillator.

The disciplining loop

The loop is a phase-locked loop with a very long time constant. Each second, a time-interval counter measures the difference between the receiver's 1PPS and a 1PPS derived from the local oscillator, typically with a resolution of a few nanoseconds or better. A loop filter, usually proportional-integral so it can remove a constant frequency error completely, computes a frequency correction and applies it to the oscillator, either through a control voltage or digitally by adjusting a synthesiser.

The time constant is the central design choice. A short one follows the receiver closely, passing its jitter straight through. A long one averages the receiver noise away but lets the oscillator's own wander through for longer before correcting it. The best value is where the two noise sources cross, and it depends on the oscillator: a cheap crystal oscillator wants a short time constant, while a high-quality oven-controlled or atomic oscillator can average for much longer.

A worked simulation, including holdover

The script below disciplines a made-up oscillator with an initial frequency error and slow ageing, compares it each second against a receiver 1PPS with 20 ns of noise, and steers it with a PI loop. At t = 2000 s the satellite signal is lost and the controller enters holdover, freezing its last correction. All values are illustrative.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
T = 3000                         # seconds simulated, one step per second
f_err = 2e-8                     # oscillator's initial fractional error
aging = 1e-12                    # fractional drift per second, made up
kp, ki = 0.05, 0.001             # loop gains, chosen for illustration

phase, steer, integ = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
log = {}
for t in range(T):
    f_err += aging                         # the crystal slowly ages
    phase += f_err - steer                 # seconds of error gained
    have_gnss = t < 2000                   # signal lost at t = 2000
    if have_gnss:
        meas = phase + rng.normal(0, 20e-9)    # 1PPS comparison, 20 ns noise
        integ += meas
        steer = kp * meas + ki * integ     # PI loop sets the correction
    # in holdover, steer is simply frozen at its last value
    if t in (10, 200, 1000, 1999, 2300, 2999):
        log[t] = phase * 1e9
for t, ns in log.items():
    print(t, round(ns))
Time (s) Phase error (ns) State
10 170 Locking: initial frequency error still present
200 −8 Locked
1000 −2 Locked
1999 0 Locked, just before the signal is lost
2300 326 Holdover
2999 1431 Holdover

While locked, the error stays within nanoseconds even though each measurement is noisy by 20 ns: the loop averages. In holdover, the frozen correction no longer matches the ageing oscillator, so the frequency error grows linearly and the phase error grows roughly with the square of time. After about 1000 seconds the clock is more than a microsecond off.

Holdover: designing for lost signals

Signals are lost for ordinary reasons: a blocked antenna, a cable fault, an equipment failure, interference or deliberate jamming. A timing system must keep operating within its specification for a defined holdover period. Three things determine how long it can:

  • Oscillator quality: a better oscillator ages more slowly and is less sensitive to temperature, so it drifts less while unsteered
  • Learning the drift: a controller that has estimated the ageing rate while locked can keep applying it in holdover, which in the simulation would remove most of the error
  • Temperature stability: keeping the oscillator's environment steady, or compensating with a temperature model

Holdover requirements, together with the accuracy the application needs, usually decide the oscillator, and the oscillator is often the most expensive part of a timing receiver.

Why timing users care, and what they worry about

Because satellite signals arrive extremely weak, they are easy to jam and can be spoofed with fake signals. Critical timing users therefore combine sources: receivers for several constellations, including NavIC over India, which adds a regionally controlled source; time delivered over networks using the precision time protocol (IEEE 1588); and good holdover oscillators. Timing receivers also check consistency, flagging sudden jumps in time or position that a genuinely fixed antenna should never show. The antenna position is surveyed once and then held fixed, so every satellite measured contributes to time alone.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the simulation, then change the loop gains to make the loop faster and slower and compare the locked phase noise.
  2. Add drift estimation: track the integrator's slope while locked and keep extrapolating it in holdover, and compare the holdover error.
  3. Feed a recorded 1PPS-versus-oscillator time-interval log, from a counter or a microcontroller timer capture, into the same loop.
