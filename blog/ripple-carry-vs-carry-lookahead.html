Ripple-carry vs carry-lookahead: where the delay goes in an adder

Digital & VLSI design Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

An adder's delay is the time for a carry to travel. See why ripple-carry grows linearly with width, how generate and propagate signals let lookahead and prefix adders grow logarithmically, and when ripple is still right.

Adding two numbers looks instantaneous in a Verilog +, but in gates it has a clear critical path: the carry. The sum bit at the top of a word cannot be known until the carry into it is known, and that carry depends on every bit below it. Every fast adder architecture is a different answer to one question: how do you find the carries without waiting for them to ripple?

The ripple-carry adder

Chain N full adders, connecting each carry-out to the next carry-in. Each full adder computes:

si = ai ⊕ bi ⊕ ci, ci+1 = aibi + ci(ai ⊕ bi)

The worst case is a carry that starts at bit 0 and travels all the way up, for example adding 1 to 0111…1. If one carry stage takes tc, an N-bit ripple adder takes about N × tc. With an illustrative tc of 0.1 ns, a 32-bit ripple adder needs about 3.2 ns just for the carry chain, and a 64-bit one about 6.4 ns. Doubling the width doubles the delay.

Its strength is that it is tiny: N full adders and nothing else, with short local wires.

Generate and propagate

The trick behind every faster adder is to ask, for each bit position, two questions that do not depend on the incoming carry:

  • Generate gi = ai · bi: this bit creates a carry on its own (both inputs 1).
  • Propagate pi = ai ⊕ bi: this bit passes an incoming carry through (exactly one input 1).

Both are computed for all bits in parallel, in one gate delay. Then the carry recurrence becomes ci+1 = gi + pi · ci, and you can expand it:

c2 = g1 + p1g0 + p1p0c0

c4 = g3 + p3g2 + p3p2g1 + p3p2p1g0 + p3p2p1p0c0

Read c4 aloud: "a carry comes out of bit 3 if bit 3 generates one, or bit 2 generates one and bit 3 propagates it, or…". Each term is an AND, the whole is an OR, so c4 takes a fixed two levels of logic regardless of the data. That is a carry-lookahead block.

Why lookahead does not scale directly

The expansion for c32 would have 33 terms, the largest an AND of 32 inputs. Gates with that many inputs are slow and large, so flattening the whole carry is not practical. Instead, designs group bits. A 4-bit lookahead block produces a group generate and propagate:

G = g3 + p3g2 + p3p2g1 + p3p2p1g0, P = p3p2p1p0

A second level of lookahead then works on those group signals exactly as the first level worked on bits. Each level multiplies the reach by four, so a 64-bit adder needs three levels of lookahead instead of 64 ripple stages.

Prefix adders

The cleanest way to see this is as a prefix computation. Define an operator ∘ that combines two (g, p) pairs, a higher span (gh, ph) and a lower span (gl, pl):

(gh, ph) ∘ (gl, pl) = (gh + phgl, phpl)

The operator is associative, so the carry into every bit is a prefix "sum" of all the (g, p) pairs below it, and any parallel-prefix tree can compute all of them. With a tree, the number of ∘ levels is log2 N: 5 for 32 bits, 6 for 64 bits. Named prefix structures, such as Kogge-Stone, Brent-Kung and Sklansky, are different trees that trade the number of ∘ cells, the wiring and the fan-out against each other. Kogge-Stone has minimum depth and many long wires; Brent-Kung has far fewer cells but roughly twice the depth.

When ripple is the right answer

Logarithmic delay sounds strictly better, but you pay in area, power and wiring. Ripple is often the right choice when:

  • The adder is narrow. For 8 bits, the carry chain is short, and the extra logic of a prefix tree buys little.
  • The path has slack. If the adder sits in a path with plenty of timing margin, a smaller adder saves area and switching power.
  • Synthesis decides anyway. When you write a + b, synthesis tools pick an adder architecture from the timing constraint. Tight constraints push them to faster structures; relaxed ones give you something close to ripple. Writing a hand-built prefix adder in RTL is usually only worth it when you have measured that the tool's choice does not meet timing.
  • FPGAs. FPGA fabrics provide dedicated fast carry chains, which make ripple-style adders quick in that technology.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a + in RTL is always a ripple adder; the netlist depends on the constraint
  • Counting only gate levels and ignoring the long wires and high fan-out in wide prefix trees
  • Forgetting the final XOR: si = pi ⊕ ci still has to be computed after the carries

Try it yourself

  1. Write a 16-bit ripple adder in Verilog with a generate loop of full adders, and a prefix version that computes (g, p) and combines them in four levels. Check both against a + b for random inputs in Icarus Verilog.
  2. Synthesise both with Yosys against a sky130 library and compare cell counts with stat.
  3. Time both in OpenSTA and compare the critical path delays. Predict the ratio before you look.
