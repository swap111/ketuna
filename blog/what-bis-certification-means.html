What BIS registration and certification mean for an electronics product

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

What India's national standards body does, how product registration and certification work in outline, what an engineer must prepare for them, and why you must check the current product lists yourself.

You can design a perfectly working power adapter, LED driver or tablet and still not be allowed to sell it in India. Many categories of electronic goods must carry a mark showing they have been tested against an Indian Standard before they can be manufactured, imported or sold. The organisation behind that mark is the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS). For engineers this is not paperwork to hand to someone else at the end: the requirements shape the design, and a failed test can push a launch back by months. This article explains, in outline, what BIS does and how certification works.

What the national standards body does

BIS is India's national standards body. It does three related things:

  • Writes and publishes standards. Indian Standards, written as IS followed by a number, cover products, materials, test methods and practices. Technical committees of experts from industry, labs, academia and government draft and revise them.
  • Runs conformity assessment schemes. These are the processes by which a manufacturer shows that a product meets a standard and earns the right to use a mark.
  • Recognises laboratories. Testing for certification must be done in labs that BIS has recognised for the relevant standard.

Many Indian Standards for electrical and electronic products are adopted from international standards, especially those of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), sometimes identically and sometimes with national changes. So if you already know an IEC safety standard, the Indian version will feel familiar, but you must test against the Indian edition that is in force.

Voluntary and compulsory certification

Using a standard is voluntary in principle. It becomes compulsory when the government notifies a product category through a quality control order or similar instrument. Once a category is notified, products in it cannot be made, imported or sold without the required certification.

For electronics, two kinds of scheme are commonly involved, described here only in outline:

Scheme type How it works in outline Typical marking
Registration-based Samples tested in a recognised lab; manufacturer registers each model on the basis of the test report Standard mark with a registration number
Licence-based product certification Testing plus assessment of the factory and its quality control, with ongoing surveillance The familiar standard mark with a licence number

Which scheme applies, which standard is referenced and which products are covered all change over time as new orders are issued and standards are revised. Always check the current official lists and rules on the BIS website before planning a product. Do not rely on a blog post, a forum thread or what a colleague remembers from a previous project.

How registration works, step by step

For a registration-based scheme, the outline process looks like this:

  1. Identify the category and standard. Find out whether your product falls in a notified category and which Indian Standard applies. A charger, a power bank and a monitor may each fall under different entries.
  2. Prepare samples and documents. The lab needs production-representative samples, not prototypes, plus documentation such as a parts list, circuit diagrams, ratings and markings.
  3. Test in a recognised lab. The lab tests the samples against the standard and issues a report.
  4. Apply for registration. The manufacturer applies online with the test report and supporting documents. Foreign manufacturers typically need an authorised representative in India.
  5. Mark the product. Once registered, the product and packaging must carry the mark and registration number in the prescribed way.
  6. Maintain it. Changes to critical components or the design can require retesting or notifying BIS, and registrations must be renewed.

Step 6 catches teams out. If you change the transformer supplier in your adapter, or swap a safety-critical capacitor for a cheaper one, you may have changed the certified product.

What it means for design engineers

Most safety standards for electronic equipment are about preventing fire, electric shock and injury under normal use and reasonably foreseeable faults. They translate into concrete design rules:

  • Creepage and clearance: minimum distances on the board and in the enclosure between mains and touchable low-voltage parts.
  • Insulation: the right insulation class for transformers and optocouplers bridging that barrier.
  • Component ratings: safety-critical parts, such as fuses, mains capacitors and connectors, chosen with appropriate approvals and ratings.
  • Fault behaviour: what happens when a component fails short or open, or a fan stops; the product must fail safe.
  • Markings and instructions: ratings labels, warnings and user manual content.

If you lay out a power supply board without the creepage distances the standard requires, no amount of testing will save it; you will be re-spinning the board. That is why the standard belongs in the design review, not just the test plan.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a product certified elsewhere is automatically acceptable in India
  • Sending prototypes for testing instead of production-representative samples
  • Changing a critical component after certification without checking the rules
  • Leaving certification until the product is ready to ship, then discovering the lab queue
  • Treating certification as purely a business task with no design input

Try it yourself

  1. Visit the official BIS website and find the current list of products under compulsory registration. Check whether a phone charger, a laptop and an LED lamp are listed and under which standard.
  2. Pick an adapter you own and read its label. Identify the ratings, the mark and the registration number, and note what each tells you.
  3. Draw your own small mains-powered design in KiCad and mark the isolation barrier. Use the design rule checker with a custom clearance rule between the mains and low-voltage nets.
  4. Write a short pre-certification checklist for that design: standard, samples, documents, critical components and markings.
