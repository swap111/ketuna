Reading and citing standards: IS, IEC and IEEE documents for engineers

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How standards are numbered, revised and adopted across national and international bodies, how to read one without getting lost, and how to cite one so a reader can find exactly the document you used.

Sooner or later every engineer is told to "design to the standard". You open the document, find a hundred pages of numbered clauses, cross-references to other standards and words like shall and informative, and wonder where to start. Standards are not hard to read once you know how they are built, and citing them correctly matters: a requirement that says "per IEC safety rules" cannot be tested, while one that names the exact document and edition can. This article explains the structure behind the numbers.

Who writes standards, and how they relate

Three families come up constantly in electronics:

  • IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) publishes international standards for electrical and electronic technology: safety of equipment, electromagnetic compatibility, components, batteries, and much more.
  • IEEE publishes standards for computing, communications and electronics, such as hardware description languages (for example the SystemVerilog standard, IEEE 1800) and networking.
  • IS (Indian Standards) are published by the Bureau of Indian Standards, India's national standards body.

The relationship between national and international standards is important. Many countries, India included, adopt international standards as national ones rather than writing their own from scratch. An adoption may be identical, with the national number placed alongside the international one, or modified, with national deviations listed. So the Indian Standard for a product may be essentially an IEC standard with an IS number. When regulations make a standard mandatory in India, it is the Indian Standard, in the edition the rules specify, that you must meet.

How standards are numbered and revised

A standard's identifier usually has three parts: the publisher, a number and a version marker. The number identifies the topic and stays the same across revisions; the version marker tells you which edition.

Part Example pattern What it tells you
Publisher prefix IS, IEC, IEEE, or a combination for adoptions Who published this version
Number and part a number, often with -1, -2 for parts The topic, and which part of a multi-part series
Year or edition a year after a colon or hyphen Which revision

Multi-part series are common. The IEC electromagnetic compatibility series, IEC 61000, has parts covering environment, limits, test methods and so on, so "IEC 61000" alone does not identify one document. Always name the part.

Standards change in three ways:

  • New edition: a full revision that replaces the previous edition, often after a transition period during which both are accepted.
  • Amendment: a smaller change published separately, to be read together with the base edition.
  • Corrigendum: a correction of errors, not a change of intent.

When you design against a standard, the version you use is as important as the number. A product tested to a withdrawn edition may no longer be acceptable. Check the current edition and any amendments every time a project starts.

Reading one without getting lost

Most standards follow a similar layout. Read them in this order rather than front to back:

  1. Scope. Does this document actually apply to your product? Many engineers skip this and then argue about requirements that never applied.
  2. Normative references. The other documents that are part of this one's requirements. If a clause says "tested according to" another standard, that one becomes required reading too.
  3. Terms and definitions. Words like accessible part or rated voltage have precise meanings that may differ from everyday use.
  4. The clauses that apply to your design. Use the scope and your product's features to decide which ones.
  5. Annexes. Each annex is marked normative (part of the requirements) or informative (guidance and examples). Know which you are reading.

The verbs carry the meaning. In most standards, shall expresses a requirement, should a recommendation, may a permission and can a possibility. When you turn a standard into a product requirement, only the shall clauses are mandatory, and each one should become a testable line in your requirements list.

Citing a standard properly

A good citation lets a reader find exactly the document you used. Include the publisher and number, the part, the title and the edition or year. In a requirements document, a citation might look like this:

REQ-PSU-012  The power supply shall meet the conducted emission
             limits of [S3], class B, measured on the mains port.

[S3] <Publisher> <number>-<part>:<year>, <title>, including
     amendment <n>. Edition used: as listed in the project's
     standards register, checked <date>.

The placeholders are deliberate: fill them from the actual document on your desk, not from memory. Keep one standards register for the project, a short table listing each standard, its edition, why it applies and who checked it. When a new edition appears, the register tells you what to re-examine.

A few habits help:

  • cite the clause number when a requirement depends on a specific clause
  • do not copy large passages from a standard into your documents; standards are copyrighted, so reference them instead
  • write "check the current edition" in anything that will outlive the project

Common mistakes

  • Citing a series number without the part
  • Leaving out the edition, so nobody knows which version was tested
  • Ignoring the normative references and testing against only half the requirements
  • Treating informative annexes as mandatory, or normative ones as optional
  • Assuming an international standard and its Indian adoption are always identical

Try it yourself

  1. Find the publicly available preview pages or catalogue entry for one IEC standard and identify its scope, normative references and edition.
  2. Look up the SystemVerilog standard, IEEE 1800, in the publisher's catalogue and note how the year is shown.
  3. Create a standards register as a simple spreadsheet or Markdown table for a small project, with columns for identifier, edition, reason and date checked.
  4. Turn three shall clauses from a standard you have access to into testable requirements in your own words.
