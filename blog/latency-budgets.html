Latency budgets: splitting end-to-end response time across subsystems

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A user-visible deadline becomes a set of allowances for sensing, sampling, filtering, processing and actuation that each owner can design to and test, with the worst case, not the average, adding up.

In this article

A requirement like "the valve shall close within 2 s of gas concentration crossing the alarm threshold" is easy to write and surprisingly easy to miss. The delay is spread across a sensor, a sampling loop, a filter, some firmware and a mechanical actuator, each built by a different person who sees only their own piece. Each piece looks fast in isolation; together they are late. A latency budget breaks the end-to-end deadline into allowances that each owner can design to and measure, and it makes the hidden delays visible before integration does.

Draw the chain first

Start by listing every stage the event passes through, in order, from the physical cause to the physical effect. For a gas shut-off controller the chain might be:

  1. Sensor response: the sensing element takes time to respond to a change in concentration.
  2. Sampling wait: the firmware reads the sensor periodically, so a change can sit unseen for up to one sample period.
  3. Filtering: a moving average smooths noise but delays the response.
  4. Decision and command: firmware compares, logs and drives an output pin.
  5. Actuation: the driver energises or de-energises the valve, and the valve physically moves to closed.

Writing the chain down is often where the first mistake is found. Teams routinely forget the sampling wait and the filter, because neither is a "component" on the block diagram.

Worst case adds, averages do not

The budget must use the worst case of each stage, because the requirement is a deadline, not an average. Two stages deserve special care because their worst case is set by design choices rather than by parts:

  • Sampling wait. If the event happens just after a sample, it is not seen until the next one. The worst-case wait is one full sample period, T.
  • Moving-average filter. An N-sample moving average needs N samples after a step before its output reaches the new level. If the step barely exceeds the threshold, the output crosses only at the last of those samples, adding up to (N − 1) × T after the first sample that sees the step.

Both shrink if you sample faster, at the cost of power and processor time. That is exactly the kind of trade the budget is meant to expose.

A worked budget

All numbers here are made up for illustration; real sensor and valve times come from datasheets and measurements. The requirement is 2,000 ms. The firmware samples every T = 100 ms and averages N = 4 samples.

Stage Basis Allowance (ms)
Sensor response to threshold sensor data, worst case 1,000
Sampling wait one period, T 100
Moving-average filter (N − 1) × T 300
Decision, log, output measured on firmware 20
Driver and valve closing valve data, worst case 300
Total 1,720
Margin 2,000 − 1,720 280

The margin is 280 ms, 14 % of the deadline. That is reasonable at concept stage but not generous: if the valve turns out slower at low supply voltage, or the sensor slower when cold, the margin disappears.

The picture also shows where to look for savings. Halving the sample period to 50 ms saves 50 ms of sampling wait and 150 ms of filter delay, 200 ms in total, for a firmware change. Making the firmware ten times faster saves 18 ms. Engineers instinctively optimise the code they wrote; the budget points at the stages that matter.

Turning allowances into tests

Each allowance becomes a requirement for its owner, and each needs a way to measure it. Timing is easiest to measure from outside, with a logic analyser or oscilloscope and spare output pins:

/* Instrumented control loop: toggle pins at stage boundaries. */
void control_tick(void)              /* called every SAMPLE_MS */
{
    gpio_set(PIN_DBG_SAMPLE);        /* rising edge: sample taken */
    int32_t raw = sensor_read();
    int32_t avg = moving_avg_push(raw);
    gpio_clear(PIN_DBG_SAMPLE);

    if (avg > ALARM_THRESHOLD && !valve_closed) {
        gpio_set(PIN_DBG_DECIDE);    /* decision made */
        event_log(EVT_GAS_ALARM, avg);
        valve_close();               /* drives the valve output */
        valve_closed = true;
        gpio_clear(PIN_DBG_DECIDE);
    }
}

On the analyser, the time from the first sample above threshold to the decision pin shows the filter delay; the width of the decision pulse shows firmware time; a valve position switch, if fitted, shows actuation. The gpio_*, sensor_read and valve_close functions stand for your platform's own drivers.

Finally, test end to end. Stage measurements prove each allowance; only an end-to-end test proves that you did not forget a stage. For a real gas safety product, end-to-end testing follows the applicable product standard and uses a controlled test setup; this example is about the budgeting method, not a design to copy.

Common mistakes

  • Adding typical values. Typical plus typical gives a number the product beats most of the time and misses some of the time, which is worse than missing always.
  • Forgetting periodic waits. Every polling loop, scheduler tick, radio slot or message queue between stages adds up to one period.
  • Measuring only at room temperature and nominal supply. Mechanical actuators and sensors slow down at the edges of their operating range.
  • No owner for the margin. If anyone can spend it silently, it will be gone before validation.

Try it yourself

  1. Write the stage chain for a system you have built, even a button that lights an LED through a polling loop, and estimate each stage's worst case.
  2. Add debug pin toggles at each stage boundary, capture them with a low-cost logic analyser and the open-source sigrok software (its PulseView viewer), and compare measured times with your budget.
  3. Change the sample period and filter length in your firmware and confirm that the measured delay moves as the formulas predict.
