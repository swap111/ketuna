Interfacing pH, conductivity and turbidity sensors: the analog side

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Three common water-quality sensors need three different front ends. Learn why a pH electrode needs a near-zero-bias buffer, why conductivity uses AC excitation, and how turbidity sensing rejects ambient light.

In this article
  1. pH: a battery with gigantic internal resistance
  2. Conductivity: measure resistance, but never with DC
  3. Turbidity: a photocurrent in daylight
  4. Try it yourself

A water-quality monitor for a tank, a pond or a treatment plant usually measures at least three things: acidity (pH), dissolved salts (conductivity) and cloudiness (turbidity). On a datasheet they are three numbers. At the analog front end they are three very different problems: a voltage source with an enormous internal resistance, a resistance that must never see DC, and a tiny photocurrent buried in daylight. Getting each interface right matters more than the choice of ADC.

pH: a battery with gigantic internal resistance

A pH electrode pair, a glass measuring electrode and a reference electrode, produces a voltage that depends on the hydrogen-ion activity of the solution. Ideally the voltage changes by the Nernst slope per pH unit:

slope = ln(10) × RT / F ≈ 59.16 mV per pH at 25 °C

where R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature and F the Faraday constant. Near pH 7 the output is close to zero; acidic solutions give positive voltages and alkaline ones negative, for the usual electrode convention. Notice the T: the slope is proportional to absolute temperature, about 61.5 mV per pH at 37 °C. A pH meter without temperature compensation is wrong whenever the water is not at its calibration temperature.

The electrical challenge is the glass membrane. Its resistance is very high, commonly hundreds of megohms, so the electrode is a voltage source with a huge series resistance. Any current drawn from it causes a voltage error. If the amplifier's input bias current is 1 nA and the electrode resistance is 100 MΩ, the error is 1 nA × 100 MΩ = 100 mV, which is about 1.7 pH units. With 1 pA of bias current the error falls to 0.1 mV, about 0.002 pH. These numbers are illustrative, but the lesson is fixed: the input stage must draw picoamperes or less.

That leads to a standard set of practices:

  • A buffer with very low input bias current, such as an op-amp with a CMOS input stage, as close to the electrode as possible
  • Guarding: a copper ring on the board around the input pin, driven at the same voltage as the input, so that leakage across the board surface has no voltage to drive it
  • Short, shielded cable, because cable movement and leakage at such impedances generate errors and noise
  • A mid-supply reference on a single-supply system, so the negative voltages from alkaline solutions stay in range
  • Calibration with buffer solutions, because real electrodes have an offset at pH 7 and a slope below the ideal that changes as they age

The conversion from voltage to pH, with a two-point calibration and temperature compensation of the slope, fits in a few lines:

from math import log

R, F = 8.314462618, 96485.33212      # gas constant, Faraday constant

def nernst_slope(temp_c):
    """Ideal electrode slope in volts per pH unit."""
    return log(10) * R * (temp_c + 273.15) / F

def calibrate(v7, v4, temp_c):
    """Two-point calibration with pH 7 and pH 4 buffers at one temperature."""
    efficiency = (v4 - v7) / (3 * nernst_slope(temp_c))
    return v7, efficiency

def ph_from_voltage(v, cal, temp_c):
    offset, efficiency = cal
    return 7 - (v - offset) / (efficiency * nernst_slope(temp_c))

# Illustrative readings: electrode gives +12 mV in pH 7, +180 mV in pH 4.
cal = calibrate(0.012, 0.180, temp_c=25)
print(f"slope at 25 C: {nernst_slope(25)*1e3:.2f} mV/pH, "
      f"efficiency {cal[1]:.3f}")
for temp in (25, 35):
    print(f"-60 mV at {temp} C -> pH {ph_from_voltage(-0.060, cal, temp):.2f}")

This electrode reaches about 94.7 % of the ideal slope. A reading of −60 mV means pH 8.29 at 25 °C but pH 8.24 at 35 °C. The code only corrects the electrode's slope; the solution's own pH also varies with temperature, which is a chemistry question, not an electronics one.

Conductivity: measure resistance, but never with DC

Conductivity tells you how much dissolved ionic material the water holds. The sensor is a cell: two electrodes of fixed geometry. The measured conductance G relates to the water's conductivity κ through the cell constant K, which depends on electrode area and spacing: κ = G × K. A cell with a small constant suits clean water; a larger constant suits salty water, keeping the measured resistance in a convenient range.

The obvious method, pass a DC current and measure the voltage, fails. Current through an electrolyte drives electrochemical reactions at the electrodes. Ions pile up at the surfaces and form a polarisation layer that adds a growing, unstable voltage. Sustained DC also decomposes the water and corrodes the electrodes.

The solution is AC excitation with no DC component: drive the cell with a symmetric square or sine wave, so charge moved in one half-cycle is moved back in the next. The excitation frequency is chosen high enough that polarisation does not build up within a half-cycle, yet low enough that the cable and electrode capacitances do not dominate the measurement. Practical details:

  • Block DC with a series capacitor so that op-amp offset cannot produce a net DC current through the cell
  • Measure synchronously: sample the cell voltage and current at the same points of each half-cycle, or demodulate with the excitation signal, to reject noise and capacitive currents
  • Switch ranges by changing a sense resistor, because conductivity spans several decades between clean and salty water
  • Compensate temperature: conductivity of natural water rises noticeably with temperature, by a few per cent per degree Celsius; instruments report a value corrected to a reference temperature, usually 25 °C

Turbidity: a photocurrent in daylight

Turbidity measures how much suspended particles scatter light. A common arrangement shines an LED into the sample and places a photodiode at 90° to the beam, so it sees only light scattered by particles, not the direct beam. Clear water scatters little; cloudy water scatters more.

The photodiode produces a current proportional to the light it receives, often nanoamperes or less. A transimpedance amplifier converts it to voltage: the photodiode feeds an op-amp's inverting input, and a feedback resistor Rf sets the gain, Vout = Iphoto × Rf. A 10 nA photocurrent with Rf = 10 MΩ gives 100 mV. A small capacitor across Rf keeps the amplifier stable with the photodiode's capacitance at its input.

Ambient light is the main enemy: daylight or room lighting reaching the photodiode can be far larger than the scattered signal. Mechanical shielding helps, but the electronic fix is modulation. Pulse the LED on and off, measure with the LED on and off, and subtract. Ambient light is present in both measurements and cancels; only light from the LED survives. Repeating this many times and averaging rejects noise further, a simple form of lock-in detection.

Fouling of the optical windows, LED ageing and temperature drift all shift the reading, so turbidity instruments are calibrated against reference suspensions and often monitor the LED's output with a second photodiode.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the pH script, then compute the error from ignoring temperature compensation at 10 °C and 40 °C.
  2. In ngspice, model a pH electrode as a voltage source in series with 100 MΩ, connect it to an op-amp model with 1 nA and then 1 pA of input bias current, and measure the output error.
  3. Simulate a transimpedance amplifier with a photodiode modelled as a current source in parallel with 50 pF, and find the feedback capacitor that removes ringing in the step response.
