Packaging basics: wire bond, flip chip and where chiplets fit

Analog & mixed-signal Beginner 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A die is useless until a package connects it to a board, protects it and removes its heat. Learn how wire bonding and flip chip work, what package parasitics do, and why systems increasingly split into chiplets.

In this article

  3. Flip chip

A finished die is a few millimetres of fragile silicon with pads smaller than a hair's width. To become a component you can solder to a board, it needs a package: something that carries signals and power between the die and the board, protects the silicon from moisture and handling, and carries heat away. Designers often treat the package as someone else's problem until a supply rail bounces or the chip runs hot. Knowing the basic options, and why multi-die packages have become common, helps you make better decisions from the floorplan onwards.

What a package has to do

Four jobs, often in tension:

  • Electrical connection for every signal, power and ground pad, with low enough resistance and inductance
  • Mechanical protection from moisture, contamination, light and stress
  • Heat removal from the die to the board or a heat sink
  • Testability and assembly: the package must be testable after assembly and solderable by normal board processes

The first decision is how the die's pads connect to the package.

Wire bonding

In wire bonding, the die is glued face up onto a lead frame or substrate. A machine then attaches a fine wire, of gold, copper or aluminium, from each pad on the die to a corresponding landing on the package, one bond at a time. The die is then covered in moulding compound.

Wire bonding is mature, flexible and inexpensive. Its limits come from geometry. Pads must sit around the edge of the die, so the number of connections is limited by the perimeter, and a design with many I/Os can end up "pad-limited", with a die larger than its logic needs just to fit the pads. Each wire is also an inductor; a common rule of thumb is about 1 nH per millimetre of length. A fast current change through that inductance creates a voltage, V = L × di/dt, which appears as noise on supply and ground. Designers counter it with many parallel power and ground pads and with on-die decoupling capacitance.

Common wire-bonded packages include QFN (a flat package with pads underneath and an exposed thermal pad) and QFP (leads around the edges). They suit microcontrollers, sensors, analog parts and many small chips.

Flip chip

In flip chip assembly, small solder or copper bumps are formed on pads across the whole face of the die. The die is flipped face down and the bumps are joined directly to a matching pattern on a package substrate, which then routes the connections to solder balls on its underside, as in a ball grid array (BGA).

Flip chip changes the trade-offs. Connections can come from anywhere on the die, not just the edge, so I/O count scales with area rather than perimeter, and power can be fed straight down into the middle of the die where it is consumed. Bumps are far shorter than wires, so their inductance is much lower. The back of the die faces up, where a heat spreader or heat sink can touch it directly. The costs are a more complex substrate, more expensive assembly, and layout constraints: the bump pattern and the on-die redistribution layer must be planned with the floorplan.

A third option, the wafer-level chip-scale package, puts solder balls directly on the die with a thin redistribution layer and no separate substrate. The package is barely larger than the die, which suits small chips in phones and wearables.

Thermal basics

Every package has a thermal resistance from the die junction to the surrounding air, written θJA in °C per watt. The junction temperature is then:

TJ = TA + P × θJA

For example, with illustrative values, a 1.5 W chip in a package and board with θJA = 40 °C/W at 35 °C ambient runs at 35 + 1.5 × 40 = 95 °C. θJA depends heavily on the board: copper area, thermal vias under an exposed pad and airflow can change it a lot, which is why datasheets also give junction-to-case values.

Why chiplets

For decades, the trend was to integrate everything onto one die. Increasingly, large systems go the other way: they are split into several smaller dies, chiplets, assembled side by side or stacked in one package.

The first reason is yield. Defects land randomly on wafers, so the chance that a die is defect-free falls exponentially with its area. A simple model gives yield Y = e−A·D, for die area A and defect density D. With an illustrative D of 0.2 defects per cm2:

  • One 4 cm2 die: Y = e−0.8 ≈ 0.45, so each good system consumes about 4 / 0.45 ≈ 8.9 cm2 of silicon.
  • Four 1 cm2 chiplets: each has Y = e−0.2 ≈ 0.82. Because chiplets are tested before assembly and bad ones discarded individually, each good system consumes about 4 / 0.82 ≈ 4.9 cm2.

Other reasons add up. Very large dies run into the maximum area one lithography exposure can pattern. Different functions suit different processes: dense logic benefits from the newest node, while analog, I/O and high-voltage circuits often work as well or better in a mature, cheaper node. And a proven chiplet can be reused across products.

Chiplets bring their own costs: the die-to-die interface consumes area and power on each chiplet, assembly is harder, the package substrate or silicon interposer that connects them adds cost, and heat is harder to remove from stacked dies. Each chiplet must be thoroughly tested before assembly, since one bad die scraps the whole package; this is the known good die requirement. To let chiplets from different designs work together, the industry has developed open die-to-die interface specifications such as UCIe and Bunch of Wires.

Try it yourself

  1. Compute yield and silicon per good system for one, two, four and eight chiplets making up the same total area, at two defect densities, and plot where splitting stops paying off once you add a fixed interface area per chiplet.
  2. In ngspice, model a ground bond wire as 2 nH, switch 8 outputs of 10 mA each in 1 ns through it, and measure the ground bounce; then put four bond wires in parallel.
  3. Look at the pad ring of an open-source chip design in KLayout and identify the power, ground and signal pads and how many of each were used.
