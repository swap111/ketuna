Why hold violations are worse than setup violations, and how tools fix them

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A setup failure on silicon can be slowed away; a hold failure cannot, because the clock period is not in the hold equation. See why, and what buffer insertion to fix hold costs in area and power.

If a chip comes back from the fab with a setup violation, you can usually still use it: turn the clock down until the slow path makes it, and you have a working, slower part. If it comes back with a hold violation on a path that matters, lowering the clock does nothing. The failing flip-flop captures the wrong value at every frequency. That single fact is why physical designers treat hold as a must-fix, and why sign-off tools spend so much effort on it.

The two checks look at different edges

Take a launch flop and a capture flop on the same clock. For setup, the data launched on edge n must arrive before edge n + 1, minus the setup time. For hold, the data launched on edge n must not arrive so early that it disturbs the value the capture flop is latching on that same edge n.

Write the hold check with clock latencies included. Let Llaunch and Lcapture be the clock arrival times at the two flops:

Llaunch + tcq + tlogic,min ≥ Lcapture + th

There is no clock period anywhere in that line. Both sides refer to the same edge, so stretching the time between edges changes nothing. Setup, by contrast, has the period T on the required side, and making T larger always helps.

How hold failures happen in practice

Hold fails when the shortest path is shorter than the hold requirement plus any skew working against it. The usual suspects:

  • Back-to-back flops with no logic between them, such as shift registers and synchronizer chains. The only delay is clock-to-Q and a short wire.
  • Clock skew where the capture clock arrives later than the launch clock. Every picosecond of that skew is added to the hold requirement.
  • Fast corners. At the fast process corner, high voltage and (for most older processes) low temperature, cells are quickest, so the minimum path is shortest. Hold is signed off there.
  • Scan paths. In test mode, flip-flops are chained directly, often across distant parts of the clock tree, giving short data paths with large skew.

Here is a worked example with illustrative numbers. A scan chain connects flop A to flop B with only a wire.

Quantity Value
Launch clock latency, Llaunch 0.40 ns
Capture clock latency, Lcapture 0.58 ns
Clock-to-Q at the fast corner 0.12 ns
Wire delay 0.02 ns
Hold time of flop B 0.03 ns

Arrival = 0.40 + 0.12 + 0.02 = 0.54 ns. Required = 0.58 + 0.03 = 0.61 ns. Hold slack = 0.54 − 0.61 = −0.07 ns. The path fails by 70 ps, and it would fail at 1 MHz just as it does at 100 MHz.

How tools fix hold

The fix is to make the short path longer, without making any long path that shares it fail setup.

  • Delay cell or buffer insertion. The tool inserts one or more buffers or dedicated delay cells on the fast path, close to the capture flop's D pin so that only the short path is slowed. In the example, a delay cell of 0.10 ns gives a slack of +0.03 ns.
  • Downsizing the driver to slow the edge slightly, when there is setup slack to spare.
  • Re-routing with a longer wire, rarely used directly but sometimes the side effect of buffering.
  • Changing skew. Moving the launch clock later or the capture clock earlier removes the cause instead of the symptom, but it changes setup on other paths through the same flops.

The trade-off is that a fix for hold can break setup. If the short path to flop B shares cells with a long path to the same flop, adding delay where both paths overlap slows the long path too. Good repair tools check setup slack at the slow corner while inserting delay for hold at the fast corner, which is why hold repair is usually done after clock tree synthesis, once real skew is known, and again after routing.

What the fix costs

Hold buffers are not free:

  • Area and placement room. Hundreds of small delay cells in a dense region can push the design towards congestion.
  • Power. Each buffer switches whenever its data changes and adds capacitance.
  • Routing. More cells mean more pins to connect.

A design that needs thousands of hold buffers is often telling you something about the clock tree, such as large skew between regions, rather than about the logic. Look at skew first.

Common mistakes

  • Checking hold only at the typical corner. Hold must be signed off at the fast corner where paths are shortest.
  • Fixing hold before CTS. Before the clock tree exists, skew is an estimate, so repairs are guesses.
  • Ignoring test-mode hold. Scan paths are real paths when the tester runs them.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a four-stage shift register in Verilog and synthesise it with Yosys against sky130.
  2. In OpenSTA, create a clock, then add skew with set_clock_latency on the capture flops only. Run report_checks -path_delay min and watch the hold slack go negative.
  3. Change the clock period by a factor of ten and confirm the hold slack does not change.
  4. Run the full flow in LibreLane and compare the hold slack before and after the post-CTS repair step in its reports.
