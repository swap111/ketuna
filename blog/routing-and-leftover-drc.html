Routing and the DRC violations it leaves behind

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Global routing plans the paths, detailed routing draws the metal, and sometimes a few spacing, short or via violations remain. Learn what each kind means and how to clear it without breaking timing.

A detailed router can finish a run, report success, and still leave a handful of design rule violations behind. On a small block that might be three markers in KLayout; on a large one it can be hundreds. They must all go before tape-out, and the way you clear them depends on understanding what the router was trying to do and why it could not. This article explains the two routing stages and the common leftovers, with a practical order for fixing them.

Global routing: a plan on a coarse grid

Global routing divides the core into a grid of global routing cells (gcells), each a few rows tall. For every net, it chooses a sequence of gcells and a layer for each segment, respecting the capacity of each gcell edge: how many tracks of each layer cross it. It does not draw shapes. Its output is a route guide for every net, and a congestion report showing where demand exceeded capacity.

Global routing is fast, so it runs repeatedly: during placement for congestion estimates, after clock tree synthesis, and before detailed routing. Its overflow numbers are the best early warning you get. A global route with significant overflow will turn into detailed routing violations almost every time.

Detailed routing: real shapes under real rules

Detailed routing (TritonRoute, run as detailed_route in OpenROAD) follows the guides and draws actual wires on the routing tracks of each layer, with vias between layers and connections to cell pins. It must obey the full set of foundry rules for each layer: minimum width, minimum spacing (which often depends on width and on how long two wires run side by side), minimum area, end-of-line spacing, via enclosure, and more.

The router works iteratively. It routes everything, finds violations, rips up the nets involved, reroutes them with higher costs on the problem areas, and repeats. Most violations vanish in the first few iterations. The ones left at the end are the hard cases: regions where there is not enough room, or pin shapes that are awkward to reach.

The usual leftovers

Violation What it means Typical cause
Metal spacing Two shapes on one layer are closer than allowed Crowded region, wide-wire spacing rules, pins packed together
Short Two different nets touch Router gave up in a congested area; overlap with a power strap
Minimum area A tiny metal island is smaller than the rule Short stub at a via or pin
Via enclosure or cut spacing A via's metal overlap is too small, or two vias are too close Dense via stacks, pin access on lower layers
End-of-line spacing A wire end is too close to a neighbour Many short segments in a tight space
Off-grid or pin access A pin cannot be reached cleanly Cell pin shapes that do not line up with tracks

A short is the most serious, because it is also an electrical error: LVS will fail and the circuit would not work. Spacing and area errors are manufacturability risks, which the foundry will not accept.

Clearing violations, in order

The best fix is the one that removes the cause, applied at the earliest step that can.

  1. Look at the markers. Load the routed layout in KLayout with the DRC report, or open the routed database in the OpenROAD GUI and use its DRC viewer. Are the violations scattered, or clustered in one area? Clusters point to congestion; scattered single errors near particular cells point to pin access problems.
  2. Rerun detailed routing with more iterations if there are only a few scattered violations. The router may simply need more passes.
  3. Relieve local congestion if the violations cluster: lower the placement density or add placement padding around the dense cells, and rerun from placement. Do not keep pushing the router.
  4. Check pin access if violations sit on the same cell type every time. Some cells are hard to access on the lowest metal; preventing their use in synthesis (a dont_use list) is a common, legitimate fix.
  5. Check the power grid. Shorts to power straps often mean the grid leaves too few tracks in a region. Adjust strap pitch or layers at the floorplan.
  6. Hand-edit only as a last resort, and rerun DRC, LVS and timing after any manual change.

Watch timing while you fix

Every rerouted net has new parasitics. A spacing fix that moves a wire next to a fast-switching neighbour adds coupling capacitance; a detour adds resistance and delay. After clearing DRC, always rerun parasitic extraction and sign-off timing. A clean DRC run with a newly failing hold path is not progress.

The router's own check is fast and good, but it is not the foundry's sign-off deck. Run the full DRC in Magic or KLayout on the final GDS as well. Differences between the router's view and the sign-off deck are common at the edges of the rule set, for example density and antenna rules, which the router may handle separately.

Common mistakes

  • Raising router effort on a congested placement. The violations come back in a slightly different place.
  • Trusting the router's "0 violations" as sign-off. Run the foundry deck on the GDS.
  • Fixing DRC and forgetting timing. Rerouting changes parasitics.

Try it yourself

  1. Harden a small design with LibreLane on sky130 at a high utilisation (for example FP_CORE_UTIL of 70) so that routing is tight.
  2. Find the detailed routing DRC report in the run directory and load it on the layout in KLayout using the marker browser.
  3. Classify each violation by the table above, then rerun at a lower PL_TARGET_DENSITY_PCT and compare the count.
  4. Run the full Magic DRC on the final GDS and compare it with the router's report.
