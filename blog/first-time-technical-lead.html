Your first time as a technical lead: planning, unblocking and reviewing

Careers & skills Advanced 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A new technical lead's job shifts from doing the work to making the team's work succeed. How to break work down, keep people unblocked, review well, and still keep your own engineering skills sharp.

In this article
  1. Breaking work down so it can be tracked
  2. Keeping the team unblocked
  3. Reviewing without becoming the bottleneck
  4. Still doing some engineering
  5. Common mistakes
  6. Try it yourself

The first time you lead a small team, the work that made you good, solving hard problems yourself, becomes only part of the job. Your output is now the team's output: whether the block closes timing, whether the firmware is ready for the board, whether the verification sign-off is honest. Most new leads struggle not with technical questions but with the change of emphasis. Four habits carry most of the load: breaking work down, keeping people unblocked, reviewing well, and protecting some time to engineer.

Breaking work down so it can be tracked

A task like "verify the DMA engine" cannot be estimated, assigned or tracked. A useful breakdown produces pieces that are small enough to finish in a few days, have one owner, and have a clear definition of done.

Start from the deliverables and their gates, then work backwards. For hardware, some dates are fixed and irreversible: a shuttle deadline, a board order, a demo. Inside those milestones, iterate in short cycles on the work that can change, such as RTL, testbenches and firmware.

A breakdown for one block might look like this:

Task Owner Done means
Interface spec reviewed lead signed off by both neighbouring block owners
RTL feature-complete engineer A lint clean, smoke test passes in CI
Reference model engineer B agrees with spec examples, reviewed
Random test with scoreboard engineer B runs nightly, fails on three planted bugs
Timing closed at target clock engineer A no setup or hold violations at all corners

The "done means" column is the most important. "RTL written" is not done; "lint clean and passing in CI" is. Without an agreed definition, you discover at integration that three tasks were only mostly finished.

Then find the critical path: the chain of dependent tasks with no slack. In the table, the random test depends on both the RTL and the reference model. If the model slips, the whole sign-off slips. Knowing this tells you where to look first every week.

Keeping the team unblocked

A blocked engineer is the most expensive thing on a project, because their time passes with nothing to show. Your most valuable daily question is "what is stopping you?", and your job is to answer it faster than the engineer could alone.

Typical blockers and what a lead does about them:

  • A missing decision. An interface detail is ambiguous. Make the call, or get the right person to make it today, and write it into the spec.
  • A dependency on another team. A library, a pin map, a test board. Chase it yourself; that is a better use of your time than theirs.
  • A tool or licence problem. Escalate early with specifics.
  • Being stuck on a hard bug. Pair for half an hour, or suggest a time-box: if not found by tomorrow, bring in a second person.

Keep stand-ups short and about blockers. When a problem needs discussion, note it and solve it afterwards with the two or three people involved. A fifteen-minute meeting that becomes an hour of debugging costs the whole team.

Report bad news early. If the critical path slips, tell your manager the same week with the cause, the new estimate and the options: cut scope, add time, or accept a risk. Adding people to a late task rarely helps in the short term, because they need time to learn the design and take time from those who already know it.

Reviewing without becoming the bottleneck

New leads often try to review everything in detail and soon become the reason work waits. Review where the risk is highest: interfaces between blocks, clock domain crossings, reset behaviour, anything going to silicon or to a board order. For lower-risk changes, let team members review each other and spot-check.

When you review, be specific and ask questions rather than issue verdicts. "If err arrives in WAIT_ACK instead of ack, there is no transition. Should it return to IDLE?" teaches more than "the FSM is wrong", and leaves room for the engineer to know something you missed. Record findings with an owner and a severity so they do not evaporate after the meeting.

Reviews are also where you grow people. Ask the engineer to walk you through their reasoning before you comment. Over time, aim for them to find the issues you would have found.

Still doing some engineering

If you stop engineering entirely, your technical judgement fades and your reviews get shallower. If you take a large critical-path task, it slips every time the team needs you. The middle path is to take work that is valuable but not on the critical path: a regression script, a reference model for a corner case, a timing investigation, a tricky integration test.

Block out time for it in your calendar, and accept that it will be smaller than before. The point is to stay close enough to the tools and the design that your estimates and reviews remain grounded.

Common mistakes

  • Doing the hardest task yourself. It feels efficient, but the team waits on you and nobody else learns that part of the design.
  • Vague task definitions. Without "done means", progress reports are optimistic until the end.
  • Hiding a slip. Late bad news removes options; early bad news keeps them.
  • Rewriting other people's work. Fix it through review and explanation, or the team learns to wait for you.

Try it yourself

  1. Take a past or current project and break it into tasks of two to four days, each with an owner and a "done means" line. Mark the critical path.
  2. Set up a small CI job, or at least a local make check target, that runs lint (verilator --lint-only -Wall) and a smoke test, so "done" can be checked automatically.
  3. Review one change from a teammate using only questions, and record findings with severity and owner.
  4. At the end of a week, list what blocked people and how long each blocker lasted. Look for the ones you could have cleared sooner.
