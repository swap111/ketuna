A risk register that people actually use

Systems & product engineering Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How to write risks as cause, event and effect, rate them on probability and impact, give each an owner and a trigger, and review the register weekly so it changes decisions instead of gathering dust.

In this article

  2. Write each risk as cause, event and effect

Most teams have a risk register. Many of them were filled in at the project kick-off, reviewed once, and never opened again, until something on the list actually happened and somebody remembered it was there. A register like that costs effort and buys nothing. A useful one is short, specific, owned and looked at every week, and it changes what the team does on Monday. This article shows how to build that kind.

Risks are not issues

A risk is something that might happen and would affect the project if it did. An issue is something that has already happened. The difference matters because they need different handling: a risk can still be prevented or prepared for; an issue can only be dealt with. The whole value of a risk register is acting while things are still risks. When a risk turns into an issue, move it to the issue list, and ask in the review whether it could have been caught earlier.

Write each risk as cause, event and effect

Vague entries such as "schedule risk" or "supplier problems" cannot be acted on, because nobody can tell what would make them better. A simple structure forces precision:

Because of cause, event may happen, leading to effect.

Compare:

Vague Cause, event, effect
"FPGA board risk" Because the FPGA board is on back-order, it may arrive after week 9, leading to integration testing having no hardware before the demo
"Sensor accuracy" Because the humidity sensor has not been tested above 40 °C, it may drift out of tolerance in summer, leading to failed field trials
"Team availability" Because two of four members have exams in week 6, firmware work may stop for a week, leading to a slip of the bring-up milestone

Each version points at something you can do: chase the supplier or find an alternative board, test the sensor in an oven this week, move firmware tasks earlier.

Rate probability and impact

Rate each risk on two short scales, with written anchors so everyone means the same thing. Five-point scales are common. An example set of anchors, made up for illustration:

Score Probability Impact
1 Very unlikely Negligible; absorbed without change
2 Unlikely Minor slip of days, no milestone moves
3 Possible A milestone moves, or a feature is reduced
4 Likely Demo or release date at risk
5 Almost certain Project goal cannot be met

The score is probability × impact, from 1 to 25. Plotting risks on a grid makes the register easy to scan in a meeting.

Do not over-engineer the numbers. Their purpose is to rank risks and start a conversation, not to compute anything precise. A rough agreement in a few seconds per risk is enough.

Owner, trigger and response

The three fields that make a register useful are the ones most often left blank.

  • Owner: one named person who watches the risk and reports on it. Not "the team", and not the project lead for everything.
  • Trigger: an observable sign that the risk is turning into an issue, with a date where possible: "board not shipped by Wednesday of week 8".
  • Response: what you are doing about it. The usual options are to avoid it (change the plan so it cannot happen), reduce it (lower probability or impact now), transfer it (someone else carries it, for example a supplier warranty), or accept it with a contingency plan for when the trigger fires.

For the FPGA board example: reduce by running every test in simulation with recorded data so integration does not depend on the board; contingency, demonstrate in simulation and on an older board if the trigger fires.

The weekly review

The review is what keeps the register alive. Keep it to ten or fifteen minutes in an existing team meeting:

  1. Look at the top five by score. Each owner says in one sentence what changed.
  2. Check every trigger date that has passed. Fire contingencies where needed.
  3. Add new risks raised during the week. Anyone may add one; the owner is agreed in the meeting.
  4. Close risks that can no longer happen, with a note of why.

A short script can help by sorting the register and flagging risks nobody has reviewed recently:

from datetime import date

register = [
    ("R1", "FPGA board late",       3, 4, "hw-lead", date(2027, 3, 1)),
    ("R2", "Sensor drift when hot", 2, 3, "fw-lead", date(2027, 2, 10)),
    ("R3", "Exams in week 6",       4, 2, "pm",      date(2027, 3, 2)),
]
today = date(2027, 3, 3)
for rid, text, p, i, owner, reviewed in sorted(
        register, key=lambda r: r[2] * r[3], reverse=True):
    stale = "  STALE" if (today - reviewed).days > 7 else ""
    print(f"{rid} score {p * i:2d} {owner:8s} {text}{stale}")

It lists R1 (score 12), then R3 (8), then R2 (6), and marks R2 as stale because its last review was more than a week before the meeting date. The owners are roles and the dates are invented for the example.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the register once and filing it. A register that is not reviewed weekly is decoration.
  • Risks without owners or triggers. Nobody watches them, and they surprise everyone.
  • Fifty risks. Long registers hide the important ones. Keep the active list short and merge duplicates.
  • Raising a risk only when it happens. By then it is an issue with fewer options.

Try it yourself

  1. Write five risks for your current project in cause, event, effect form, with probability, impact, owner and trigger.
  2. Keep them in a CSV under version control and adapt the script above to read it, sort by score and flag stale entries.
  3. Run a ten-minute review with your team each week for a month, and at the end count how many risks you closed, how many became issues, and whether any trigger fired early enough to help.
