Design for manufacture: small PCB decisions that change yield

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Footprints, part spacing, thermal balance, panelisation, fiducials and test points are small layout choices that decide whether an assembly line builds your board cleanly or produces rework.

A board that works on your bench, assembled by hand with patience and a fine soldering tip, can still be a poor board to manufacture. On an assembly line, parts are placed by machine, soldered in a reflow oven in one pass, inspected by camera and tested on a fixture, thousands of times. Small layout choices that cost nothing at design time decide whether that process runs smoothly or produces a steady stream of boards for rework. Design for manufacture (DFM) is the habit of making those choices deliberately.

Footprints decide solder joints

Most assembly defects start at the footprint, the copper land pattern a part is soldered to.

  • Use standard land patterns. Libraries built to the industry land-pattern conventions, such as the ones shipped with KiCad, are a far safer starting point than a footprint drawn from a datasheet sketch at midnight. If you must draw one, have someone else check it against the part's drawing.
  • Watch pad size balance on small two-terminal parts. When the two pads of a small resistor or capacitor heat at different rates in reflow, the solder on one side melts first and its surface tension can pull the part upright. This is called tombstoning. Common causes are one pad connected to a large copper pour while the other is on a thin trace, or unequal pad sizes.
  • Use thermal relief on plane connections for pads that need to be soldered: a few narrow spokes instead of a solid connection, so the pad heats up with its neighbours.
  • Keep vias out of pads unless they are filled and capped. An open via in a pad wicks solder away from the joint during reflow, leaving a starved or voided joint. Via-in-pad is a valid technique, but it is a fabrication option you specify and pay for, not a default.
  • Define solder mask between fine-pitch pads. A thin web of mask between pins helps prevent bridges. Check your fabricator's minimum mask web.

Spacing and orientation

Parts too close together create two problems: the placement nozzle may disturb a neighbour, and inspection cameras and rework tools cannot reach the joints. Leave clearance around tall parts so short parts beside them are still visible to automated optical inspection (AOI). Keep parts away from the board edge, especially near V-score lines, where the board flexes during separation and can crack ceramic capacitors.

Orient similar parts the same way where you can. A row of diodes all facing one direction is easier to inspect and less likely to be placed backwards. Put clear polarity marks on the silkscreen that are still visible after the part is placed, not hidden under its body.

Put all surface-mount parts on one side if the design allows. A second side means a second reflow pass, with parts on the first side hanging upside down through the oven.

Panels, rails and fiducials

Small boards are assembled several at a time in a panel, with rails along the edges for conveyor transport, tooling holes to locate the panel, and fiducials, small bare copper dots the machine's camera uses to find the board's exact position and rotation.

Use at least two, preferably three, global fiducials on the panel rails, placed asymmetrically so the machine cannot accept a panel loaded the wrong way round. Add local fiducials near fine-pitch parts on each board. Keep a clear area free of mask and copper around each fiducial so the camera sees a crisp target. If you are unsure, ask your assembler for their preferred panel format before you lay out the board: many will panelise for you, but only if the board outline and keep-outs allow it.

Choose the separation method with the layout in mind. V-scores need straight lines across the whole panel. Tab routing with small perforated breakaway tabs allows irregular outlines but leaves rough edges and stresses parts near the tabs.

Test points are part of DFM

A board that is easy to assemble but hard to test still has a poor yield, because faults escape or take too long to diagnose. Bring every power rail, reset, the programming interface and key signals to test pads that a fixture's spring probes can reach, ideally all on one side. Space them generously, keep them away from tall parts, and do not rely on probing component leads. A programming and debug connector, or at least pads for one, saves hours on every board that needs reflashing.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing footprints from memory or a datasheet sketch without a second check. A wrong pin 1 or pitch scraps a whole batch.
  • A small part bridging a ground pour on one pad and a thin trace on the other. A classic tombstoning setup.
  • No fiducials, or fiducials covered by solder mask. The machine cannot align accurately.
  • Ceramic capacitors right beside a V-score. They crack when the panel is snapped apart, often invisibly.
  • Ignoring the fabricator's design rules. Minimum track, gap, drill and annular ring vary; load them into your design rule check.

Try it yourself

  1. Open a board in KiCad, load your fabricator's published minimum track, spacing and drill sizes into the board setup, and run the design rule checker.
  2. Look for every small two-terminal part with one pad on a copper pour and add thermal relief or rebalance the copper.
  3. Build a two-by-two panel of your board with rails, tooling holes and three global fiducials, and ask a classmate to find what a machine or an inspector would struggle with.
