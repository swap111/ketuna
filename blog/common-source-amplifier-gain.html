The common-source amplifier: gain, headroom and the gm-ro trade-off

Analog & mixed-signal Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

The common-source stage is the workhorse gain stage of CMOS analog design. Work out its small-signal gain, its output swing limits, and why longer transistors buy gain at the cost of speed.

In this article
  1. Gain as transconductance times resistance
  2. The surprising result: gain does not depend on current
  3. Worked example
  4. Why longer transistors are slower
  5. Headroom: how far the output can swing
  6. Try it yourself

If you remember one amplifier from analog design, make it the common-source stage. It is a single transistor with the input on the gate, the source at a fixed potential and the output on the drain, and it provides the voltage gain in nearly every op-amp, comparator preamplifier and sensor interface. Three questions decide whether a given common-source stage works: how much gain it gives, how far its output can swing, and how fast it is. They are linked, and the link runs through two small-signal parameters, gm and ro.

Gain as transconductance times resistance

Bias an NMOS in saturation and wiggle its gate by a small vin. The drain current changes by gmvin, where the transconductance gm is the slope of ID against VGS at the bias point. In the square-law model:

gm = 2ID / VOV

That current change flows into whatever resistance sits at the drain node, Rout, and produces an output voltage. Because a rising gate voltage pulls more current down and the drain voltage falls, the gain is inverting:

Av = −gm Rout

Every gain question about this stage is a question about gm or Rout. With a resistor load RD, Rout = RD ∥ ro. A resistor large enough for high gain would drop too much DC voltage, so on chip the load is usually a PMOS current source (an active load). Then Rout = ro1 ∥ ro2, the parallel output resistances of the NMOS and the PMOS load.

The surprising result: gain does not depend on current

Substitute ro ≈ 1 / (λID) for both devices:

|Av| = (2ID / VOV) × 1 / ((λn + λp) ID) = 2 / (VOV (λn + λp))

The current cancels. Doubling the bias current doubles gm but halves ro, and the gain stays put. In the square-law picture, the gain of an actively loaded stage is set by only two things you choose: the overdrive voltage and the channel length (through λ). Smaller VOV means more gm per microampere; longer L means smaller λ and larger ro.

This is why analog designers talk about the intrinsic gain gmro of a device. It is the most gain one transistor can give you, and it is a property of how you bias and size it, not of how much power you spend. Short-channel devices deviate from the square law, so in practice designers read gm/ID and gmro from simulated lookup curves, but the conclusion holds in shape.

Worked example

An NMOS with a PMOS current-source load, numbers made up for illustration: ID = 50 µA, VOV = 0.2 V for both devices, λn = λp = 0.1 V−1, and a 1 pF load capacitance.

  • gm = 2 × 50 µA / 0.2 V = 500 µS
  • ro1 = ro2 = 1 / (0.1 × 50 µA) = 200 kΩ, so Rout = 100 kΩ
  • |Av| = 500 µS × 100 kΩ = 50, about 34 dB
  • Output pole: 1 / (2π × 100 kΩ × 1 pF) ≈ 1.59 MHz
  • Gain-bandwidth product: gm / (2πCL) ≈ 79.6 MHz

Now double L on both devices. If λ roughly halves to 0.05 V−1, Rout doubles to 200 kΩ and the gain doubles to 100 (40 dB). The output pole halves to about 0.8 MHz. The gain-bandwidth product, set by gm and CL, is unchanged on paper.

from math import pi, log10

def cs_stage(i_d, v_ov, lam_n, lam_p, c_load):
    gm = 2 * i_d / v_ov                 # square-law transconductance
    r_out = 1 / ((lam_n + lam_p) * i_d) # r_o1 || r_o2
    av = gm * r_out                     # magnitude of the gain
    f_p = 1 / (2 * pi * r_out * c_load) # output pole
    return av, f_p, av * f_p            # gain, bandwidth, GBW

for label, lam in (("L", 0.1), ("2L", 0.05)):
    av, f_p, gbw = cs_stage(50e-6, 0.2, lam, lam, 1e-12)
    print(f"{label:>2}: gain {av:5.1f} ({20*log10(av):4.1f} dB), "
          f"pole {f_p/1e6:5.2f} MHz, GBW {gbw/1e6:5.1f} MHz")

Why longer transistors are slower

The example says GBW does not change, so where is the speed penalty? It is in the transistor's own capacitance. To keep the same current at the same overdrive after doubling L, you must also double W, because current goes as W/L. Gate area WL is now four times larger, and so is the gate capacitance. That capacitance loads whatever stage drives this one, and the drain junction capacitance adds to CL at this stage's output.

The device-level measure is the transit frequency fT ≈ gm / (2πCgs). With gm fixed and Cgs four times larger, fT falls to roughly a quarter. A long-channel transistor is a high-gain, slow device; a short one is a low-gain, fast one. Picking L is choosing your point on that curve, and op-amps often use long devices in the gain stage and shorter ones where speed matters.

Headroom: how far the output can swing

Gain is only small-signal. For large signals the output must stay inside the range where both transistors remain saturated. The NMOS needs VDS ≥ VOV,n, so the output cannot go below about VOV,n. The PMOS needs VSD ≥ VOV,p, so the output cannot go above about VDD − VOV,p.

On a 1.8 V supply with 0.2 V overdrives, the usable swing is roughly 0.2 V to 1.6 V, a range of 1.4 V. Raising VOV to improve matching or speed eats into swing from both ends, and so does stacking cascode devices to boost gain. That three-way pull between gain, swing and speed is what "the gm-ro trade-off" means in practice.

Common mistakes:

  • Reading AC gain from a simulation without first checking that both devices are saturated in the op results
  • Expecting gain to rise with bias current in an actively loaded stage
  • Forgetting that the high-impedance output node makes the DC output voltage extremely sensitive to small current mismatches; real circuits fix it with feedback
  • Choosing minimum length everywhere and then wondering where the gain went

Try it yourself

  1. In ngspice with sky130 models, build an NMOS common-source stage with a PMOS current-source load and a 1 pF capacitor. Tune the input DC voltage until the output sits near mid-supply.
  2. Run op and print gm and gds for each device with ngspice's show command (sky130 transistors sit inside subcircuits, so use the full internal device name that show lists), and compute the expected gain by hand.
  3. Run ac dec 20 1 1G, read the low-frequency gain and the −3 dB frequency, and compare with your estimate. Then double L and W on both devices and repeat.
