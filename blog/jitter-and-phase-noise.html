Jitter and phase noise: what they are and why ADCs and radios care

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Jitter is clock timing error seen in the time domain; phase noise is the same error seen in frequency. Learn the jitter definitions, convert a phase-noise plot to RMS jitter, and see how jitter caps ADC resolution.

In this article
  1. Jitter in the time domain
  2. Phase noise in the frequency domain
  3. Converting phase noise to jitter
  4. Why ADCs care

  6. Try it yourself

No clock edge arrives exactly when it should. The error is tiny, picoseconds or femtoseconds, but it sets a hard ceiling on how well a high-speed ADC can digitise, how cleanly a radio can receive, and how fast a serial link can run. Digital designers meet it as jitter, a time-domain number in a timing budget. RF designers meet it as phase noise, a curve in dBc/Hz. They are the same physical thing, and moving between the two views is a skill worth having.

Jitter in the time domain

Take an ideal clock with period T and compare each real edge with where the ideal edge would be. That timing error, edge by edge, is the raw material for several jitter definitions:

  • Absolute jitter (time interval error): the deviation of each edge from its ideal position. Its RMS value is what limits sampling systems.
  • Period jitter: the deviation of each period from the nominal period. This is what a synchronous digital path sees, since setup timing depends on the time from one edge to the next.
  • Cycle-to-cycle jitter: the change between consecutive periods. It matters for circuits that track the clock, such as some clock-data recovery loops.

These are not interchangeable. A clock whose frequency wanders slowly can have large absolute jitter but small period jitter, because neighbouring periods are almost identical. A datasheet number is meaningless until you know which definition, and over what measurement bandwidth, it refers to.

Jitter is also split by cause. Random jitter comes from thermal and flicker noise in the oscillator and is usually close to Gaussian, so it is described by an RMS value and has no hard peak. Deterministic jitter comes from identifiable sources such as supply ripple, crosstalk or reference spurs, and is bounded. For a timing budget, random jitter is converted to a peak value by multiplying its RMS by a factor chosen for the error rate you can accept.

Phase noise in the frequency domain

Look at the same clock on a spectrum analyser. An ideal oscillator is a single line at f0. A real one has skirts: power spread to nearby frequencies because its phase is being randomly modulated. Phase noise L(f) describes those skirts. It is the noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at an offset f from the carrier, relative to the carrier power, in dBc/Hz.

A typical oscillator plot falls with offset. Close in, flicker noise in the devices makes it fall steeply; further out, thermal noise gives a gentler slope; far out, it flattens into a floor set by buffers and dividers. A PLL reshapes this: inside the loop bandwidth, the output follows the (multiplied) reference noise and the in-band plateau is fairly flat; outside the bandwidth, the free-running VCO noise appears and rolls off.

Converting phase noise to jitter

The bridge between the two views is integration. The RMS phase error in radians is the square root of the area under the phase-noise curve, counting both sidebands:

σφ = √(2 ∫ 10L(f)/10 df), then σt = σφ / (2π f0)

The integration limits matter as much as the curve. The lower limit is usually set by what the system can track or ignore: slow wander that a receiver's own loops follow does not hurt it. The upper limit is often a filter bandwidth or half the clock frequency.

A quick check by hand: a 100 MHz clock with flat phase noise of −120 dBc/Hz from 10 kHz to 10 MHz. The area is 10−12 × (107 − 104) ≈ 10−5, so σφ = √(2 × 10−5) ≈ 4.47 mrad, and σt = 4.47 × 10−3 / (2π × 108) ≈ 7.1 ps. The script below does the same calculation for any piecewise profile, interpolating straight lines on a log-frequency axis:

import numpy as np

def rms_jitter(f0, offsets, l_dbc, f_lo, f_hi):
    """Integrate single-sideband phase noise L(f) into RMS jitter (s)."""
    f = np.logspace(np.log10(f_lo), np.log10(f_hi), 4000)
    l = np.interp(np.log10(f), np.log10(offsets), l_dbc)  # straight on log axis
    s = 10 ** (l / 10)                                    # dBc/Hz to 1/Hz
    area = np.sum((s[1:] + s[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(f))      # trapezoid rule
    phase_rms = np.sqrt(2 * area)                         # both sidebands, rad
    return phase_rms / (2 * np.pi * f0)

f0 = 100e6
flat = rms_jitter(f0, [1e4, 1e7], [-120, -120], 1e4, 1e7)
print(f"flat -120 dBc/Hz: {flat*1e12:.2f} ps")

# A PLL-like profile: flat in-band, then rolling off. Made up.
offsets = [1e3, 1e5, 1e6, 1e7]
l_dbc = [-100, -105, -125, -145]
sigma = rms_jitter(f0, offsets, l_dbc, 1e3, 1e7)
print(f"PLL-like profile: {sigma*1e12:.2f} ps")
for f_in in (5e6, 50e6):
    snr = -20 * np.log10(2 * np.pi * f_in * sigma)
    print(f"f_in {f_in/1e6:4.0f} MHz: jitter-limited SNR {snr:5.1f} dB")

For the made-up PLL-like profile the total is about 6.05 ps. Integrating each segment separately shows where it comes from: about 4.5 ps from 1 kHz to 100 kHz, 3.8 ps from 100 kHz to 1 MHz, and only 1.2 ps from 1 MHz to 10 MHz (the parts add in quadrature, not linearly). The in-band plateau dominates, even though the curve is lowest far out, because a flat region covering decades of frequency has a lot of area. Lowering the PLL's in-band noise, by a cleaner reference or a smaller divide ratio, usually pays more than improving the far-out floor.

Why ADCs care

An ADC samples at the clock edge. If the edge is early or late by Δt, the sample is taken where the input has moved by slope × Δt. The steepest slope of a sine of amplitude A and frequency fin is 2πfinA, so timing error turns into voltage error that grows with input frequency. Averaged over the sine, this sets a ceiling:

SNRjitter = −20 log10(2π fin σt)

With σt = 6.05 ps, a 5 MHz input is limited to about 74.4 dB (roughly 12 effective bits), while a 50 MHz input is limited to about 54.4 dB (under 9 bits). The same converter with the same clock loses more than three bits just because the input frequency rose tenfold. Note what is absent from the formula: the sample rate and the ADC's resolution. A 16-bit converter clocked by this source and digitising 50 MHz is a 9-bit converter. For high-frequency sampling, the clock is part of the ADC.

Why radios care

In a receiver, the local oscillator mixes the wanted channel down to baseband. Phase noise on the oscillator means the mixer is effectively a range of frequencies, so a strong signal in a neighbouring channel gets mixed down too, its energy spread by the oscillator's skirts onto the wanted channel. This is reciprocal mixing, and it is why radio standards push synthesiser phase noise at the offsets where adjacent channels sit. In the transmitter, the same skirts leak power into neighbouring channels. Close-in phase noise also rotates constellation points, limiting how dense a modulation can be used.

Common pitfalls:

  • Comparing jitter figures with different definitions or integration bandwidths
  • Ignoring supply noise; ripple on an oscillator's supply appears as spurs and deterministic jitter
  • Routing a sampling clock through ordinary logic gates, each of which adds its own jitter
  • Budgeting only random jitter and forgetting reference spurs

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script with numpy, change the in-band plateau by 6 dB, and check that the jitter roughly doubles or halves when that region dominates.
  2. Generate a sampled sine with numpy, perturb each sample time by Gaussian noise of a chosen RMS, compute SNR from an FFT, and compare with the formula.
  3. Simulate a ring oscillator in ngspice with transient noise enabled (see the trnoise source option in the ngspice manual), record the edge times, and compute period and cycle-to-cycle jitter from them.
