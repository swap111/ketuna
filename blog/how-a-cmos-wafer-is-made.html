How a CMOS wafer is made: the fabrication steps every designer should picture

Analog & mixed-signal Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Every layer you draw in a layout becomes a mask and a set of process steps. Follow a CMOS wafer from bare silicon through lithography, implant, etch, deposition and metallisation, mapped to the layers in a layout.

In this article

  2. Front end: building the transistors
  3. Back end: wiring them together

When you open a layout in a tool such as Magic or KLayout, you see coloured rectangles on named layers: well, diffusion, poly, contact, metal. It is easy to treat them as abstract drawing layers. They are not. Each one becomes a photomask, and each mask drives a sequence of physical steps that add, remove or modify material on a silicon wafer. Picturing those steps explains why design rules exist, why some structures match better than others and why some mistakes are expensive. This article walks through a simplified CMOS process in the order a wafer experiences it.

The four basic operations

Almost every step in a fab is one of four things, repeated dozens of times:

  • Lithography: coat the wafer with a light-sensitive resist, expose it through a mask, and develop it so that resist remains only where the mask says. The resist pattern decides where the next step acts.
  • Adding material: grow an oxide by heating silicon in oxygen, or deposit a film (oxide, nitride, polysilicon, metal) by chemical or physical deposition.
  • Removing material: etch away whatever the resist does not protect. Plasma (dry) etching removes material mostly straight down, which keeps fine features sharp; wet etching attacks in all directions.
  • Modifying material: fire ions of a dopant element, such as boron or phosphorus, into the exposed silicon (ion implantation), then heat the wafer (anneal) to repair the crystal and activate the dopant.

A fifth operation, chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP), flattens the wafer between layers so the next lithography step has a flat surface to focus on.

Front end: building the transistors

The front end of line creates the transistors in the silicon itself. Start with a polished wafer of lightly doped p-type silicon.

  1. Isolation. Shallow trenches are etched around every region that will hold a transistor, filled with oxide and polished flat. This shallow trench isolation electrically separates neighbouring devices. Everything that is not a trench is called active area or diffusion.
  2. Wells. An implant through a mask creates n-type n-wells where PMOS transistors will live. NMOS transistors sit in the p-type substrate or a p-well.
  3. Gate stack. A very thin, high-quality gate oxide is grown on the active areas. Polysilicon is deposited on top, patterned and etched to form the gates. Where poly crosses diffusion, a transistor is born: the gate width is set by the diffusion, the gate length by the poly line.
  4. Source and drain. Implants of n-type dopant (for NMOS) and p-type (for PMOS), each through its own mask, dope the diffusion on both sides of each gate. The gate itself blocks the implant from the channel underneath, so the source and drain line up with the gate automatically. This self-alignment is why transistors can be so small and so reproducible.
  5. Silicide. A thin metal layer reacted with exposed silicon lowers the resistance of the source, drain and gate surfaces.

Back end: wiring them together

The back end of line builds the interconnect above the transistors.

  1. Pre-metal dielectric. An insulating layer covers the transistors and is polished flat.
  2. Contacts. Holes are etched through the dielectric down to source, drain and gate, and filled with a metal plug.
  3. Metal layers. A metal layer is deposited and patterned into wires; then another dielectric, then vias to connect to the next metal layer, and so on. Older processes pattern aluminium by etching; newer ones etch trenches into the dielectric, fill them with copper and polish off the excess (the damascene process). Upper metal layers are usually thicker, for power distribution and long wires.
  4. Passivation. A tough protective layer seals the top, with openings only over the bond pads.

From layout layers to process steps

Every drawn layer maps to these steps. Taking the open sky130 process as an example, the GDS layer names used in its documentation and in KLayout correspond roughly as follows (Magic shows simpler abstract layers such as ndiff and poly and generates several of these, like the implant layers, for you):

Layout layer (sky130 naming) What it controls
nwell N-well implant region for PMOS devices
diff, tap Active areas (everything not trench); taps are well and substrate contacts
poly Gate polysilicon; transistor = poly crossing diff
nsdm, psdm Which active areas receive n+ or p+ source/drain implant
licon1, li1 Contacts and a local interconnect layer specific to this process
mcon, met1 … met5, via … via4 Contacts to metal, metal layers and vias between them

Some layers are drawn directly; others are generated by the tools from combinations, and some masks exist only in the fab's data preparation. The principle holds: what you draw is what gets patterned.

Why the rules look the way they do

Design rules are the process steps written as geometry.

  • Minimum width and spacing follow from what lithography can resolve and how etching widens or narrows features.
  • Poly must extend past diffusion (gate endcap) so that a slight misalignment between the two masks does not leave a path from source to drain around the end of the gate.
  • Enclosure rules, such as metal around a contact, exist because two masks never align perfectly.
  • Density rules come from CMP: areas with too little or too much metal polish unevenly.
  • Antenna rules exist because long metal wires collect charge during plasma etching, and if connected only to a gate, that charge can damage the thin gate oxide.

Try it yourself

  1. Install Magic with the sky130 technology files and draw one NMOS transistor by crossing a poly line over n-diffusion. Write it out as GDS and note which mask layers Magic generated from your two drawn shapes.
  2. Run DRC, deliberately shorten the poly endcap, and read the rule that fails. Explain it in terms of mask misalignment.
  3. Open the same layout in KLayout and look at it layer by layer, sketching the cross-section you expect for a cut through the gate.
