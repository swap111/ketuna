Writing requirements that can be tested: shall, measurable, one thing each

Systems & product engineering Beginner 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

How to turn a wish like "long battery life" into a requirement with a number, a unit, a condition and an agreed test, so that everyone can tell whether the product passes.

Every product starts as a list of wishes: it should be cheap, rugged, fast and last a long time on a battery. Wishes are useful for a conversation, but you cannot build or test against them, because two honest engineers will read "long battery life" and picture different numbers. A requirement is a wish rewritten so that a test can say yes or no. Learning to write them well is one of the cheapest skills you can pick up, and it pays off on every project afterwards.

What makes a statement a requirement

A good requirement has a small number of parts, and you can check each one:

  • A subject: which thing the statement is about (the system, the radio board, the firmware).
  • "Shall": the word that marks a binding requirement.
  • One measurable behaviour: a quantity with a unit, or an observable event.
  • Conditions: temperature, supply voltage, load, operating mode, measurement window.
  • A verification method: how you will prove it, agreed before the design starts.
  • A unique ID: so tests, reviews and change requests can point at it.

The word choice matters because many teams use a convention: "shall" for something that must be met, "should" for a goal that is desirable but not binding, and "will" for a statement of fact or intent that is not tested (for example, "the enclosure will be supplied by the mechanical team"). If your team uses this convention, use it consistently. Mixing "must", "needs to" and "is required to" in the same document leaves readers guessing which ones count.

From wish to requirement, step by step

Take the wish "the sensor node should have long battery life". Ask questions until a number falls out:

  1. What does the user actually need? A farmer does not want to replace batteries during a growing season. Ask how long that is for this crop and region, and write the answer down.
  2. What quantity controls that? Average supply current, given a battery of known capacity.
  3. Under what conditions? A reporting interval, a supply voltage, a temperature range.
  4. How will we measure it? Over a fixed window long enough to include sleep, wake-up and transmission.

The result might read: SR-12: The node shall draw an average supply current of no more than 50 µA at 3.0 V, with one measurement and transmission every 10 minutes, averaged over 24 hours at 25 °C. Notice what it does not say. It does not name a microcontroller or a sleep mode. Those are design choices, and writing them into the requirement would close off better designs before anyone has looked for them.

Here are a few more rewrites. The numbers are made up for illustration; real ones come from your users and your analysis.

Wish Testable requirement
"Should start quickly" The display shall show the first reading within 2 s of the power switch being turned on, at supply voltages from 3.0 V to 4.2 V
"Rugged" The unit shall operate normally after six drops from 1.0 m onto concrete, one on each face
"Accurate temperature" The reported temperature shall be within ±0.5 °C of a calibrated reference from 0 °C to 50 °C
"Easy to charge" The battery shall charge from 10 % to 90 % in no more than 3 h from a 5 V, 1 A supply

One thing per requirement

The most common defect in a requirements list is the word "and". Consider: The badge shall detect a fall within 1 s and send an alert to the phone within 3 s. If detection works but the alert takes 5 s, has the requirement passed or failed? Half of it has. The test report now needs a paragraph of explanation, and the tracking spreadsheet cannot show the status honestly. Split it into two IDs, one for detection and one for the alert, and each gets a clean pass or fail.

The same reasoning applies to ranges hidden in adjectives. "Shall work in hot and humid conditions" is two requirements with no numbers. Write one for temperature and one for humidity, each with limits, and say whether condensation is allowed.

Choosing the verification method

Not every requirement is proven by a bench test. Systems engineers usually pick one of four methods, and it is worth recording the choice next to each requirement:

Method What it means Example
Inspection Look at the item or its documents Connector type, label text, colour
Analysis Calculation or simulation Thermal margin, worst-case timing
Demonstration Operate it and observe, without detailed measurement The user can pair the device with a phone
Test Controlled stimulus, measured response, pass limit Average current, accuracy, drop survival

Writing the method early exposes requirements that nobody can verify. If the only honest method for "the product shall last ten years" is to wait ten years, you need to rewrite it as something you can check now, such as derating rules for parts plus an accelerated life test plan.

A small checker you can run

A short script will not judge whether a requirement is sensible, but it catches mechanical problems before a human reviewer spends time on them. This one flags vague words, missing "shall", likely compound requirements and missing digits.

import re

VAGUE = ["fast", "quick", "easy", "robust", "user-friendly", "low",
         "high", "sufficient", "adequate", "approximately", "etc"]

def check(req_id, text):
    issues = []
    t = text.lower()
    if " shall " not in f" {t} ":
        issues.append("no 'shall'")
    if re.search(r"\band\b", t):
        issues.append("contains 'and' - one requirement or two?")
    for w in VAGUE:
        if re.search(rf"\b{re.escape(w)}\b", t):
            issues.append(f"vague word '{w}'")
    if not re.search(r"\d", t):
        issues.append("no number")
    return [f"{req_id}: {i}" for i in issues]

reqs = {
    "SR-12": "The node shall draw no more than 50 uA average at 3.0 V.",
    "SR-13": "The app should be easy to use.",
    "SR-14": "The badge shall detect a fall and alert the phone quickly.",
}
for rid, text in reqs.items():
    for line in check(rid, text):
        print(line)

Running it prints no findings for SR-12, three for SR-13 (no "shall", "easy", no number) and two for SR-14 (the "and" and the missing number). That last result is the point: SR-14 also has a vague word, "quickly", which the script missed because the pattern only matches the whole word "quick". Tools catch the obvious cases; a reviewer catches the rest. Use both.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the design as the requirement. "The node shall use a 2,000 mAh cell" is a design decision. State the need (operating time) and let the architecture choose the cell.
  • Numbers without conditions. "Accuracy ±1 %" means little without the range, temperature and reference.
  • Untestable negatives. "The firmware shall never crash" cannot be proven by testing. Write what must happen instead: a watchdog recovery time, a fault log entry.
  • Reusing IDs. Once SR-12 is deleted, nobody should ever reuse the number, or old test reports will point at the wrong thing.

Try it yourself

  1. Pick a device you own, such as a power bank or a fan, and write five testable requirements for it with IDs, conditions and a verification method for each.
  2. Run the script above on them with Python 3, extend the vague-word list, and see which of your requirements it flags.
  3. Swap lists with a friend and review each other's requirements for hidden "and"s and missing conditions. Agree on a pass limit for each before you argue about anything else.
