Running a field pilot for sensing hardware: from a handful of prototypes to real users

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How to plan a field pilot for sensing hardware, choosing sites, working through local partners, logging and updating devices remotely, planning maintenance visits and deciding up front what the pilot must prove.

In this article

A sensor node that has worked on your bench for a month has proved one thing: it works on your bench. The field adds heat, dust, insects, monsoon humidity, unreliable power, patchy connectivity and people who unplug things to charge their phones. A field pilot is a small, planned deployment that exposes your design to those conditions before you build hundreds. Run well, it turns unknowns into a list of specific fixes. Run badly, it produces a few weeks of silence and no idea why.

Decide what the pilot must prove

Write down, before anything is deployed, the questions the pilot exists to answer. Each should have a measurable pass condition. For example:

Question Evidence that answers it
Does the device survive the site conditions? Days of continuous operation, resets logged, enclosure inspected
Is the data good enough? Comparison against a reference instrument at some sites
Does connectivity work where users are? Fraction of expected readings received, per site
Does the battery or solar budget hold? Measured supply voltage over time, including cloudy days
Will users actually use it? Usage records and structured conversations with users

Keep the list short. A pilot that tries to answer everything usually answers nothing clearly. Also decide what would make you stop or redesign, so a bad result leads to a decision instead of an argument.

Choose sites deliberately

The temptation is to deploy wherever it is convenient, such as a relative's farm or the campus rooftop. Convenient sites tend to be the easy ones. Pick sites that span the conditions you expect to meet: at least one with weak mobile signal, one with harsh heat or dust, one where power is unreliable and one where you can reach the device quickly.

Place a reference instrument alongside a few of your devices where data quality matters. Without a reference you can tell that readings changed, but not whether they are right.

Get access through local partners

You will rarely be able to visit every site yourself. A local partner, such as a farmer producer organisation, a school, a cooperative, a local technician or a field NGO, can host devices, check on them and tell you what is really happening. Treat them as part of the team:

  • explain plainly what the device does, what data it collects and who will see it
  • agree in writing who owns the device and the data, and what happens at the end of the pilot
  • give them a one-page guide with pictures: what normal looks like, what to do if a light blinks red, whom to call
  • respect their time; a pilot that creates work for the host without benefit will not last

Collect only the data you need, tell people what you collect, and get consent where people are involved. That is both the right thing to do and the way to keep a partner's trust.

Build for remote visibility and updates

Every visit costs time and money, so design devices to tell you their own health. Each node should send a small heartbeat at a regular interval, even when it has no new data, carrying firmware version, uptime, reset cause, supply voltage, signal strength and an error counter. Store readings locally when the link is down and send them later with timestamps.

With heartbeats, you can spot problems from your desk. A short script that flags silent or rebooting nodes is often the most useful tool in a pilot. The example below works on a made-up log of heartbeats, one line per node per message:

from collections import defaultdict

log = """n01,1000,boot=0 v=3.9
n02,1000,boot=0 v=3.7
n01,1600,boot=0 v=3.9
n02,1600,boot=1 v=3.4
n03,1000,boot=0 v=4.0
n01,2200,boot=0 v=3.8
n02,2200,boot=2 v=3.2"""

now, period = 2400, 600                 # seconds, illustrative
last, boots = {}, defaultdict(int)
for line in log.splitlines():
    node, t, rest = line.split(",")
    fields = dict(kv.split("=") for kv in rest.split())
    last[node] = int(t)
    boots[node] = int(fields["boot"])

for node in sorted(last):
    late = now - last[node] > 2 * period
    flag = "SILENT" if late else ("REBOOTING" if boots[node] else "ok")
    print(node, flag)

It prints n01 ok, n02 REBOOTING and n03 SILENT. Here n02 is resetting while its voltage falls, which points to a power problem, and n03 has gone quiet, which needs a call to the local partner.

Over-the-air updates need care. An update that fails halfway in a remote field can turn a device into a brick. Use a bootloader that keeps the previous image, verifies the new one and falls back if it does not start properly. Roll updates out to one or two devices first.

Plan maintenance visits and learn from them

Schedule visits rather than waiting for failures, and treat each as a data-collection trip. Photograph every device before touching it. Note dust, water marks, insect nests, corrosion, loose cables and how the device was mounted. Check the reference instrument and clean sensors where needed, recording what you did, because cleaning changes the readings.

Keep a simple log per device: site, date, what was observed, what was changed. Patterns across sites, such as connectors corroding, mounts loosening or the same reset cause, are the real output of a pilot.

Common mistakes

  • Deploying before deciding what the pilot must prove
  • Picking only easy, convenient sites
  • No heartbeat, so a silent device could be dead, offline or stolen
  • Updating all devices at once and bricking the pilot
  • Changing firmware and hardware during the pilot without recording when
  • Treating local partners as caretakers rather than collaborators

Try it yourself

  1. Write a one-page pilot plan for a sensor you have built, with three questions and a pass condition for each.
  2. Add a heartbeat message to your firmware with version, uptime, reset cause and supply voltage, and extend the script above to read it.
  3. Use the open-source MCUboot bootloader with Zephyr to test an update that fails halfway, and confirm the device rolls back.
  4. Leave one device outdoors for two weeks, then inspect and photograph it as if it were a pilot visit.
