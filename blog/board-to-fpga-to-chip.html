From board to FPGA to chip: when a product earns a custom chip

Systems & product engineering Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

How one-time engineering cost, unit cost, volume, power, size and risk decide whether a function should stay on a board, move into an FPGA or become a custom chip, with a break-even calculation and its caveats.

Engineers who love chips tend to see every product as a future custom chip. Engineers who have paid for one tend to be more careful. Moving a function from a microcontroller and discrete parts, to an FPGA, to an application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) is a ladder, and each rung trades higher one-time cost and risk for lower unit cost, lower power or smaller size. A product should climb only when it has earned it. This article shows how to tell, with a break-even calculation and the factors the calculation leaves out.

The ladder and what each rung buys

  • Board with standard parts. A microcontroller, perhaps a small accelerator or analogue parts. Lowest one-time cost, fastest to change, available from many suppliers. Limited by what off-the-shelf parts can do in performance, power and size.
  • FPGA. Custom digital logic without fabricating silicon: parallel datapaths, precise timing, custom interfaces. Changeable after shipping. Higher unit cost and higher power than an equivalent ASIC, because programmable routing and lookup tables cost area and energy compared with fixed gates.
  • ASIC. Logic, and possibly analogue, fixed in silicon. Lowest unit cost at volume, lowest power and smallest size for the function, the ability to integrate several board parts into one. Highest one-time cost, longest cycle, and a mistake can mean another fabrication run.

The one-time costs are called non-recurring engineering (NRE): design and verification effort, tools, masks or shuttle fees, packaging and test development, qualification. For an ASIC they dominate; for a board they are small.

A break-even calculation

The simplest model treats total cost as NRE plus unit cost times volume:

Total(N) = NRE + c × N

For two options, the break-even volume is where their totals are equal:

Nbreak-even = (NREASIC − NREFPGA) / (cFPGA − cASIC)

Take illustrative values in arbitrary cost units, made up for this example: the FPGA option has NRE of 20 and a unit cost of 1.0; the ASIC has NRE of 10,000 and a unit cost of 0.1. Then

N = (10,000 − 20) / (1.0 − 0.1) ≈ 11,100 units

Below that volume the FPGA is cheaper over the product's life; above it the ASIC is. At 5,000 units the average cost per unit is about 1.0 for the FPGA and 2.1 for the ASIC; at 50,000 units it is about 1.0 against 0.3.

What the simple model leaves out

The calculation is a starting point. Several factors move the break-even, usually upwards.

Respin risk. If there is a real chance the first silicon needs a correction, the expected NRE is higher. With an illustrative 30 % chance of a respin costing half the original NRE again, the expected ASIC NRE becomes 10,000 × (1 + 0.3 × 0.5) = 11,500, and the break-even moves to about 12,800 units. Strong verification lowers this risk, which is one reason chip teams invest so heavily in it.

Time. An ASIC adds months of design, fabrication and qualification before revenue. If the market window is short, an FPGA product shipping now may earn more than a cheaper ASIC product shipping later. A common path is to launch on an FPGA and move to an ASIC once volume is proven.

Volume uncertainty. Break-even uses the volume you will sell, which you do not know. If the ASIC only pays off at the optimistic forecast, that is a business bet, and it should be presented as one.

Board-level savings. An ASIC can absorb other parts: a microcontroller, an ADC, glue logic, a regulator. Count the board cost, area and assembly saved, not just the FPGA it replaces. This can lower the break-even substantially.

Flexibility. An FPGA can be updated in the field when a standard changes or a bug is found. An ASIC cannot, except for whatever programmability you design in, such as a small processor core running firmware.

Reasons that are not about cost

Some products move to custom silicon at volumes where the arithmetic alone says no:

  • Power. A battery wearable may simply not be possible with an FPGA's static and dynamic power; a dedicated low-power chip makes the product exist.
  • Size. Hearing aids, implants and small sensors may have no room for a board of standard parts.
  • Performance per watt. Dedicated datapaths, such as a neural network accelerator or a signal processing pipeline, can do far more work per joule than general-purpose parts.
  • Security and control of the design. A custom chip can hold keys and logic that are hard to extract, and reduces dependence on specific off-the-shelf parts.
  • Integration of analogue and digital. Sensor interfaces, radios and power management combined on one die.

These are legitimate reasons, but each should be written as a requirement with a number, such as an energy per inference or a board area, so the decision can be checked rather than asserted.

Lowering the first step

The NRE barrier for a first chip has come down for learning and prototyping. Open process design kits such as sky130, gf180 and IHP SG13G2, open-source flows such as LibreLane and OpenROAD, and multi-project wafer shuttles, where many designs share one mask set, let a small team build a test chip at a fraction of a full mask set's cost. That does not change the economics of volume production, but it means you can retire technical risk, such as a custom analogue block or an accelerator architecture, on real silicon before betting on a production chip.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing unit costs and ignoring NRE. The cheapest unit is not the cheapest product.
  • Using the optimistic volume only. Show the break-even against a range of forecasts.
  • Forgetting the time to market. Months of delay have a cost the model does not show.
  • Skipping the FPGA step for a new design. Proving the logic on an FPGA first retires risk before the expensive step.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a Python function for total cost against volume for three options (board, FPGA, ASIC) and plot them with matplotlib over a range of volumes, then add respin risk to the ASIC.
  2. Take a small RTL design, such as a UART or a filter, synthesise it with Yosys for an FPGA target and run it through LibreLane on sky130, and compare the reported resources and area.
  3. For a product idea of your own, list which non-cost reasons, if any, would justify a custom chip, and write each as a requirement with a number.
