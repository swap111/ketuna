Which corners to sign off: process, voltage and temperature for digital timing

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Setup is signed off where cells are slowest and hold where they are fastest. Learn which process, voltage and temperature combinations that means, and how on-chip variation derates tighten every check.

In this article

  2. Setup at slow corners, hold at fast corners
  3. On-chip variation: two flops on one die are not identical

A timing report is only true for the conditions it was computed at. Real chips are made on wafers where transistors come out a little faster or slower, run from supplies that sag and overshoot, and sit in environments from cold to hot. Sign-off has to show that timing holds across all of that, without running an infinite number of analyses. The answer is a small set of carefully chosen corners, plus a model of variation within a single chip.

The three axes

Each corner fixes three things:

  • Process (P). Fabrication varies from wafer to wafer and lot to lot. Foundries characterise the extremes as corners named by the speed of NMOS and PMOS devices: typical-typical (TT), slow-slow (SS), fast-fast (FF), and the skewed corners slow-fast (SF) and fast-slow (FS). Standard-cell libraries are characterised at several of these.
  • Voltage (V). The supply has a tolerance around its nominal value, plus IR drop inside the chip. Lower voltage means less drive current and slower cells.
  • Temperature (T). The specified operating junction temperature range. At older nodes, cells are slower when hot, because carrier mobility falls. At many newer nodes with low supply voltages, the threshold-voltage effect can dominate and cells may be slower when cold; this is called temperature inversion. Check what your library data shows, rather than assuming.

A library file name in an open PDK usually encodes its corner, for example a sky130 library named with ss_100C_1v60 is the slow-slow process at 100 °C and 1.60 V.

Setup at slow corners, hold at fast corners

The logic follows from the two checks.

  • Setup fails when data is too slow. So you check it where cells and wires are slowest: slow process, low voltage, and whichever temperature is slower for your process. This is often written as SS, low V, high T for older nodes.
  • Hold fails when data is too fast. So you check it where everything is quickest: fast process, high voltage, and the faster temperature, often FF, high V, low T.

Interconnect has its own corners. Metal and dielectric dimensions vary too, so the extraction provides RC corners such as maximum capacitance and minimum capacitance. Setup is usually paired with the high-RC corner and hold with the low-RC corner, though a path dominated by wire resistance can behave differently, which is why foundries specify the combinations to sign off.

Typical corner should still be analysed. It tells you the performance most parts will actually achieve, and it is where power is often estimated. But it is not a sign-off corner on its own.

On-chip variation: two flops on one die are not identical

Corners describe the whole chip being slow or fast together. Within one chip, neighbouring transistors still differ slightly, and voltage and temperature vary from region to region. A timing check compares two paths, the launch side and the capture side, so even small differences between them matter.

On-chip variation (OCV) derating models this. For a setup check, the tool makes the launch clock path and the data path a little slower (late derate) and the capture clock path a little faster (early derate), because that combination is the worst case. For hold, it does the opposite.

An illustrative setup check with a 5.0 ns clock, clock latency 1.00 ns to both flops, a data path of 3.00 ns, and setup time 0.10 ns. Derates of 1.05 late and 0.95 early are made up for illustration.

Quantity No derate With derate
Arrival: launch latency + data 1.00 + 3.00 = 4.00 ns 1.05 + 3.15 = 4.20 ns
Required: period + capture latency − setup 5.00 + 1.00 − 0.10 = 5.90 ns 5.00 + 0.95 − 0.10 = 5.85 ns
Slack 1.90 ns 1.65 ns

Derating cost 0.25 ns of slack here.

Part of that is pessimistic. If the first 0.80 ns of clock latency is a shared buffer chain that feeds both flops, that chain cannot be both slow (for launch) and fast (for capture) at the same moment. Common path pessimism removal (CPPR) gives back the difference on the shared part: 0.80 × (1.05 − 0.95) = 0.08 ns, bringing the slack to 1.73 ns. This is why clock trees that keep related flops on common branches make closure easier.

Flat derates are crude. More refined approaches scale the derate with path depth and distance, because random variations partly cancel over many stages, or describe each cell's delay as a statistical distribution. The idea is the same: the two halves of a timing check do not see identical silicon.

A practical corner list

For a small digital block on an open PDK, a reasonable sign-off set looks like this:

Check Process Voltage Temperature RC
Setup SS minimum slow-side extreme high
Hold FF maximum fast-side extreme low
Hold, second case SS minimum slow-side extreme high
Reference TT nominal room typical

The second hold case catches paths whose clock skew grows at slow corners, where a long clock path can be slowed more than a short data path. Many flows also check hold at typical. Your foundry's sign-off guide, not this table, is the final word, and it may add skewed process corners for some circuits.

Common mistakes

  • Signing off at typical only. Typical is the middle of the distribution; half the parts are slower.
  • Assuming hot is always slow. Check the library tables for temperature inversion.
  • Forgetting IR drop in the voltage corner. The minimum voltage at the cell is the supply minimum minus the grid drop.
  • Turning off CPPR. Without it, every check carries pessimism from shared clock paths.

Try it yourself

  1. List the Liberty files in your sky130 PDK's standard-cell library directory and decode the corner in each name.
  2. In OpenSTA, load the same netlist with the slow library, create the clock, run report_checks -path_delay max, then repeat with the fast library and -path_delay min.
  3. Apply set_timing_derate -late 1.05 and set_timing_derate -early 0.95, rerun, and compare slack with the table above.
  4. Look at the multi-corner summary that LibreLane produces and identify which corner limits setup and which limits hold.
