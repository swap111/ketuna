Thermal design basics: junction temperature and heat paths

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How to estimate a part's junction temperature from its power and the thermal resistances between die and air, why copper area and airflow change the answer, and how to check the result against the part's limits.

In this article

  2. A worked example: a linear regulator

Every watt a part dissipates has to leave as heat, and the only question is how hot the part gets while that happens. Too hot, and it shuts down, drifts out of specification or wears out early. The calculation that answers the question is short, uses the same arithmetic as Ohm's law, and is worth doing for every regulator, power switch, motor driver and processor on a board before layout, not after the first prototype is too hot to touch.

Heat flow as a circuit

Heat flows from the hot silicon die (the junction) through the package, into the board, and out to the surrounding air. Each step resists the flow. The thermal resistance θ, in °C per watt, says how many degrees of temperature difference it takes to push one watt through that step. The analogy with electrical circuits is exact enough to use directly:

Thermal quantity Electrical analogue
Power P (W) Current
Temperature difference ΔT (°C) Voltage
Thermal resistance θ (°C/W) Resistance

So the junction temperature is

TJ = TA + P × θJA

where TA is the ambient air temperature around the part and θJA is the total junction-to-ambient resistance. Resistances in series add, just as in a circuit.

A worked example: a linear regulator

A linear regulator drops the difference between input and output voltage as heat. Suppose one takes 5 V from USB and supplies 3.3 V at 300 mA. The numbers that follow are illustrative; take real thermal resistances from the part's datasheet and your board.

P = (5.0 − 3.3) V × 0.3 A = 0.51 W

The product sits in a closed plastic case, and you estimate the air inside reaches 45 °C on a hot day. The part's absolute maximum junction temperature is 125 °C, and you want at least 20 °C of margin, so you aim for TJ ≤ 105 °C. The largest θJA you can accept is

θJA,max = (105 − 45) / 0.51 ≈ 118 °C/W

Now compare two layouts:

  • Small package, minimal copper, with an illustrative θJA of 150 °C/W: TJ = 45 + 0.51 × 150 = 121.5 °C. Under the absolute maximum, but with almost no margin and likely to hit the part's thermal shutdown on a hot day.
  • Same part, exposed pad soldered to a copper area with vias to an inner plane, giving θJC = 10, θCB = 5 and θBA = 45 °C/W, a total of 60 °C/W: TJ = 45 + 0.51 × 60 = 75.6 °C. Comfortable.

The silicon did not change. The copper did. If no layout gets you under the limit, change the design: a switching regulator wastes far less power for the same job, or a lower input voltage reduces the drop.

Where the numbers come from

Datasheets give θJA, but usually measured on a standard test board under standard conditions, which may have much more or much less copper than yours. Treat it as a comparison between packages, not as your board's value. Many datasheets also give θJC (junction to case) and a characterisation parameter such as ψJT for estimating junction temperature from a measured case-top temperature. Read the definitions in the datasheet itself; they are not interchangeable.

The board-to-air term depends on:

  • Copper area connected to the part's thermal pad or leads, and its thickness. Copper spreads heat sideways so a larger area of board surface sheds it.
  • Thermal vias under exposed pads, carrying heat to inner and bottom layers.
  • Airflow. Still air inside a sealed case removes heat slowly; even gentle natural convection through vents helps, and a fan changes the picture entirely.
  • Neighbours. Two hot parts side by side heat each other; the ambient for each is the other's surroundings, not the room.

Checking against limits

A complete thermal check for each hot part has four steps:

  1. Worst-case power: highest input voltage, highest load current, highest duty cycle.
  2. Worst-case ambient: the hottest air the product will see inside its enclosure, not the room temperature of your lab. Sunlight on an outdoor case, or a closed cabinet, can raise it a long way.
  3. Junction temperature from the thermal chain, compared against the datasheet's recommended operating limit with margin, not just the absolute maximum.
  4. Measurement on the prototype: a thermocouple on the case or a thermal camera, at worst-case load, in the closed enclosure, after it has reached steady state.

Remember that reliability also depends on temperature. Many failure mechanisms accelerate as parts get hotter, so lower junction temperatures generally mean longer life, not just avoiding shutdown.

Common mistakes

  • Using the datasheet θJA as if it applied to your board. It was measured on a different one.
  • Designing for room temperature. The inside of a closed box in summer is the real ambient.
  • Forgetting the exposed pad. An unsoldered or unconnected thermal pad can multiply θJA.
  • Measuring too soon. Boards take minutes to reach thermal steady state; a measurement after thirty seconds understates the problem.

Try it yourself

  1. List every part on a board you know that dissipates more than about 100 mW, compute its worst-case power, and estimate TJ with a simple Python function using the datasheet values.
  2. In KiCad, add a copper zone and a grid of thermal vias under a regulator's exposed pad and note how much board area you are giving the part.
  3. Run the regulator at full load in its enclosure, measure the case temperature with a thermocouple meter after it settles, and compare with your estimate. Explain any gap.
