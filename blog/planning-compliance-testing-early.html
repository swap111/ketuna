Planning compliance testing early: EMC, safety and environmental tests

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

Which kinds of compliance tests electronic products typically face, how to run cheap pre-compliance checks on the bench, and the design habits that give a product a good chance of passing the first time.

A product that fails a formal compliance test in a lab is a product that goes back to the drawing board, often for a new board spin, while the launch date slips and the test fee is paid again. Most failures are not surprises in hindsight: a fast clock routed over a split ground plane, a switching regulator with a long loop, a connector with no protection, an enclosure that traps heat. They could have been caught, or designed out, months earlier. This article explains the families of tests products typically face, how to check for problems on your own bench and how to design to pass first time.

The families of tests

Requirements depend on the product category, where it is sold and the standards that apply, so always check the current rules for your product. Broadly, electronic products face three families of tests:

Family Question it answers Typical tests
EMC: emissions Does the product disturb other equipment? Radiated emissions, conducted emissions on power and signal cables
EMC: immunity Does the product keep working when disturbed? Electrostatic discharge, radiated fields, fast transients, surges, conducted disturbances, voltage dips
Safety Can it cause shock, fire or injury? Insulation, creepage and clearance, temperature rise, abnormal and fault conditions, enclosure strength
Environmental Does it survive its conditions of use? Temperature, humidity, vibration and shock, dust and water ingress

International standards define most of these. The IEC electromagnetic compatibility series (IEC 61000) defines many immunity test methods; product-family standards set the limits and criteria that apply to particular kinds of equipment. The ingress protection rating that describes dust and water resistance is defined in IEC 60529. In India, the corresponding Indian Standards and any notified requirements apply. Read the product standard first: it tells you which tests apply and what counts as a pass.

Why products fail

The mechanisms are physical and predictable:

  • Radiated emissions come mostly from fast edges driving currents around large loops, or from cables acting as antennas when common-mode noise leaks onto them.
  • Conducted emissions come from switching power supplies pushing noise back onto the mains or DC input.
  • ESD failures happen when a discharge to a connector or seam finds a path through sensitive circuits instead of straight to ground.
  • Surge and fast transient failures happen at power and long-cable ports with no protection, or protection with a long return path.
  • Temperature-rise failures in safety testing come from parts running hotter in the sealed enclosure than on the open bench.

Notice that the cables and the enclosure appear again and again. A bare board on the bench is not the product; test the product as it will ship, with its real cables, supply and enclosure.

Pre-compliance on the bench

Formal tests need an accredited lab, but you can find most problems early with modest equipment:

  1. Near-field probes and a spectrum analyser. Sweep a small magnetic loop over the board and cables. Hot spots at clock harmonics or regulator switching frequencies show where emissions come from. Compare before and after each fix; relative changes are what matter here.
  2. Cable current clamp. Common-mode current on a cable is a strong predictor of radiated emissions. Measure it on every cable leaving the product.
  3. Line impedance stabilisation network (LISN). It presents a defined impedance to the product's power input so you can measure conducted noise against limits in a repeatable way.
  4. ESD gun, if you can borrow one. Zap connectors, buttons and seams at increasing levels, and watch for resets, lock-ups or corrupted data.
  5. Thermal check. Put a thermocouple or thermal camera on the hottest parts, inside the closed enclosure, at the highest ambient temperature you expect, at full load.

Treat these as screening, not proof. A pre-compliance result with comfortable margin is encouraging; a marginal one means more design work before booking the lab.

Designing to pass the first time

Most EMC and safety performance is set by decisions made in the schematic and layout:

  • Solid ground plane under high-speed signals, with no splits under their return paths
  • Small current loops for switching regulators: input capacitor, switch and diode or synchronous switch placed tightly together
  • Slow edges where speed is not needed, using series resistors or slew-rate settings on fast outputs
  • Filtering and protection at every connector, because cables are where noise leaves and disturbances enter
  • A clear ESD path from exposed metal and connector shells to chassis or ground, away from sensitive circuits
  • Creepage and clearance designed in from the start on mains products, with the right insulation across the isolation barrier
  • Thermal design with margin for the enclosure and the hottest climate of use
  • Enclosure seams, vents and gaskets considered together with the electronics

Then hold a design review dedicated to compliance before the layout is released, with someone who did not design the board. A second pair of eyes catches the regulator loop you stopped seeing.

Planning the schedule

Put compliance in the plan from the first week: identify the standards, budget pre-compliance time after each board spin, book the formal lab early and leave time for one round of fixes. Keep production-representative samples aside for testing; test results on a prototype that differs from the shipping product prove little.

Common mistakes

  • Testing a bare board instead of the product with its cables and enclosure
  • Booking the formal lab before any pre-compliance work
  • Adding filters and ferrites at the end instead of designing for low noise
  • Forgetting immunity, then discovering the product resets during ESD tests
  • Changing a component after testing without checking whether results still hold

Try it yourself

  1. Make a near-field probe from a small loop of coaxial cable and use it with a software-defined radio or spectrum analyser to find the noisiest spot on one of your boards.
  2. Review a switching regulator layout of yours in KiCad and draw the high-current loop; see if you can make it smaller.
  3. Simulate a regulator's switching node with and without a snubber in ngspice and compare the ringing.
  4. Write a one-page compliance plan for a product: standards, tests, pre-compliance steps and samples.
