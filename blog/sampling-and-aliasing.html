Sampling and aliasing: why an anti-alias filter comes before every ADC

Analog & mixed-signal Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Sampling folds every frequency above half the sample rate back into the band you care about, where no software can remove it. See how folding works, compute where a tone lands, and size a simple filter.

An ADC does not watch a signal continuously. It takes snapshots, one per sample period, and everything that happens between snapshots is invisible. That has a surprising consequence: a signal that changes too fast does not simply disappear; it shows up disguised as a slower signal that was never there. This is aliasing, and once it has happened, no digital filter can undo it. That is why the last analog block before almost every ADC is a low-pass filter.

The sampling theorem in one sentence

If you sample at rate fs, you can represent faithfully only frequencies below fs/2, called the Nyquist frequency. A signal band that stays below fs/2 can, in principle, be reconstructed exactly from its samples. This is the sampling theorem, and its condition is usually written as fs > 2fmax.

The theorem is a promise about signals that are band-limited. Real inputs are not: there is always some noise, some interference from a switching regulator, some harmonic of the mains, at higher frequencies. The question is what happens to them.

Why high frequencies fold back

Sample a 7 kHz cosine at 10 kHz. Between two samples (0.1 ms), the 7 kHz wave advances by 0.7 of a cycle. A 3 kHz wave advances by 0.3 of a cycle, which looks identical from the sampler's point of view, because advancing 0.7 of a cycle forward lands at the same phase as moving 0.3 backward, and a cosine looks the same either way. The two sets of samples are exactly equal:

import numpy as np

fs = 10_000
n = np.arange(8)
seven = np.cos(2 * np.pi * 7_000 * n / fs)
three = np.cos(2 * np.pi * 3_000 * n / fs)
print(np.round(seven, 3))
print(np.round(three, 3))
print("identical:", np.allclose(seven, three))

Once the samples are taken, the information that it was 7 kHz is gone. The ADC output is a perfectly clean 3 kHz tone.

The general rule is that the frequency axis folds like a paper strip at every multiple of fs/2. A tone at frequency f appears at:

falias = |f − k·fs|, with the integer k chosen so the result lies between 0 and fs/2

With fs = 10 kHz: 7 kHz lands at 3 kHz, 9.5 kHz lands at 0.5 kHz, 12.3 kHz lands at 2.3 kHz and 23 kHz lands at 3 kHz. Every frequency in the world maps to somewhere between 0 and 5 kHz.

A worked example of the danger

Suppose you measure a temperature or pressure signal with useful content below 1 kHz, sampling at 10 kHz. Nearby there is a switching circuit radiating a tone at 9.5 kHz. After sampling, that interference sits at 500 Hz, right in the middle of your measurement band, indistinguishable from a real 500 Hz signal. A digital low-pass filter at 1 kHz will pass it untouched, because in the digital domain it is a 500 Hz signal.

The only place to remove it is before the sampler, in the analog domain, while it still has its true frequency.

Sizing a simple anti-alias filter

The simplest filter is one resistor and one capacitor. A first-order RC low-pass has its −3 dB corner at fc = 1 / (2πRC), and above the corner it attenuates by 20 dB per decade. With R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF, fc ≈ 1.59 kHz, comfortably above the 1 kHz band.

At 9.5 kHz the attenuation is 1 / √(1 + (9.5 / 1.59)2) ≈ 0.165, or about −15.6 dB. That is not much. A full-scale interferer would still appear at about one sixth of full scale. For a 12-bit converter, whose quantization step is about −72 dB below full scale, you would need far more suppression than that to push the alias below one LSB.

There are three levers, and real designs combine them:

  • Steeper filter. Two cascaded poles at the same corner give about −31.3 dB at 9.5 kHz (ignoring the loading between stages, which a buffer would remove). Active filters with more poles roll off faster still, at the cost of op-amps, power and phase shift in the band.
  • Sample faster. At fs = 100 kHz, the first frequency that folds onto the top of a 1 kHz band is 99 kHz. The same single RC attenuates 99 kHz by about −35.9 dB, more than double the decibels of the 10 kHz case, with no extra components. Oversampling relaxes the analog filter and moves the sharp filtering into digital logic, where it is cheap and precise.
  • Know your interference. You only need to suppress energy that actually exists. If the board has nothing above 20 kHz except broadband noise, the requirement is about total noise folding in, not about one strong tone.

The digital filter after the ADC then trims the band precisely and can reduce the data rate. That combination, a gentle analog filter, a fast ADC and a sharp digital filter, is the standard modern approach.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the input is band-limited because the sensor is slow. The wiring between sensor and ADC picks up interference at any frequency.
  • Sampling at exactly twice the highest frequency. The theorem requires strictly more than twice, and any practical filter needs room to roll off, so sample several times faster.
  • Forgetting the ADC's own input. A switched-capacitor ADC input draws charge spikes; a capacitor directly at the ADC pin supplies them, and the filter resistor must be small enough not to spoil settling.
  • Filtering only in software. A digital filter cannot distinguish an alias from a real signal.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the snippet above with numpy, then try other pairs of frequencies with the folding formula and confirm that their samples match.
  2. Sample a 1 kHz sine plus a 9.5 kHz sine at 10 kHz, take an FFT with np.fft.rfft, and find the 500 Hz spike that should not be there.
  3. In ngspice, simulate the RC filter with .ac dec 50 10 1meg, read its response at 9.5 kHz and 99 kHz, and compare with the hand calculation.
