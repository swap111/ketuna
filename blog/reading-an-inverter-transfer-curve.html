Reading a CMOS inverter's transfer curve: noise margins and switching point

Analog & mixed-signal Beginner 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

The voltage transfer curve of a CMOS inverter shows where it switches and how much noise it tolerates. Learn to read the switching point and noise margins, and how transistor sizing moves them.

In this article
  1. What the curve looks like and why
  2. The points that matter
  3. The switching point and how sizing moves it
  4. Pitfalls when reading a real curve
  5. Try it yourself

A logic gate is an analog circuit that we agree to read as digital. The inverter's voltage transfer curve (VTC), output voltage plotted against input voltage, is where that agreement is made precise. It tells you which input voltages count as a clean 0 or 1, how much noise a signal can pick up between gates before it is misread, and where the gate flips. Reading one is the first analog skill a digital designer should have.

What the curve looks like and why

A CMOS inverter is a PMOS from VDD to the output and an NMOS from the output to ground, with their gates tied together as the input. Sweep the input slowly from 0 to VDD and watch the output.

  • Input near 0: the NMOS is off and the PMOS is fully on. The output sits at VDD.
  • Input near VDD: the PMOS is off and the NMOS is fully on. The output sits at 0.
  • In between: both transistors conduct, fighting each other. Over a narrow input range, a small change of input makes a large change of output. This steep part is where the inverter acts as a high-gain amplifier.

The output reaches the rails at both ends because, in steady state, one device is off and the other has no current to carry, so there is no voltage drop across it. That full rail-to-rail swing is a big reason CMOS logic is so robust.

The points that matter

Four voltages describe the curve:

  • VOH and VOL: the output high and low levels. For a static CMOS inverter they are essentially VDD and 0.
  • VIL: the highest input still guaranteed to be read as a 0.
  • VIH: the lowest input still guaranteed to be read as a 1.

VIL and VIH are defined as the two points where the slope of the curve equals −1. The reason is gain. Between those points the magnitude of the slope is greater than 1, so the gate amplifies any disturbance on its input. Outside them, the slope is shallower than 1 and the gate attenuates disturbances, so noise shrinks as it passes through a chain of gates. That is the real meaning of a valid logic level: a region where errors die out instead of growing.

From these come the noise margins:

NML = VIL − VOL and NMH = VOH − VIH

A noise margin is how much noise a driven signal can pick up on the wire, from coupling or ground bounce, before the next gate might misread it. Suppose, for illustration, a 1.8 V inverter has VIL = 0.75 V and VIH = 1.05 V. Then NML = 0.75 V and NMH = 1.8 − 1.05 = 0.75 V. The margins are equal, which happens when the switching point is near the middle of the supply.

The switching point and how sizing moves it

The switching threshold VM is the input voltage at which output equals input. At that point both transistors are in saturation and carry the same current. Setting the two square-law saturation currents equal and solving gives:

VM = (VTN + r(VDD − |VTP|)) / (1 + r), with r = √(kp / kn)

Here kn = μnCox(W/L)n and kp = μpCox(W/L)p are the strengths of the two devices. If r = 1 and the thresholds match, VM sits exactly at VDD/2. A stronger PMOS (larger r) pulls VM up; a stronger NMOS pulls it down.

Holes move more slowly than electrons, so for equal sizes the PMOS is weaker. Here is a worked example with numbers made up for illustration: VDD = 1.8 V, VTN = |VTP| = 0.45 V, equal lengths, and electron mobility 2.5 times hole mobility.

PMOS width / NMOS width r VM
1 0.63 0.80 V
2 0.89 0.87 V
2.5 1.00 0.90 V
5 1.41 0.98 V

Two lessons hide in this table. First, with equal widths VM sits below the middle, which shrinks NML. Second, VM depends on the square root of the strength ratio, so it moves slowly: doubling the PMOS width from 2.5 to 5 moves VM by only about 80 mV. That is good news for robustness, because process variation that changes device strength by a few per cent hardly moves the switching point.

Designers sometimes skew VM on purpose. A gate that should react early to a rising input, or a level detector, may be sized with a deliberately strong NMOS or PMOS.

Pitfalls when reading a real curve

  • Assuming VM = VDD/2. It is only true when the strengths are balanced; always measure it.
  • Reading noise margins at one corner. At a slow-NMOS, fast-PMOS corner the curve shifts right, and one margin shrinks. Check the corners that skew the two devices in opposite directions.
  • Sweeping too fast. The VTC is a DC curve. A transient with fast edges adds delay and makes the curve look skewed. Use a .dc sweep.
  • Confusing the VTC with delay. Equal noise margins do not guarantee equal rise and fall times; those also depend on load capacitance and drive current.

Try it yourself

  1. With ngspice and the sky130 PDK models, write an inverter netlist and run .dc VIN 0 1.8 0.005. Use meas dc vm when v(out)=v(in) to find VM.
  2. Plot the derivative of the output with let gain = deriv(v(out)) and find the two inputs where gain crosses −1. Those are VIL and VIH; compute both noise margins.
  3. Change the PMOS width in steps and fill in a table like the one above. Compare the trend with the formula, then rerun at the sf and fs corners and see which margin suffers.
