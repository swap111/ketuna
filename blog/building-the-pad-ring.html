Building the pad ring: I/O cells, power pads and padframe planning

Physical design & tape-out Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

The pad ring connects the core to the package and protects it from the outside world. Learn how to choose pad order and power pad count, what bonding constrains, and how the ring meets the core grid.

A digital block ends at its pins; a chip ends at its pads. Between the core logic and the package sits the pad ring: a frame of I/O cells around the die that drives signals off chip, receives them, brings in power, and absorbs electrostatic discharge before it reaches thin-oxide transistors. Planning it is a negotiation between the core's needs, the package's geometry and the electrical rules of the I/O library, and its decisions are fixed early because both the package and the core floorplan depend on them.

What lives in the ring

An I/O library for a process provides a family of cells designed to abut each other around the die edge:

  • Signal pads: input, output and bidirectional cells, each with a bond pad, drivers or receivers, and ESD protection. Many offer options such as drive strength, pull-ups or Schmitt-trigger inputs.
  • Core power pads: bring the core supply and ground in, and connect to the core's power ring.
  • I/O power pads: supply the output drivers, often at a higher voltage than the core, on separate rails inside the ring.
  • Corner cells: turn the ring's internal power buses around the corners.
  • Filler cells: fill gaps between pads so the ring's buses and wells stay continuous.

The I/O cells abut so that several supply buses run all the way around the ring inside them, typically core supply, I/O supply and their grounds. Each power pad connects its supply to the matching bus; every signal pad draws from the buses. A gap in the ring breaks those buses, which is why fillers matter.

Choosing the pad order

Pad order is decided with the package and the board in mind, not only the core.

  • Follow the package. Each pad bonds to a package lead or ball. Wires should not cross, so the pad order around the die must match the lead order around the package. If the board layout or a standard pinout fixes the package pins, that fixes your pad order.
  • Keep related signals together. A bus, a serial interface or a clock and its data should sit on adjacent pads, which also keeps them adjacent on the board.
  • Face the core logic. Put pads on the edge closest to the core logic they connect to. A pin on the wrong side forces long wires across the core and can cost timing.
  • Separate noisy from sensitive. Keep fast-switching outputs away from analog inputs, clock inputs and reference pins, and give sensitive pads their own nearby ground.

How many power pads

Two separate limits set the count.

Core current. Each power pad and its bond wire can carry a limited current, set by electromigration in the pad metal and by the wire itself. With illustrative numbers: if the core draws a peak of 100 mA and the library allows 30 mA per power pad, you need at least 100 / 30 ≈ 3.3, so 4 core supply pads, and as many ground pads. Spread them across all four sides, so that no part of the core is far from a supply and the core's ring is fed evenly.

Simultaneous switching outputs. When many outputs switch at once, their combined current through the inductance of the I/O power and ground bond wires produces a voltage bounce (ground bounce) that can upset other pads and the core. I/O libraries give rules of the form "at most so many outputs of a given drive strength per I/O power and ground pair". Follow the rule for your library, and put the pairs close to the outputs they serve.

Add pads for every separate supply: analog supplies, an always-on domain, a different I/O voltage. Each needs its own power pads and often its own section of the ring, separated by cells that break the buses.

Bonding and geometry constraints

  • Pad pitch must be at least the minimum the packaging process can bond, and pads should be evenly spaced.
  • Die size can be pad-limited. If the ring needs more pads than fit around a die sized for the core, the die grows to fit the pads, and the core has spare room. If the core is large and pads are few, the die is core-limited. Know which case you are in before you finalise either.
  • Bond wire length and angle limit how far the die can be from the leads and how steeply wires may run at the corners. Corner pads are often left unused for this reason.
  • Seal ring around the die edge, outside the pads, protects the circuit from cracks and moisture after the wafer is sawn. Leave the space it requires.

Where the ring meets the core

The core's power ring connects to the core power pads with short, wide straps on upper metals, ideally from every core power pad. Signal pads connect to the core's top-level pins, so the core's pin placement should mirror the pad order. Plan the two together; a core floorplanned with pins in a different order from the pads produces a tangle of long wires in the space between them.

Level shifting between core and I/O voltages usually happens inside the I/O cells. Check which side of the cell expects which voltage.

Try it yourself

  1. List the I/O cells provided in the sky130 PDK and read their documentation for signal, power, corner and filler types.
  2. Draw a pad ring for a 24-pin package by hand: signal pads, core and I/O power pairs, corners. Check the core supply pad count against your current estimate.
  3. Write the pin table as a spreadsheet or CSV, ordered around the die, and check that buses are adjacent and noisy outputs are away from the clock input.
  4. Open an open-source chip design that includes a padframe in KLayout and identify the cell types around its ring.
