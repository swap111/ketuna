Why a two-flop synchronizer works, and when it doesn't

Digital & VLSI design Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A two-flop synchronizer buys a metastable flip-flop one full clock period to settle. Here is the MTBF reasoning behind it, and the buses, pulses and fast-to-slow cases it cannot fix.

In this article

Any signal that comes from another clock domain will, sooner or later, change inside a flip-flop's setup and hold window. You cannot prevent that with timing constraints, because the two clocks have no fixed phase relationship. What you can do is give the flip-flop time to make up its mind before anyone looks at its output. The two-flop synchronizer is the cheapest way to buy that time, and knowing exactly what it buys tells you when it is not enough.

What metastability actually costs you

When D changes inside the window, the flip-flop's internal latch can land near its balance point, halfway between 0 and 1. It will fall to one side eventually, because any small noise or imbalance grows. The growth is exponential: the probability that it is still undecided after a time t falls roughly as e−t/τ, where τ (tau) is a time constant of the cell, set by its transistor gain and node capacitance.

Two things follow from that one exponential:

  • The flip-flop will almost always resolve within a small multiple of τ.
  • You never get a hard guarantee, only a probability. Each extra τ of waiting divides the failure chance by about 2.7.

So the design question is never "does it go metastable?" It is "how long do I wait before I use the value, and how rare does that make a failure?"

How the second flop buys time

In a two-flop synchronizer the first flop samples the asynchronous signal and the second flop samples the first. Nothing else is connected to the first flop's output.

The resolution time available to FF1 is the clock period minus FF1's clock-to-Q and FF2's setup time. Because there is no logic on that wire, almost the entire period counts. If FF1 settles in time, FF2 captures a clean value and the rest of the design never sees the problem. FF2's output is late by one or two cycles, which is the price you pay.

The MTBF intuition, with numbers

The usual estimate for the mean time between failures is:

MTBF = etr/τ / (Tw × fclk × fdata)

Here tr is the resolution time you allow, Tw is a cell constant describing how wide the "dangerous" window is, fclk is the destination clock and fdata is how often the input toggles. The denominator counts how often you roll the dice; the numerator is how unlikely each roll is to fail.

Take these values, made up for illustration: τ = 50 ps, Tw = 50 ps, fclk = 100 MHz (10 ns period), fdata = 1 MHz. The denominator is 50 × 10−12 × 108 × 106 = 5000 per second.

  • One flop, with logic after it, leaving tr = 1 ns: e20 ≈ 4.9 × 108, so MTBF ≈ 97,000 s, about 27 hours. A product that fails once a day is broken.
  • Two flops, leaving tr ≈ 9.5 ns: e190 divided by 5000 is around 1078 seconds, far longer than anything you will ship.

The jump comes entirely from the exponent. That is why the rule is "no logic between the two flops": every nanosecond of logic you add comes straight out of tr. It is also why designs with very fast clocks, or flops with poor τ at low supply voltage, sometimes use three stages. Real τ and Tw come from the cell library characterisation, and should be checked at the slowest corner.

When two flops are not enough

The synchronizer solves exactly one problem: a single bit that stays stable long enough to be sampled. It fails in these cases.

Multi-bit buses. If you put a two-flop synchronizer on each bit of an 8-bit value, each bit resolves independently. When the value changes from 0x7F to 0x80, all eight bits flip together, and on the critical cycle some bits may show the old value and some the new. The receiver can see 0xFF or 0x00, values that never existed. Use Gray-coded counters, a handshake or an asynchronous FIFO instead.

Short pulses. A one-cycle pulse from a 200 MHz domain lasts 5 ns. A 50 MHz destination samples every 20 ns, so it can miss the pulse completely. The synchronizer cannot catch what it never sampled. Convert the pulse to a toggle (a level that flips once per event) in the source domain, synchronize the toggle, and detect its edges in the destination.

Fast-to-slow levels that change too often. Even a level signal must stay stable for longer than one destination period plus some margin, or changes get lost. A common rule of thumb is that the source signal should be held for at least about 1.5 destination clock periods. If it changes faster than that, you need a handshake or a FIFO.

Reconvergence. Two related bits synchronized separately can arrive one cycle apart. If logic later combines them, it can briefly see an impossible combination. Synchronize one combined signal, or encode the pair so only one bit changes at a time.

Writing it so the tools see it

module sync2 #(parameter int STAGES = 2) (
  input  logic clk_dst,
  input  logic rst_n,
  input  logic d_async,
  output logic q_sync
);
  (* ASYNC_REG = "TRUE" *) logic [STAGES-1:0] sync_q;
  always_ff @(posedge clk_dst or negedge rst_n)
    if (!rst_n) sync_q <= '0;
    else        sync_q <= {sync_q[STAGES-2:0], d_async};
  assign q_sync = sync_q[STAGES-1];
endmodule

Keep synchronizers in one small, named module. CDC checking tools and reviewers can then recognise every crossing by its instance name, and placement tools can be told to keep the flops close together. The attribute shown is one common way of marking them; the exact attribute your flow honours depends on the tool, so check its documentation.

Try it yourself

  1. Write sync2 above and a testbench in Icarus Verilog (iverilog -g2012) with two unrelated clocks, for example 7 ns and 10 ns periods. Watch the two-cycle latency in GTKWave.
  2. Drive a one-cycle pulse from the fast clock into a slow-clock sync2 and count how many pulses the output misses. Then add a toggle and an edge detector and confirm none are lost.
  3. Put the MTBF formula into a short Python script and plot MTBF against tr with numpy. Work one point by hand first, then let the script check you.
