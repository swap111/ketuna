Reliability basics: failure rates, MTBF and the bathtub curve

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

What a failure rate and MTBF actually say, why an MTBF of centuries does not mean a product lasts centuries, how early failures and wear-out differ, and how derating parts keeps the failure rate low.

A reliability figure of "MTBF 6.8 million hours" sounds like a promise that the product will run for seven centuries. It is nothing of the kind, and engineers who read it that way make poor decisions about warranties, spares and maintenance. This article explains what failure rate and MTBF mean, where they apply, and where they do not, using one illustrative board as a worked example.

Failure rate and FIT

The failure rate, written λ (lambda), is the expected number of failures per unit of operating time, for a population of units. For electronic parts it is tiny, so it is usually quoted in FIT: failures per 109 device-hours. A part with λ = 20 FIT is expected to produce 20 failures if a billion device-hours are accumulated, for example one million parts running for 1,000 hours each.

Two points matter. First, λ describes a population. It says nothing about which unit fails, only how many to expect. Second, the figure is only meaningful under stated conditions: temperature, voltage stress and environment. A capacitor running hot and near its rated voltage has a higher failure rate than the same part run cool and lightly loaded.

MTBF is a rate, inverted

If the failure rate is constant, the mean time between failures is simply its inverse:

MTBF = 1 / λ

With a constant failure rate, the probability that one unit survives to time t is

R(t) = e−λt = e−t / MTBF

Put t = MTBF into that formula and you get e−1, about 0.37. Only 37 % of units are expected to survive to the MTBF; 63 % will have failed. MTBF is not a typical lifetime, not a minimum lifetime, and not even the time by which half have failed. It is the reciprocal of a rate, and its natural use is to predict how many failures a fleet will see per year.

A worked example

Here is a small sensor board with failure rates made up for illustration. Real values come from part reliability data, test results or a recognised prediction method, at your operating conditions.

Part Quantity λ each (FIT) λ total (FIT)
Microcontroller 1 20 20
Voltage regulator 1 15 15
Ceramic capacitors 40 0.5 20
Resistors 60 0.2 12
Connectors 3 10 30
Radio module 1 40 40
Crystal 1 10 10
Board 147

For a series system, where any one part failing stops the board, the failure rates add. The board's λ is 147 FIT, so

  • MTBF = 109 / 147 ≈ 6.8 million hours.
  • Probability one board survives 5 years of continuous use (43,800 hours): exp(−147 × 10−9 × 43,800) ≈ 0.9936, about 99.4 %.
  • A fleet of 2,000 boards running all year (8,760 hours each) accumulates 17.52 million device-hours, so expect about 2,000 × 8,760 × 147 × 10−9 ≈ 2.6 failures per year.

The last number is the useful one. It tells you how many spares to stock and how many field visits to plan. The 6.8 million hours says nothing about how long a board lasts, because the constant-rate assumption stops being true long before then.

import math

fit = {"mcu": 20, "reg": 15, "caps": 40 * 0.5, "res": 60 * 0.2,
       "conn": 3 * 10, "radio": 40, "xtal": 10}
lam = sum(fit.values()) * 1e-9            # failures per hour
print(f"board lambda = {sum(fit.values()):.0f} FIT")
print(f"MTBF = {1 / lam:,.0f} h")
print(f"R(5 years) = {math.exp(-lam * 5 * 8760):.4f}")
print(f"failures/year in 2,000 units = {2000 * 8760 * lam:.1f}")

The bathtub curve

Plot the failure rate of a population against age and you often get a shape like a bathtub. It has three regions, and the constant-λ maths above applies only to the middle one.

Early life (infant mortality). A falling failure rate caused by manufacturing defects: a marginal solder joint, a contaminated part, a cracked capacitor from board flexing. Screening removes these before shipping: good process control, inspection, functional test and, for some products, a period of powered burn-in at elevated temperature.

Useful life. A low, roughly constant rate of random failures. This is where FIT, MTBF and the fleet calculation above are valid.

Wear-out. A rising rate as parts with finite life reach it. Electrolytic capacitors dry out, faster when hot. Rechargeable batteries lose capacity with cycles and age. Fans, relays, switches and connectors wear mechanically. Flash memory has a limited number of erase cycles. Wear-out is not described by MTBF at all; it is handled by choosing parts whose rated life exceeds the product's design life at its real operating conditions, and by planning replacement where that is not possible.

Derating keeps λ low

Derating means operating a part well inside its ratings: a capacitor at a fraction of its rated voltage, a resistor at a fraction of its rated power, a semiconductor junction well below its maximum temperature. Failure mechanisms in electronics generally speed up with temperature and electrical stress, so a modest reduction in stress can lower the failure rate and extend wear-out life noticeably. Many organisations write a derating policy, with a fraction for each part type, and check every schematic against it at design review. It is one of the cheapest reliability measures available, because it costs a slightly larger part rather than a redesign.

Common mistakes

  • Reading MTBF as lifetime. Use it for fleet failure counts in the useful-life region; plan life from wear-out data.
  • Ignoring conditions. A failure rate quoted at 25 °C does not hold inside a hot enclosure.
  • Adding parallel parts as if they were in series. Redundant paths need a different calculation; the simple sum applies only when any failure stops the system.
  • Leaving out connectors, solder joints and cables. They are often among the larger contributors in field failures.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the Python snippet above, then add a second radio module in parallel and work out by hand why the simple sum no longer applies.
  2. Pick a board you have designed and list every part with a rated voltage, power or temperature. Compare each with its actual stress, from your schematic or an ngspice simulation, and flag any above 50 % of rating for discussion.
  3. Write down which parts on that board wear out (batteries, electrolytics, buttons, flash) and estimate their life at your operating conditions from their datasheets.
