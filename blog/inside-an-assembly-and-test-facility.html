What happens in an assembly and test facility: from wafer to packaged, tested part

India's electronics ecosystem Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

The steps a finished wafer goes through to become a packaged, tested chip, in order, from wafer probing and dicing to die attach, bonding, moulding, final test and burn-in, and what each step guards against.

When a foundry finishes a wafer, it holds hundreds or thousands of chips, but none of them is usable yet. Each die is a fragile sliver of silicon with microscopic pads, no protection and no way to be soldered to a board. Some of them do not work. An assembly and test facility turns that wafer into packaged parts on a reel, and makes sure that only good parts leave. Designers rarely see this stage, yet it shapes pad layout, test logic, power budgets and cost. This article walks through the steps in order.

The flow at a glance

Wafer probe: test before you package

The first step is wafer probe, also called wafer sort. The wafer sits on a precision stage called a prober, and a probe card, a board with fine needles or micro-springs aligned to the die's pads, touches one die (or several at once) at a time. Automatic test equipment (ATE) then powers the die and runs a test program: shorts and opens on each pin, supply currents, digital tests using scan chains and built-in self-test, and basic analog measurements.

The result is a wafer map, a record of which dies passed and which failed, often sorted into bins by failure type. Bad dies are simply not assembled. Since packaging costs money, catching bad dies here saves it. The map also feeds back to the foundry and the design team: a cluster of failures at the wafer edge suggests a process problem, while the same failing test on every die suggests a design or test bug.

This is where design-for-test pays off. Scan chains, memory built-in self-test and a test access port let a few probe needles exercise a whole chip in seconds.

Thinning and dicing: separating the dies

Wafers are thicker than a package can hold, so the back is ground down, called backgrinding, to the thickness the package needs. Then the wafer is mounted on a tape in a frame and cut along the scribe lines between dies, usually with a thin diamond saw or a laser, called dicing. The tape holds the separated dies in place for the next step.

Dicing stresses the silicon edges. Designers protect the die with a seal ring around its edge and must keep active circuits a safe distance from the scribe line.

Die attach and bonding: connecting to the package

A pick-and-place machine lifts each good die, using the wafer map to skip bad ones, and places it on a package substrate or lead frame with an adhesive or solder, called die attach. The attach layer also carries heat away from the die, so its quality affects thermal performance.

Then the die's pads are connected to the package:

  • Wire bonding uses very fine gold or copper wire, welded by heat, pressure and ultrasound, one wire per pad. It is mature, flexible and inexpensive, but each wire adds inductance, which matters for fast signals and noisy supplies.
  • Flip-chip puts solder or copper bumps on the die's pads, turns the die face down and solders it directly to the substrate. Connections are shorter and can cover the whole die area, at higher cost.

Pad placement, pad pitch and the bond diagram are decided during chip design, which is why the design team and the assembly house must agree on them before tape-out.

Moulding, marking and finishing

The die and wires are encapsulated in an epoxy moulding compound that protects them from moisture, light and mechanical damage. Each part is then marked with a part number and lot code for traceability. Depending on the package, leads are plated, trimmed and formed, or solder balls are attached, and individual packages are separated from the strip.

Final test and burn-in

Packaging can break things: a wire can short, the die can crack and moulding stress can shift analog behaviour. So every packaged part goes through final test, on ATE with a handler that feeds parts into a test socket, often at more than one temperature. Final test checks function and the datasheet parameters that matter, and sorts parts into bins, for example by speed grade.

Some products also go through burn-in: parts run at raised temperature and voltage for a period to make weak parts fail in the factory rather than in the customer's product. Early-life failures are more common than failures in the middle of a part's life, so burn-in removes the weakest parts. It costs time and equipment, so it is used where reliability demands it, such as automotive or infrastructure parts.

Passing parts are dried if needed, packed in trays or on tape and reel in moisture-barrier bags, labelled with their lot and shipped.

Common mistakes designers make

  • Leaving test access as an afterthought, so probe and final test cannot reach internal logic
  • Placing pads without checking the package's bonding rules
  • Ignoring supply bond-wire inductance in noisy or fast designs
  • Not having the test program and probe card ready when wafers arrive

Try it yourself

  1. Add a scan chain to a small design by hand, a multiplexer in front of each flip-flop selected by a scan-enable input, and write a test in Icarus Verilog that shifts a pattern through it.
  2. Open a finished sky130 design in KLayout and find the seal ring, pad ring and scribe area.
  3. Draw a bond diagram for a small design: which pad goes to which package pin, keeping supply pins close to the pads they feed.
