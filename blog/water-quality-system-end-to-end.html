Designing a water quality monitoring system end to end

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

A water quality monitor is sensors, calibration, enclosure, power, connectivity and maintenance working as one system. How to write requirements that tie them together, with a pH calibration worked through in code.

A water quality monitor looks like a sensor problem: put a pH probe and a conductivity cell in a tank, read them with an ADC, send the numbers somewhere. Teams that treat it that way usually get good data for a few weeks and then a slow slide into nonsense, as probes drift, foul and dry out, the enclosure fills with condensation and nobody visits the site. The sensors are the easy part. The system is the sensors plus calibration, housing, power, connectivity and the people who maintain it, and the requirements have to cover all of them together.

This article is about the engineering of a monitoring system. A monitor like this does not certify water as safe to drink; that requires accredited laboratory testing against the relevant Indian Standard for drinking water.

Start from what the data is for

Begin with the user and the decision the data supports. A village water committee checking a treatment plant, a fish farmer watching dissolved oxygen at night, and an agency tracking river pollution need different parameters, accuracy, sampling rates and alerting. Write the decision down first, because it sets everything else:

  • Parameters: commonly pH, electrical conductivity (often converted to an estimate of total dissolved solids), turbidity, temperature, and sometimes dissolved oxygen, residual chlorine or specific ions.
  • Accuracy and range for each, tied to the decision. An alert threshold is only useful if the measurement error is small compared with the gap between normal and abnormal.
  • Sampling interval and latency: hourly logging for trends, or minutes for alerts.
  • Site: inline in a pipe, dipped in a tank, or floating in a pond or river.

Calibration is a requirement, not a lab step

Most water sensors are not absolute. A pH electrode produces a voltage that depends on pH, temperature and the electrode's own condition, and that relationship changes as the electrode ages. Calibration measures known reference solutions and computes the conversion.

An ideal pH electrode produces about 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C, with roughly 0 mV at pH 7. Real electrodes are less steep, and the slope falls as they age. A two-point calibration with buffer solutions finds the actual offset and slope:

NERNST_25C = 59.16      # mV per pH unit, ideal, at 25 degC

def calibrate(mv_at_7, mv_at_4, ph_low=4.01, ph_mid=7.00):
    slope = (mv_at_4 - mv_at_7) / (ph_low - ph_mid)   # mV per pH
    efficiency = abs(slope) / NERNST_25C * 100
    return slope, efficiency

def to_ph(mv, mv_at_7, slope, ph_mid=7.00):
    return ph_mid + (mv - mv_at_7) / slope

slope, eff = calibrate(mv_at_7=3.0, mv_at_4=172.0)
print(f"slope {slope:.2f} mV/pH, efficiency {eff:.1f} %")
print(f"sample at -60 mV -> pH {to_ph(-60.0, 3.0, slope):.2f}")

With these illustrative readings, the slope is −56.52 mV/pH, about 95.5 % of ideal, and a sample reading −60 mV converts to pH 8.11. The efficiency is also a health check: as an electrode ages, its slope falls, and below a threshold the firmware should flag it for replacement. Both calibrations here assume 25 °C; a real system measures temperature and corrects the slope, because it scales with absolute temperature.

Conductivity has its own correction. It rises with temperature, so instruments usually report it referred to 25 °C using a temperature coefficient; about 2 % per °C is a common default for natural water, but the right value depends on the water, so make it configurable.

Calibration then becomes a set of requirements:

ID Requirement
CAL-1 The system shall support two-point pH calibration in the field with standard buffers, completed by one person in under 15 minutes
CAL-2 The system shall store calibration date, offset and slope, and report them with every data upload
CAL-3 The system shall flag the pH channel as suspect when slope efficiency falls below 90 % or calibration is older than 30 days

The numbers are illustrative. The pattern is what matters: calibration is designed, recorded and checked, not left to whoever visits.

Fouling, drift and maintenance

In real water, sensors foul. Algae, biofilm, silt and mineral scale coat the sensing surfaces, and the readings drift without any obvious failure. Design responses include mechanical wipers on optical sensors, anti-fouling materials, mounting that lets sediment fall away, and an easy way to remove and clean the probe. The firmware can help by watching for signatures of fouling, such as a turbidity reading that only ever increases, or a pH signal whose noise disappears.

Maintenance must be planned as part of the system, with requirements for its interval and its difficulty. Ask who will visit, how often, with what skills and tools, and write requirements such as "all routine maintenance shall be possible without tools other than a screwdriver, in under 20 minutes". A system that needs an engineer every two weeks at a remote pond will not be maintained.

Enclosure, power and connectivity

The electronics live beside water, often outdoors. The enclosure needs a suitable ingress protection rating, vents that equalise pressure without letting water in, and sealed cable glands for probe cables. Keep probe connectors above the waterline and drip-looped. Condensation inside the box is a common failure; see the discussion of dew point in designing for Indian conditions.

Power usually comes from a battery, often with a small solar panel. Budget energy from the sampling plan: each measurement cycle includes sensor warm-up, which for some sensors is the dominant energy cost. Size the panel and battery for the worst season, not the average day.

Connectivity follows the site: a cellular modem where coverage exists, a low-power long-range radio to a gateway where it does not, or local logging with periodic collection. Requirements should cover what happens when the link fails: store a set number of days of readings locally and upload them when it returns, with timestamps that survive.

Tie it together with system requirements

The value of thinking end to end is that requirements constrain each other. A 15-minute sampling interval drives sensor warm-up energy, which drives battery size, which drives enclosure size. A 30-day calibration interval drives how often someone visits, which drives the cost of running the system. Review them together, and trace each one back to the user's decision.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the probe as permanent. Electrodes age; plan for replacement and detect when it is due.
  • No temperature compensation. Both pH and conductivity depend on temperature.
  • Sending numbers without calibration metadata. Data with no record of when the sensor was calibrated cannot be trusted later.
  • Ignoring the maintainer. If routine care is hard, it will not happen.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the calibration script with readings from a real pH probe and buffer solutions, and compute your electrode's slope efficiency.
  2. Add temperature correction: scale the ideal slope with absolute temperature and compare results at 15 °C and 35 °C.
  3. Write a requirements table for a monitor at a site you know, covering parameters, calibration, maintenance, power and connectivity, and trace each requirement to the decision the data supports.
