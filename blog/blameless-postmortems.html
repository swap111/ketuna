Writing a blameless postmortem after a failed lab or project

Careers & skills Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

How to write a postmortem after a project goes wrong: a factual timeline, contributing causes found with "why" questions, what went well, and concrete actions with owners, all without blaming people.

In this article

  2. The parts of a postmortem
  3. Finding contributing causes
  4. What went well is not filler
  5. Actions that actually change something
  6. Common mistakes
  7. Try it yourself

Projects fail. A capstone demo does not boot, a board comes back with a swapped connector, a tape-out precheck fails the night before the deadline. What separates a team that improves from one that repeats the failure is what happens in the week after. A postmortem is a short written analysis of what happened and what will change, and a blameless one describes systems and decisions rather than naming culprits, because that is the only way to get the honest facts it needs.

Why blame destroys the evidence

Suppose a team's FPGA demo failed because nobody re-ran the full regression after a late change to the UART baud divider. The easy story is "a teammate pushed an untested change". It is quick, it feels satisfying, and it teaches the team nothing except to hide mistakes next time.

The useful questions are different. Why was it possible to push without the regression? Why did the change happen the night before? Why did nobody notice the baud rate in the demo rehearsal? Each answer points at something the team can change: a CI check, a freeze date, a rehearsal checklist. Everyone makes mistakes; good processes catch them before they matter. If the write-up names a person, everyone involved becomes defensive and starts leaving facts out, and a postmortem without facts is just an argument on paper.

Blameless does not mean consequence-free or vague. It means the analysis assumes everyone acted reasonably given what they knew at the time, and then asks why what they knew was not enough.

The parts of a postmortem

Keep it to one or two pages. The structure below follows the postmortem template used in the program's capstone, and it works for a two-week lab as well as a long project.

Section What goes in it
Summary One line: what happened and its impact
Timeline Dated events and the decisions made at each point
What went well Things to keep doing
What went badly Facts, not feelings
Contributing causes Found by repeatedly asking "why"
Estimates vs actuals Planned against real dates, with the gap explained
Actions Specific change, owner, due date, what it prevents

Write the timeline first, from evidence: commit history, CI logs, chat messages, the issue tracker. Memory reorders events and fills gaps with guesses; logs do not. Each row should record not just what happened but what the team decided, because decisions are where the process can improve.

Finding contributing causes

The common tool is five whys: start with the problem and ask why it happened, then ask why about the answer, and continue until you reach something the team controls. Five is a rough number, not a rule; stop when the next answer is outside your control or is a person's name. Here is an illustrative example from a made-up capstone:

Problem: the demo board printed garbage on the UART.

1. Why? The firmware used a 9600 baud divider; the
   host terminal was set to 115200.
2. Why? A late commit changed the divider while
   testing a slower sensor link, and it was merged.
3. Why was it merged? The PR was approved without the
   hardware-in-the-loop test, which only ran manually.
4. Why only manually? The test needed the board, and
   nobody had set up a scripted run.
5. Why not? It was never on the plan; we assumed the
   simulation regression was enough.

Contributing causes:
- No automated hardware test before merge
- No code freeze before the demo
- Demo rehearsal did not check the console output

Notice that there is rarely one root cause. Real failures come from several small gaps lining up. Listing them as contributing causes is more honest than forcing a single "root cause", and it gives you more places to put a fix.

What went well is not filler

Teams skip this section because it feels like a consolation prize. It is evidence too. If the team recovered in two hours because one member had written a clear README for flashing the board, that practice should be kept on purpose, not by luck. Writing it down also keeps the document balanced, which makes people more willing to be candid in the "went badly" section.

Actions that actually change something

An action is only useful if someone could check, later, whether it was done. Compare:

Weak action Useful action
Be more careful before demos Code freeze 48 hours before any demo; only fixes for logged blockers after it
Test more Add the UART loopback test to CI so a wrong baud divider fails the PR
Communicate better Rehearsal checklist includes "console output read aloud"; owner signs it

Every action needs an owner and a date, and the list should be short, two to five items. A list of fifteen actions is a list of none, because nobody will do them all. Prefer actions that change the system (a check, a template, a gate) over actions that ask people to try harder, since effort fades and a CI job does not.

Finally, revisit the actions at the next team meeting or review. A postmortem whose actions are never checked becomes a ritual, and people notice.

Common mistakes

  • Writing it weeks later. Details fade within days; write the timeline while logs and memory are fresh.
  • Names in the causes. Replace "X forgot" with the gap that let it happen: "the release step was not on a checklist".
  • Stopping at the first why. "The baud rate was wrong" is a symptom; keep asking.
  • Only the leader writes it. Ask each member for one sentence on what they would carry to the next team; different people saw different parts.
  • No estimates versus actuals. The gap between planned and real dates is often where the deepest lesson is.

Try it yourself

  1. Take a lab or project that went worse than planned. Rebuild its timeline from git log --date=short --format='%ad %s' and any CI history, before you write a word of analysis.
  2. Run five whys on the biggest problem with a teammate. Each time an answer names a person, rewrite it as the process gap behind it.
  3. Write two to four actions, each with an owner, a date and the failure it prevents. If one of them can be a test, write that test in your regression flow (for example a cocotb test or a make check target) and confirm it fails on the old code.
  4. Put the postmortem in the project repository next to the README, and check the actions at your next review.
