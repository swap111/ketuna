Decoupling capacitors: why, how many and where to place them

Systems & product engineering Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Chips draw current in short bursts that a distant regulator cannot supply. Local capacitors provide that charge, and a short calculation shows why their placement and loop area matter as much as their value.

  3. Where to place them

Every schematic you have seen has small capacitors sprinkled next to the chips, usually labelled 100 nF, often with no explanation. They are decoupling capacitors, and a board can misbehave in strange ways without them: a microcontroller that resets when a radio transmits, an ADC whose readings jump, a clock with jitter. Understanding why they are there, rather than copying them, tells you how many to use and where they must go.

Why chips need local charge

Digital logic does not draw a smooth current. Every clock edge switches thousands of transistors at once, and for a few nanoseconds the chip draws a burst of current far above its average. The regulator that supplies the rail is too far away and too slow to respond in that time. It sits at the end of a track, and every track has inductance.

Inductance resists changes in current. The voltage it drops is

V = L × di/dt

so a fast change in current (large di/dt) through even a small inductance produces a voltage dip at the chip's supply pin. If the dip is large enough, logic thresholds shift, the ADC's reference moves or the brown-out detector trips.

A decoupling capacitor is a small local reservoir of charge, placed so close to the supply pin that the current burst comes from it instead of from the distant regulator. Between bursts, the regulator quietly recharges it.

Sizing: a worked example

The numbers below are made up for illustration. Suppose a chip draws an extra 50 mA for 5 ns on each clock edge, and you want its supply to dip by no more than 50 mV.

Charge. The capacitor must supply Q = I × t, and its voltage falls by ΔV = Q / C. Rearranging:

C ≥ I × t / ΔV = 0.05 A × 5 ns / 0.05 V = 5 nF

A 100 nF capacitor is twenty times that. Charge alone, it would dip by only 0.05 × 5 × 10−9 / 100 × 10−9 = 2.5 mV. So why does the board still misbehave sometimes? Because capacitance is not the limiting factor.

Inductance. The current must flow from the capacitor, along a track to the supply pin, through the chip, out of its ground pin and back to the capacitor. That loop has inductance, roughly proportional to its area. Suppose the loop has 2 nH and the current rises by 50 mA in 1 ns:

V = 2 nH × 50 mA / 1 ns = 0.1 V

That is a 100 mV dip, twice the budget, from a capacitor that has plenty of charge. The only way to reduce it is to reduce the inductance: a shorter, smaller loop. Placement matters more than value.

Where to place them

Practical rules that follow from the loop:

  • One small capacitor per supply pin, or per pair of adjacent power and ground pins, placed as close as the layout allows.
  • Short, wide connections. Connect each capacitor pad to the power and ground planes with a via right at the pad, ideally one via per pad. A thin track of several millimetres between the capacitor and the via can undo the benefit.
  • Put the capacitor between the plane connection and the pin, or right at the pin, so the current path is direct.
  • Use a solid ground plane on a layer next to the components. The return current then flows directly under the outgoing path, which keeps the loop small.
  • On the same side as the chip if possible; if not, directly underneath on the other side with short vias.

How many and which values

A capacitor is not ideal. It has its own inductance (from its package and connections) and resistance. Together with its capacitance, that makes it resonate at a frequency f = 1 / (2π√(LC)); above that frequency it behaves like an inductor. A 100 nF capacitor with 1 nH of total mounted inductance resonates near 16 MHz; a 1 µF one with the same inductance, near 5 MHz. Smaller packages have lower inductance, which is one reason small ceramic capacitors are preferred for decoupling.

A sensible scheme for a typical microcontroller board is:

  • a 100 nF ceramic capacitor at each supply pin, in a small package
  • one or two capacitors of a few microfarads near the chip, for slower, larger demands
  • bulk capacitance at the regulator output, sized from its datasheet for stability
  • whatever the chip's datasheet asks for on analogue, PLL or core supplies; follow it exactly

Ceramic capacitors lose a large part of their capacitance under DC bias, especially in small packages with high values. Check the manufacturer's bias curve for the part you choose, rather than trusting the number printed on the reel.

Common mistakes

  • Placing all decoupling capacitors in one tidy row away from the chip, for a neat layout. Neatness does not supply current.
  • Long thin tracks to a distant via. They add inductance that no capacitor value can overcome.
  • Assuming more capacitors always helps. Without good placement, extra parts add cost but little benefit.
  • Ignoring datasheet requirements for specific pins, such as an internal regulator's output capacitor.

Try it yourself

  1. Repeat the two calculations above for a chip on a board you know, using its datasheet's supply current and your own guesses for the burst duration and loop inductance.
  2. In ngspice, model a current pulse load fed through 2 nH from a 100 nF capacitor and a distant regulator, and compare the dip with 0.5 nH.
  3. Open a KiCad layout and, for each chip, measure the track length from each decoupling capacitor to the nearest supply pin and plane via. Move any that are more than a few millimetres away.
