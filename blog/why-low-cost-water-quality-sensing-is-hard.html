Why low-cost water quality sensing matters, and what makes it hard

India's electronics ecosystem Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Why continuous water quality monitoring is useful, and why sensor drift, fouling, calibration and maintenance, not the electronics, are the real obstacles, with a simulation of drift and recalibration.

Clean water is a health issue, and knowing what is in the water is the first step to keeping it clean. Testing usually means taking a sample to a lab or using a test kit by hand, which gives an accurate picture of one moment at one place. Continuous, low-cost sensors could watch tanks, wells, pipelines and rivers all the time and raise an alarm when something changes. The electronics for this are not the hard part. The hard part is the sensor that sits in the water, because water slowly changes it. This article explains why.

What is commonly measured

A few parameters are popular for continuous monitoring because electronic sensors exist for them:

Parameter What it tells you Common sensing principle
pH Acidity or alkalinity Glass electrode producing a small voltage
Conductivity or dissolved solids How much dissolved salt and mineral is present AC current between electrodes
Turbidity Cloudiness from suspended particles Light scattered by the particles
Residual chlorine Whether disinfection is still effective Electrochemical cell
Dissolved oxygen Health of rivers and ponds Electrochemical or optical sensor
Temperature Needed to correct most other readings Thermistor or digital sensor

Notice what is missing: most bacteria, many metals and many chemical contaminants cannot be measured reliably by a cheap continuous sensor. A low-cost node can tell you that something has changed, which is valuable, but it is not a replacement for laboratory testing against the national drinking water standard. Be clear about that in anything you build or claim.

The electronics are the easy part

The readout circuits are well understood. A pH electrode produces roughly 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C, from a source with very high internal resistance, so it needs an amplifier with extremely low input bias current and careful guarding on the board. A conductivity cell is driven with alternating current to avoid polarising the electrodes. A turbidity sensor needs an LED, a photodiode and a way to reject ambient light. Each is a solid but standard analog design exercise, and a microcontroller with a decent ADC handles the rest.

What these circuits cannot fix is a sensor whose response has changed.

Drift, fouling and why readings wander

Sensors in water change over time in several ways:

  • Drift: the sensor's offset and sensitivity change slowly as it ages. A pH electrode's reference junction gets contaminated, and its glass membrane responds more sluggishly.
  • Fouling: a film of bacteria and algae, called biofilm, grows on any surface in water, along with mineral scale and sediment. A fouled turbidity window reads cloudy when the water is clear; a fouled conductivity cell reads low.
  • Damage and exposure: electrodes dry out if a tank empties, membranes crack and cables are chewed by animals.
  • Temperature effects: almost every reading depends on temperature, which changes through the day.

The insidious part is that a drifting sensor still produces smooth, plausible numbers. Nothing in the data looks broken. Only a comparison against a known reference reveals the error.

A simulation of drift and recalibration

The script below models a pH electrode whose offset and slope drift with age. All drift rates are made up for illustration. The water's true pH stays at 7.8. One estimate uses only the factory calibration; the other recalibrates with pH 4 and pH 7 buffer solutions every 14 days.

import numpy as np

days = np.arange(0, 61)
offset = 0.5 * days                  # mV of offset drift per day, made up
slope = -59.16 * (1 - 0.002 * days)  # mV/pH, ageing electrode, made up
true_ph = 7.8                        # the water does not change

mv = offset + slope * (true_ph - 7.0)          # electrode output
naive = 7.0 + mv / -59.16                      # factory calibration only

def calibrate(day):                  # two-point calibration with pH 4 and 7
    m7 = offset[day]
    m4 = offset[day] + slope[day] * (4.0 - 7.0)
    return m7, (m7 - m4) / 3.0       # offset and slope seen that day

recal = np.empty_like(naive)
for d in days:
    m7, s = calibrate(d - d % 14)    # recalibrate every 14 days
    recal[d] = 7.0 + (mv[d] - m7) / s

for d in (0, 13, 30, 60):
    print(d, round(naive[d], 2), round(recal[d], 2))

The output, day by day:

Day Factory calibration only Recalibrated every 14 days
0 7.80 7.80
13 7.67 7.67
30 7.50 7.78
60 7.20 7.75

Without recalibration the reading slides steadily away from the truth, and nothing in the data shows it. With periodic recalibration the error stays bounded, but only if someone actually visits with buffer solutions. That visit is the real cost of the system.

Designing for calibration and maintenance

Since maintenance cannot be avoided, the design should make it easy and make its absence visible:

  • Make sensors easy to reach and swap, with connectors rather than soldered cables, so a fouled probe can be replaced in the field
  • Measure temperature alongside everything and correct for it in firmware
  • Use redundancy and cross-checks, such as two sensors of the same kind, or physically linked parameters that should move together
  • Detect fouling where possible, for example by watching for slow, steady trends that real water rarely shows, or a slower sensor response
  • Reduce fouling with wipers, flow-through cells kept away from light, or measuring only when a sample is drawn
  • Record calibration history on the device, so every reading can be traced to the calibration that produced it
  • Report sensor age and time since calibration with the data, so users know how much to trust it

These choices cost more than the electronics, and they decide whether the system is still telling the truth after three months.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script, then change the recalibration interval to 7 and 30 days and plot the error with matplotlib.
  2. Build a pH amplifier around a low-bias op-amp model in ngspice, include a source resistance in the hundreds of megaohms, and see how input bias current shifts the reading.
  3. Leave a cheap conductivity or turbidity probe in a bucket of tap water for two weeks, log its readings with a microcontroller, and photograph the probe at the end.
