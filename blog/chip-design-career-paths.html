Career paths in chip design: what each role does day to day

Careers & skills Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Front-end design, verification, physical design, DFT, analog, CAD and post-silicon validation, described by the work each role actually does in a normal week, the tools it lives in and the skills it rewards.

Job titles in chip design are short and often confusing: "DV engineer", "PD engineer", "DFT", "CAD". Students pick a direction from the title, then discover that the daily work is very different from what they imagined. A better way to choose is to ask what each role does on an ordinary Tuesday: what is open on the screen, what question is being answered, and what counts as a good day.

One chip, many hands

Every digital or mixed-signal chip moves through the same broad stages: a specification, a register-transfer level (RTL) description, verification, synthesis to gates, physical layout, signoff checks, fabrication and then testing of real silicon. Each role owns one or two of these stages and hands its output to the next. Knowing where a role sits in this chain tells you what it receives, what it produces and who complains when it goes wrong.

The digital flow, role by role

Front-end (RTL) design. You turn a block specification into synthesizable Verilog or SystemVerilog: state machines, datapaths, register interfaces, clock and reset handling. A typical day mixes writing RTL, reading lint and clock-domain-crossing reports, answering questions from verification about intended behaviour, and updating the spec when something was ambiguous. Good front-end engineers think about timing and area while they write, not after synthesis complains.

Design verification (DV). You prove the RTL does what the spec says before it is too late to change. The work is writing a verification plan, building testbenches with stimulus, checkers and coverage, running regressions overnight and debugging failures in the morning. Most of the day is debugging: is this a design bug, a testbench bug or a spec misunderstanding? Verification is often the larger share of a project's effort, because showing that logic is correct in every situation is harder than writing it.

Design for test (DFT). A fabricated chip must be testable in seconds on a tester. DFT engineers insert scan chains, memory built-in self-test and test compression, generate test patterns and check how many manufacturing faults those patterns can detect. The daily work is reading coverage reports, fixing logic that cannot be scanned, and coordinating with physical design because scan chains add wires.

Physical design (PD). You take the gate-level netlist and produce a layout that meets timing, power and area: floorplanning, placement, clock tree synthesis, routing, then fixing static timing violations, design-rule violations and IR drop. A typical day is a loop of running a flow step, reading a timing or congestion report, changing constraints or placement, and running again. Scripting matters here, because flows are long and must be repeatable.

Analog, CAD and validation

Analog and mixed-signal design. Analog engineers design the circuits that do not reduce to gates: amplifiers, data converters, voltage references, regulators, phase-locked loops and input/output circuits. The day is spent in a schematic editor and a circuit simulator, sweeping process, voltage and temperature corners, then working with layout engineers on matching and parasitics. Progress is slower and more mathematical than in digital work, and intuition built from many simulations is the main asset.

CAD and flow engineering. CAD engineers keep everyone else productive. They build and maintain the scripts that run synthesis, layout and signoff, set up regression infrastructure, debug tool issues and write utilities that parse reports. If you enjoy both programming and hardware, this role uses both every day. Python, Tcl and shell are the working languages.

Post-silicon validation. When chips return from the foundry, validation engineers bring them up on boards, run tests across voltage and temperature, and find the bugs that escaped pre-silicon verification. The day involves lab equipment, firmware or test scripts, and careful debugging of a system you cannot look inside. It rewards patience and a methodical habit of changing one thing at a time.

What each role rewards

Role Core skill Main tools (open-source examples)
Front-end design Clean RTL, timing awareness Verilator lint, Yosys
Verification Debugging, test planning Icarus Verilog, cocotb, SymbiYosys
DFT Fault models, test logic Yosys netlists, scripting
Physical design Timing closure, scripting OpenROAD, OpenSTA, KLayout
Analog Circuit theory, simulation Xschem, ngspice, Magic
CAD Programming, flow design Python, Tcl, LibreLane
Validation Lab debugging, firmware GCC, Python, oscilloscopes

The open-source tools are not identical to what industry teams use, but the concepts and reports transfer. A slack value in OpenSTA means the same thing as a slack value anywhere else.

Common mistakes when choosing

  • Choosing by perceived prestige. Every stage can stop a chip. Pick the work you can happily do for hours, because that is what you will be doing.
  • Assuming verification is a lesser role. It is a full discipline with its own depth, and it needs strong design knowledge to do well.
  • Ignoring scripting. Every role above automates. An engineer who can write a small parser saves a team hours each week.
  • Deciding from a course alone. A semester of theory does not show you the daily loop. A small project in each area does.

Try it yourself

  1. Write a tiny block, such as an 8-bit counter with enable, and lint it with verilator --lint-only. That is a front-end task.
  2. Write a self-checking testbench for it with Icarus Verilog or cocotb, and plant a bug to confirm the test fails. That is verification.
  3. Synthesise it with Yosys (synth -top <name>, then stat) and run it through a LibreLane flow on sky130 to see a layout in KLayout. That is physical design in miniature.
  4. Note which of the three steps you enjoyed debugging most. That reaction is better evidence for a career choice than any job title.
