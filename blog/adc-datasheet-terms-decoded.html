ADC datasheet terms decoded: ENOB, INL, DNL and SNR

Analog & mixed-signal Beginner 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

An ADC's bit count says little on its own. Learn what SNR, SINAD, ENOB, THD, SFDR, DNL and INL each measure, how they relate, and which ones matter for audio, sensing or control.

In this article

  2. The ideal staircase and its errors
  3. Computing DNL and INL from measurements

A converter advertised as "12-bit" produces 12-bit numbers, but that does not mean all 12 bits carry information. Noise, distortion and uneven steps eat into the useful resolution, and an ADC datasheet describes each of those losses with its own specification. Once you know what each term measures, you can pick a converter for your application, or judge your own design, without being misled by the bit count.

Two families of specifications

ADC specifications fall into two groups, measured in different ways.

  • Static (DC) specifications describe the staircase that maps input voltage to output code: offset, gain error, DNL and INL. They are measured with slow inputs, often a slow ramp or a histogram of codes.
  • Dynamic (AC) specifications describe how faithfully the converter digitises a changing signal: SNR, THD, SINAD, ENOB and SFDR. They are measured by feeding a pure sine wave, capturing thousands of samples and taking an FFT.

A precision weighing scale lives or dies by the first group. An audio or vibration recorder lives or dies by the second.

The ideal staircase and its errors

An ideal N-bit ADC with full-scale range VFS divides the range into 2N equal steps. The step size is one LSB (least significant bit):

1 LSB = VFS / 2N

For a 12-bit converter on 3.3 V, that is 3.3 / 4096 ≈ 0.806 mV. A real converter's staircase deviates from the ideal in four ways:

  • Offset error: the whole staircase is shifted left or right. Easy to calibrate away by measuring a known zero input.
  • Gain error: the staircase is stretched or squeezed, so the last transition is in the wrong place. Also easy to calibrate with a known full-scale input.
  • DNL (differential non-linearity): individual steps are wider or narrower than 1 LSB. DNL for a code is its actual width divided by the ideal width, minus 1. A code of width zero has DNL = −1 LSB and is a missing code: no input voltage ever produces it.
  • INL (integral non-linearity): after removing offset and gain error, how far each transition sits from a straight line. INL is the running sum of the DNL errors, so small step errors that lean the same way accumulate into a bowed curve.

Offset and gain can be trimmed with two calibration points. DNL and INL cannot, because they vary along the range. That makes them the real limit for accurate DC measurement.

Computing DNL and INL from measurements

The test is simple in principle: sweep the input slowly and record the voltage at which each code transition happens. This short script computes both errors for a 3-bit converter with made-up transition voltages:

# Input voltages (V) at which a 3-bit ADC steps to the next code:
# T[0] is the 0->1 transition, T[6] the 6->7. Made up for illustration.
T = [0.130, 0.360, 0.640, 0.800, 1.120, 1.400, 1.600]

lsb = (T[-1] - T[0]) / (len(T) - 1)  # end-point fit: average step
print(f"average step = {lsb*1e3:.0f} mV")

for k in range(1, len(T)):           # codes 1..6 have a width
    width = T[k] - T[k - 1]
    print(f"code {k}: width {width*1e3:4.0f} mV  "
          f"DNL {width/lsb - 1:+.2f} LSB")

for k, t in enumerate(T):            # deviation from the straight line
    inl = (t - (T[0] + k * lsb)) / lsb
    print(f"transition {k}->{k+1}: INL {inl:+.2f} LSB")

The average step is 245 mV. Code 3 is only 160 mV wide (DNL −0.35 LSB) and code 4 is 320 mV wide (DNL +0.31 LSB). The 3→4 transition sits 0.27 LSB early, the worst INL. Using the first and last transitions to define the straight line, called an end-point fit, removes offset and gain error automatically; some datasheets use a best-fit line instead, which gives smaller INL numbers for the same converter, so check which one is quoted.

A guarantee of "no missing codes" means DNL stays above −1 LSB everywhere. It matters in control loops: if a code is missing, a feedback loop trying to hold the input there will hunt between neighbours.

Noise, distortion and the effective number of bits

Feed a full-scale sine wave and take an FFT of the output. You see one tall spike at the input frequency, smaller spikes at its harmonics (distortion), and a floor of noise.

  • SNR (signal-to-noise ratio): signal power over noise power, harmonics excluded. Even a perfect converter has quantization noise, because rounding to the nearest step adds an error spread uniformly over ±½ LSB. For a full-scale sine this gives the ideal limit SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB, about 74.0 dB for 12 bits and 98.1 dB for 16 bits.
  • THD (total harmonic distortion): power in the harmonics relative to the signal. It comes from the curvature of the transfer function, so INL and THD are two views of the same non-linearity.
  • SINAD (signal to noise and distortion): signal over everything else. Always the worst of the three.
  • ENOB (effective number of bits): SINAD turned back into bits by inverting the ideal formula:

ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) / 6.02

A 16-bit converter with a measured SINAD of 86 dB has ENOB = (86 − 1.76) / 6.02 ≈ 14.0 bits. The bottom two bits are, on average, noise and distortion. These numbers are illustrative; real values depend on the part, the input frequency and the board.

  • SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range): the gap between the signal and the single tallest unwanted spike, whether harmonic or not. It matters when you look for a weak tone next to a strong one, as in radio receivers and vibration analysis.

Dynamic specifications change with conditions. ENOB usually falls as input frequency rises, because sampling-clock jitter and the front end's bandwidth start to dominate. A datasheet that quotes ENOB at a low input frequency tells you little about performance at a high one.

Which specification matters for your application

Application Specifications to read first
DC measurement: temperature, weighing, battery voltage INL, offset and gain drift, noise in LSB rms
Control loops: motor current, regulators DNL (no missing codes), conversion latency
Audio and vibration SNR, THD, SINAD or ENOB at the relevant input frequencies
Radio and wideband signals SFDR, ENOB at high input frequency, jitter sensitivity

Common mistakes:

  • Choosing by bit count alone and then discovering that ENOB is two or three bits lower
  • Comparing INL figures measured with different fitting methods
  • Reading ENOB at a 1 kHz test tone and applying it at 1 MHz
  • Forgetting that a noisy reference or poor layout lowers the ENOB you get on your own board

Try it yourself

  1. Run the DNL/INL script above, then change one transition so that a code width becomes zero, and confirm DNL reaches −1 LSB.
  2. With numpy, generate a sine wave, quantize it to 10 bits with np.round, take an FFT with a window, and compute SNR. Compare with 6.02 × 10 + 1.76 ≈ 62 dB.
  3. Add a small cubic term to the sine before quantizing and watch harmonics appear. Measure THD and SINAD, and convert SINAD to ENOB.
