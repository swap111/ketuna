Choosing an ADC architecture for a multi-channel sensor-fusion array

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

When several sensors must be combined, when each channel is sampled matters as much as how well. Compare multiplexed and simultaneous sampling, resolution against speed, and area and power per channel.

Sensor fusion combines several measurements into one estimate: microphones in an array to find where a sound came from, current and voltage to compute power, accelerometer and gyroscope axes to track orientation, a row of strain gauges to locate a load. Each channel needs a converter, and the obvious question is resolution and speed. The less obvious question, and often the deciding one, is timing: whether all channels describe the same instant. This article works through the architecture choice for a multi-channel array, from the timing requirement down to area and power per channel.

Start from what the fusion algorithm needs

Before comparing converters, write down three requirements for the combined system, not for one channel.

  1. Bandwidth and resolution per channel. The usual specifications: signal band, required effective number of bits, noise floor.
  2. Inter-channel timing. How closely in time must samples from different channels align? Algorithms that compare phase or arrival time between channels (direction finding, power measurement, vibration mode analysis) are sensitive to it. Algorithms that only combine slow magnitudes (averaging temperatures, fusing a slow pressure reading with an accelerometer) are not.
  3. Latency. How long after an event must the fused result exist? A control loop and an offline analysis give very different answers.

The second requirement is the one most often missed, because it does not appear on any single ADC datasheet.

The cost of multiplexing

The cheapest architecture shares one fast ADC among N channels through an analog multiplexer. The ADC converts channel 0, then channel 1, and so on, and each channel is sampled at the aggregate rate divided by N. The catch is that the channels are sampled at different instants.

Worked example, with illustrative numbers: eight channels share a 1 MS/s converter. Each channel gets 125 kS/s, plenty for a 10 kHz band. But channel 7 is sampled 7 µs after channel 0. For a sine of frequency f, a time skew Δt is a phase error of 360° × f × Δt:

  • at 1 kHz, 360 × 1,000 × 7 µs ≈ 2.5°
  • at 10 kHz, about 25°

For a power meter computing voltage times current, a few degrees of phase error between the two channels corrupts the result at low power factor. For a microphone array, compare the skew with the signal you are trying to measure: two microphones 5 cm apart see a sound with at most 0.05 / 343 ≈ 146 µs of arrival difference. A 7 µs sampling skew is a noticeable fraction of that and biases the computed bearing.

Multiplexing has a second cost. When the multiplexer switches, the ADC's sampling capacitor must recharge from the previous channel's voltage to the new one. To settle within half an LSB, an RC network needs about (N + 1)·ln 2 time constants: about 9 time constants for 12 bits and 11.8 for 16 bits. If half of each 1 µs slot is available for acquisition, a 12-bit conversion needs a time constant of about 55 ns or less, which constrains the multiplexer resistance, the sensor's source impedance and any filter in front. Sensors with high source impedance usually need a buffer per channel, which erodes the cost saving.

Skew is deterministic, so it can sometimes be corrected digitally: if each channel is band-limited well below its Nyquist frequency, a fractional-delay filter can interpolate every channel to a common instant. That works, but it costs computation and adds latency, and it fails for content near the band edge.

Simultaneous sampling options

Three structures give truly aligned samples.

  • Sample-and-hold per channel, one shared ADC. All channels are captured at the same instant on their own hold capacitors, then converted one after another. Timing is aligned; the converter is shared. The hold capacitors must keep their voltage, without droop, for the whole conversion sequence, and each hold stage needs a driver.
  • One SAR ADC per channel. Each channel has its own converter, all triggered by a common start signal. SAR converters are compact and draw power roughly in proportion to their conversion rate, which suits moderate rates and resolutions. Area and reference loading scale with the number of channels.
  • One sigma-delta modulator per channel, common clock. Each channel oversamples with its own modulator, all clocked together and decimated by identical digital filters, so their outputs are inherently time-aligned. Sigma-delta suits high resolution at low bandwidth, and the anti-alias filter becomes simple because of oversampling. The price is filter latency, often many output samples, and digital area for N decimation filters.

Comparing the options

Architecture Inter-channel timing Area and power trend Fits well
Multiplexed, one ADC Skewed; correct digitally if band-limited Lowest; one converter Slow, independent channels
S/H per channel, shared ADC Aligned Low; N hold stages plus one ADC Moderate rates, few channels
SAR per channel Aligned Grows with N; efficient at moderate rates Mid-rate arrays, low latency
Sigma-delta per channel Aligned; long filter latency Grows with N; digital filters dominate High resolution, low bandwidth

These are trends, not numbers. Actual area and power depend heavily on the process and the design, so compare candidate designs or parts on the same basis: energy per conversion at the needed resolution, and area including references and drivers.

Shared resources that couple channels

Whatever the architecture, some things are shared, and shared things couple channels.

  • The reference. Every converter draws charge from it at each conversion. If one channel's conversion disturbs the reference while another is deciding a bit, the error shows up as crosstalk. Simultaneous SARs make this worse because all of them load the reference at the same moments; a strong reference buffer or per-channel decoupling is needed.
  • The sampling clock. For simultaneous sampling, the clock must arrive at every channel with matched delay. Clock skew between channels is just a smaller version of the multiplexing problem, and clock jitter limits each channel's resolution at high input frequency.
  • Substrate and supply. Many converters switching together inject correlated noise; staggering them helps noise and hurts alignment.
  • Calibration. Gain and phase differences between channels, including those of each sensor and its analog filter, must be calibrated together. An anti-alias filter whose components vary by a few per cent adds a phase shift that differs from channel to channel, comparable to sampling skew.

Try it yourself

  1. With numpy, generate two copies of a 1 kHz sine, sample the second 7 µs late, and compute the phase difference from their FFTs. Repeat at 10 kHz.
  2. Implement a fractional-delay correction (for example, a windowed-sinc FIR) and measure how well it removes the skew for tones near and far from the band edge.
  3. In ngspice, model a multiplexer switch resistance and a sampling capacitor, step between two channels at different voltages, and measure how many time constants it takes to settle within half an LSB at 12 bits.
