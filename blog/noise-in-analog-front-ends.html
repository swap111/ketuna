Thermal and flicker noise in analog front ends

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

Resistors and transistors add random noise that sets the smallest signal a front end can see. Learn where thermal and flicker noise come from, how to refer noise to the input, and how to size a stage for low noise.

In this article

Every sensor interface eventually hits a floor: a level below which the signal is buried in random fluctuations generated by the circuit itself. Gain cannot help, because the amplifier amplifies its own noise along with the signal. Knowing where that noise comes from, and which part dominates in your band, decides how you size the input stage, how much current you spend, and whether you need tricks such as chopping. The two mechanisms that matter most in CMOS front ends are thermal noise and flicker noise.

Thermal noise: resistance at a temperature

Any resistance at a temperature above absolute zero has charge carriers jostling randomly, producing a fluctuating voltage across its terminals. Its power spectral density is flat (white) across all frequencies of practical interest:

vn2 = 4kTR (V2/Hz)

At 300 K, a 10 kΩ resistor produces about 12.9 nV/√Hz. Noise densities are quoted per square root of hertz because noise power adds with bandwidth: the RMS voltage over a band B is the density times √B.

A MOSFET channel is a resistance too. In saturation its thermal noise is modelled as a current source between drain and source with density 4kTγgm, where γ is about 2/3 for long-channel devices and can be larger in short-channel ones. Dividing by gm2 refers it to the gate as an equivalent input voltage:

vn,in2 = 4kTγ / gm

The design lesson is direct: thermal noise falls as gm rises. A transistor with gm = 1 mS and γ = 2/3 contributes about 3.3 nV/√Hz. Doubling gm reduces the noise power by half, and the density by √2. Raising gm costs current, so low thermal noise is bought with power. Biasing at high gm/ID, towards weak inversion, gets the most gm per microampere.

Flicker noise: the low-frequency rise

Below some frequency, MOSFET noise rises as 1/f. The common explanation is that carriers are trapped and released by defects at the silicon–oxide interface, randomly modulating the channel's charge. A simple model refers it to the gate as:

vn,1/f2 = K / (Cox W L f)

where K is a process-dependent constant. Two features matter. The noise grows without limit as frequency falls, and it shrinks with gate area W·L, not with current. Flicker noise also varies between processes and between NMOS and PMOS devices; in many processes PMOS devices show less of it, which is one reason for PMOS input pairs in low-frequency amplifiers. Check your PDK's models rather than assuming.

The frequency where the 1/f line meets the thermal floor is the flicker corner fc. Below it flicker noise dominates. For small devices it can be tens of kilohertz or more.

Input-referred noise and the first stage

To compare noise with signal, refer everything to the input: take each source, find how it appears at the output, and divide by the gain from input to output. Sources in the first stage are divided by nothing; sources in the second stage are divided by the first stage's gain, and so on. That is why the first stage sets the noise of a well-designed chain. Give it enough gain, typically ten or more, and later stages barely matter.

Do not forget the source. A sensor with 10 kΩ of output resistance contributes 12.9 nV/√Hz before your circuit does anything. There is little point designing an amplifier much quieter than its source; the goal is to make your contribution small relative to it.

A worked example

Consider a front end for a low-frequency sensor with a band of 0.5 Hz to 100 Hz and a 10 kΩ source resistance. The input transistor has gm = 1 mS, γ = 2/3 and a flicker corner of 10 kHz. These values are illustrative.

The flicker density at frequency f is the thermal density scaled by √(fc/f), and integrating 1/f over the band gives a logarithm. The script adds up the three contributions:

from math import log, sqrt

K_B, T = 1.380649e-23, 300.0

def input_noise(gm, gamma, f_corner, f_lo, f_hi, r_source=0.0):
    """RMS input-referred noise (V) of one transistor plus source resistor."""
    s_th = 4 * K_B * T * gamma / gm             # thermal density, V^2/Hz
    thermal = s_th * (f_hi - f_lo)              # flat part
    flicker = s_th * f_corner * log(f_hi / f_lo)  # 1/f part, integrated
    source = 4 * K_B * T * r_source * (f_hi - f_lo)
    return sqrt(s_th), sqrt(thermal), sqrt(flicker), sqrt(source)

dens, th, fl, src = input_noise(gm=1e-3, gamma=2/3, f_corner=10e3,
                                f_lo=0.5, f_hi=100, r_source=10e3)
print(f"thermal density {dens*1e9:.2f} nV/rtHz")
print(f"0.5-100 Hz: thermal {th*1e9:.0f} nV, flicker {fl*1e9:.0f} nV, "
      f"10 kohm source {src*1e9:.0f} nV (rms)")
print(f"total {sqrt(th**2 + fl**2 + src**2)*1e9:.0f} nV rms")

The result: thermal noise of the transistor contributes about 33 nV RMS, the source resistor about 128 nV, and flicker noise about 765 nV. The total, about 776 nV RMS, is almost all flicker. Spending more current to raise gm would barely help here, because it attacks the wrong term.

Sizing for low noise

Match the remedy to the dominant mechanism.

  • Thermal-limited (wide band, above the corner): raise gm of the input devices, bias at high gm/ID, keep load and cascode devices from adding noise by giving them low gm relative to the input pair.
  • Flicker-limited (low frequency): increase input gate area. In the example, ten times the area cuts the flicker noise power by ten, lowering the corner to 1 kHz and the flicker contribution to about 242 nV. The cost is capacitance, which lowers bandwidth and increases the noise penalty of any capacitive feedback network.
  • Chopping: modulate the input signal to a frequency well above the flicker corner, amplify it there, and demodulate after the amplifier. The amplifier's own low-frequency noise and offset are moved up and filtered out. Chopping trades flicker noise for switching artefacts and ripple that need care.
  • Do not over-filter late. Noise outside the signal band still matters if the ADC aliases it in; band-limit before sampling.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script and find the gate area increase that makes flicker equal to the source resistor's contribution.
  2. In ngspice with sky130 models, bias a common-source stage and run noise v(out) VIN dec 20 0.1 1meg, then plot the input-referred noise spectrum and read the flicker corner.
  3. Repeat with the input device four times larger in W·L at the same current, and compare the low-frequency noise and the corner.
