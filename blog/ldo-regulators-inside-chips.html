Low-dropout regulators inside chips: the loop, stability and dropout

Analog & mixed-signal Advanced 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

An LDO is an op-amp driving a large pass transistor inside a feedback loop. Learn where dropout comes from, why the output pole moves with load current, and how the capacitor choice decides stability.

In this article
  1. The loop in four parts
  2. Dropout
  3. Two poles that will not sit still
  4. Large capacitor or no capacitor
  5. Load transients: the capacitor buys time
  6. Try it yourself

Most chips do not run their blocks directly from the board supply. A sensitive analog block, a PLL or a memory array gets its own regulated rail, and the circuit that makes it is usually a low-dropout regulator (LDO). An LDO is conceptually just an op-amp in feedback, but it drives a huge transistor into a load that can change by orders of magnitude in nanoseconds. That makes it one of the harder feedback problems in analog design, and a good place to see how loop poles depend on operating conditions.

The loop in four parts

An LDO has four pieces.

  • A reference VREF, usually from a bandgap.
  • An error amplifier that compares VREF with a scaled copy of the output.
  • A pass transistor, typically a large PMOS between the input supply and the output.
  • A feedback divider R1, R2 from the output to ground.

The amplifier adjusts the PMOS gate until the divided output equals the reference, giving VOUT = VREF × (1 + R1/R2). With VREF = 0.6 V and R1 = R2, the output is 1.2 V. Note the polarity: a PMOS common-source pass device inverts (raising its gate lowers the output), so the feedback goes to the amplifier's non-inverting input to keep the overall loop negative.

A PMOS pass device is what makes the regulator "low dropout". To turn a PMOS fully on, the amplifier only has to pull its gate towards ground, which it can do even when VIN is barely above VOUT. An NMOS source follower would need its gate above the output by a threshold plus overdrive, which needs headroom the input may not have.

Dropout

Dropout voltage is the smallest VIN − VOUT at which the regulator still regulates. As the input falls towards the output, the amplifier pulls the gate lower and lower until the PMOS is fully on and sits in its triode region, behaving like a resistor Ron. Beyond that point the loop has nothing left to give, and the output simply follows the input minus ILOAD × Ron.

Worked example, illustrative numbers: a pass device with Ron = 2 Ω at full gate drive, carrying 50 mA, drops 100 mV. Halving dropout means doubling the transistor width, which doubles its gate capacitance. Keep that capacitance in mind; it returns in the stability analysis.

There is a subtler cost. Near dropout the PMOS leaves saturation, its gain collapses, and loop gain with it. Line and load regulation, and supply rejection, degrade well before the hard dropout point.

Two poles that will not sit still

The loop has two main poles.

  • The output pole, set by the output capacitor COUT and the resistance at the output node. That resistance is roughly the pass transistor's output resistance in parallel with the load, and the transistor's ro ≈ 1/(λILOAD) falls as load current rises.
  • The gate pole, set by the error amplifier's output resistance and the large gate capacitance of the pass device.

Here is why that is hard. Take λ = 0.1 V−1 and COUT = 1 µF, with numbers made up for illustration. At 1 mA load, ro ≈ 10 kΩ and the output pole is 1 / (2π × 10 kΩ × 1 µF) ≈ 16 Hz. At 50 mA, ro ≈ 200 Ω and the pole moves to about 800 Hz. A fifty-fold change in load moves the pole fifty-fold. Meanwhile loop gain changes too, because the pass device's transconductance rises with current. A compensation that gives good phase margin at one load may give almost none at another.

Large capacitor or no capacitor

LDOs split into two families, depending on which pole is made dominant.

External-capacitor LDOs use a large off-chip COUT, typically microfarads. The output pole is the dominant pole, and the gate pole must stay well above the unity-gain frequency. The worst case is usually heavy load, where the output pole moves up towards the gate pole. Historically these regulators relied on the capacitor's equivalent series resistance (ESR) to add a stabilising zero, which is why discrete LDO datasheets specify an allowed ESR range. Modern designs add internal zeros so ceramic capacitors with very low ESR also work.

Capacitor-less (on-chip) LDOs have only on-die capacitance, typically hundreds of picofarads. The output pole is now high, so the dominant pole is made internal, at the amplifier output, often with Miller compensation across the pass device. The worst case flips: at light load, the output resistance is high and the output pole drops towards the unity-gain frequency. Designers add a minimum bleed current, or adaptive biasing that raises the amplifier current with load, to keep the poles apart.

A buffer between the error amplifier and the gate is common in both families. It drives the large gate capacitance with low output resistance, pushing the gate pole up, at the cost of some headroom and quiescent current.

Load transients: the capacitor buys time

When the load current jumps, the loop cannot respond instantly. Until it does, the output capacitor supplies the difference, and the output droops by about ΔV ≈ ΔI × tresponse / COUT.

  • External capacitor: a 50 mA step, a loop that responds in 1 µs and 1 µF give about 50 mV of droop.
  • On-chip: a 5 mA step, a 5 ns response and 200 pF give about 125 mV.

The second example shows why capacitor-less LDOs need very fast loops and are suited to loads whose current steps are small or predictable. A digital block that wakes from sleep all at once can exceed what an on-chip LDO can absorb; staggered wake-up, or an advance warning to the regulator, helps.

Pitfalls when designing or simulating one:

  • Checking stability only at one load; sweep from minimum to maximum load and across corners
  • Forgetting the package and bond-wire inductance in series with an external capacitor
  • Measuring supply rejection only at DC; it falls as loop gain falls with frequency
  • Leaving out a start-up and current-limit path, so a short on the output destroys the pass device

Try it yourself

  1. In ngspice, build an LDO from an ideal transconductance amplifier, a sky130 PMOS and a resistor divider, with a current-source load.
  2. Break the loop for AC with a large inductor in the feedback path and a capacitor-coupled AC source, then run ac at 1 mA and 50 mA loads and compare the crossover frequency and phase margin.
  3. Run a tran with a pulsed load current and measure the droop. Compare with ΔI × t / C, then double COUT and repeat.
