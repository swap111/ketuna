Setup and hold time, explained with one flip-flop and a clock edge

Digital & VLSI design Beginner 27 Nov 2026 5 min read By Ketuna

A flip-flop needs its input to sit still for a short window around the clock edge. Here is what that window is, why missing it breaks a design, and how to work out the fastest safe clock by hand.

Every synchronous digital design rests on one small promise: when the clock edge arrives, each flip-flop sees a clean, settled value on its input. Setup time and hold time are the two numbers that define what "settled" means. Once you understand them, timing reports stop looking like a wall of numbers and start reading like a story about a signal racing a clock.

The window around the edge

A D flip-flop does not sample its input at a single mathematical instant. Inside the cell, transistors need a little time to capture the value before the edge and a little time to let go of the old value after it. So the cell's library data gives two numbers:

  • Setup time (tsu): how long before the active clock edge the input D must already be stable.
  • Hold time (th): how long after the edge D must stay stable.

Together they form a keep-out window. D may change as often as it likes outside the window, but not inside it. A third number matters too: clock-to-Q (tcq), the delay from the clock edge until the new value appears on the output Q.

What happens if you miss the window

If D changes inside the window, the flip-flop may capture the old value, the new value, or neither for a while. That last case is metastability: the internal node hovers between 0 and 1 before falling one way, and nobody can say exactly when. Downstream logic may see a late value, or two flip-flops fed by the same signal may disagree.

In a fully synchronous design you avoid this by construction. Static timing analysis (STA) checks every path between flip-flops and proves that the data always arrives outside the window. Signals that come from another clock domain cannot be proven this way, which is why they need synchronizers, a topic for another article.

One register-to-register path

Here is the smallest interesting circuit: two registers with an adder between them.

module add_path (
  input  wire       clk,
  input  wire [7:0] a_in, b_in,
  output reg  [8:0] sum_q
);
  reg [7:0] a_q, b_q;
  always @(posedge clk) begin
    a_q   <= a_in;          // launch registers
    b_q   <= b_in;
    sum_q <= a_q + b_q;     // capture register
  end
endmodule

On one clock edge, a_q and b_q launch new values. They appear after tcq, ripple through the adder in some logic delay, and must reach sum_q at least tsu before the next edge. That gives the setup check:

Tclk ≥ tcq + tlogic,max + tsu

The hold check looks at the same edge. The capture flop is still latching the previous result, so the new data must not arrive too early and corrupt it:

tcq + tlogic,min ≥ th

Notice that the clock period does not appear in the hold check at all. That fact explains most of what follows.

A worked example

Suppose the library and the synthesised netlist give these numbers. They are made up for illustration; real values depend on the process, the cell and the operating corner.

Quantity Value
Clock-to-Q, tcq 0.30 ns
Longest adder path, tlogic,max 2.10 ns
Shortest path (least significant bit), tlogic,min 0.05 ns
Setup time, tsu 0.15 ns
Hold time, th 0.10 ns

Setup: 0.30 + 2.10 + 0.15 = 2.55 ns. The clock period must be at least 2.55 ns, so the fastest safe clock is 1 / 2.55 ns, about 392 MHz. If you ask for a 2.5 ns clock (400 MHz), the path misses by 0.05 ns: the timing report shows a slack of −0.05 ns. Negative slack means a violation.

Hold: 0.30 + 0.05 = 0.35 ns, which is at least 0.10 ns, so hold passes with 0.25 ns of slack.

Now imagine a different cell with a hold time of 0.40 ns. The shortest path now fails hold by 0.05 ns, and slowing the clock does nothing, because the period is not in the equation. The fix is to add delay to the short path, which is exactly what tools do by inserting buffers. A setup failure can be rescued on the bench by lowering the clock frequency; a hold failure on fabricated silicon usually cannot. That is why designers treat hold violations with extra respect.

Where clock skew enters

So far we assumed the clock reaches both flip-flops at the same moment. In reality the clock network has delay, and the capture flop may see its edge slightly later or earlier than the launch flop. If the capture clock arrives later by tskew, the data gets a little more time (setup gets easier) but must also stay out of the way a little longer (hold gets harder). Clock tree synthesis tries to keep skew small and predictable for this reason.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Trusting RTL simulation for timing. A plain RTL simulation has no real gate delays, so it cannot show a setup or hold violation. Timing is proven by STA on the netlist, not by waveforms.
  • Confusing hold time with "holding the value until the next edge". Hold time is a short interval just after the edge, usually far smaller than a clock period.
  • Reading only the worst setup path. Hold paths are often the very short ones, such as a register feeding another register directly.

Try it yourself

You can run this whole example on open-source tools:

  1. Save the module above as add_path.v and synthesise it with Yosys against a standard-cell library from an open PDK such as sky130: read the Verilog, run synth -top add_path, map flip-flops with dfflibmap -liberty <lib>, map logic with abc -liberty <lib> and write the netlist.
  2. Load the netlist into OpenSTA with read_liberty, read_verilog and link_design add_path, then create_clock -period 2.5 [get_ports clk].
  3. Run report_checks -path_delay min_max and find tcq, the logic delay, the setup or hold requirement and the slack in the report. Shrink the period until setup fails, and note which bit of the adder is the critical path.

Work the numbers on paper first, then compare them with the report. The habit of predicting before you run is what turns a timing report into something you understand.
