Production test strategy: what to test on every unit and what to sample

Systems & product engineering Intermediate 27 Nov 2026 6 min read By Ketuna

How to decide which tests every board gets, which are sampled, and where calibration fits, using fault coverage, escape rates and the rising cost of finding a fault later in the chain.

In this article

  3. Every unit or a sample

  5. Calibration and testability
  6. Common mistakes
  7. Try it yourself

Design verification proves that the design is right. Production test answers a different question: was this particular unit built right? Every board off the line can have a bridged pin, a missing part, a part placed backwards or a component from a bad batch, even though the design is perfect. A production test strategy decides what to check on every unit, what to check on a sample, and in what order, so faults are caught where they are cheapest to find.

Why late faults cost more

A fault found at a test station costs a few minutes and maybe a part. The same fault found after the board is in its enclosure means disassembly. Found by the customer, it means a return, shipping, a replacement unit and damage to your reputation that no spreadsheet captures. The mechanism is simple: each later stage adds labour and material to the unit, and adds people who must handle the failure. That is why the rule of thumb in manufacturing is to find faults at the earliest station that can detect them, even if that station costs money to set up.

The usual layers

Production test is a series of filters, each good at catching certain faults.

Stage What it catches Usually
Automated optical inspection (AOI) Missing, shifted or reversed parts, solder bridges, poor fillets Every board
X-ray inspection Hidden joints under ball-grid-array parts, voids Sample, or every board for critical parts
In-circuit or flying-probe test Shorts, opens, wrong resistor or capacitor values Every board, if access allows
Boundary scan (JTAG) Opens and shorts on pins of digital chips, without test pads on every net Every board with capable parts
Programming Loading firmware, unique IDs, keys Every board
Functional test Power rails, current draw, interfaces, radio, sensors working together Every board
Calibration Per-unit correction of offsets and gains Every unit that needs it
Burn-in, environmental, destructive tests Early-life failures, process drift Sample or per batch

The choice of in-circuit test versus boundary scan versus functional test depends on access. In-circuit test needs a test point on nearly every net and an expensive bed-of-nails fixture, but it pinpoints the faulty part. Flying probe needs no fixture but is slow, so it suits low volumes. Boundary scan reaches the pins of digital chips through their own test logic, which is why the design should bring the JTAG port to a connector or pads. Functional test catches what the others cannot, such as a radio that does not transmit, but often only tells you that something is wrong, not where.

Every unit or a sample

Test every unit when the fault is random and unit-specific: solder defects, wrong parts, calibration constants, firmware loading. A sample cannot tell you anything about the unit that was not sampled.

Sample when the property is set by the process or a batch rather than by the individual unit, or when the test is destructive or slow. Examples include cross-sectioning a solder joint, a long temperature-cycling run, a drop test on a finished product, or a full radio characterisation across channels. Sampling tells you whether the process is still in control; if a sample fails, you test the batch more heavily and look for the cause.

Coverage, escapes and yield

Two numbers describe a test stage. Fault coverage is the fraction of real faults it catches. Yield is the fraction of units that pass. They interact, and a short calculation makes the trade-off concrete. The numbers below are made up for illustration.

boards = 5000
defect_rate = 0.05          # 5 % of boards built have a defect
coverage = [0.60, 0.80, 0.70]   # AOI, in-circuit, functional

remaining = boards * defect_rate
for name, c in zip(["AOI", "in-circuit", "functional"], coverage):
    caught = remaining * c
    remaining -= caught
    print(f"{name:11s} catches {caught:6.1f}, {remaining:5.1f} left")
print(f"escapes to customers: {remaining:.1f} boards "
      f"({remaining / boards * 1e6:.0f} per million)")

Of the 250 defective boards, AOI catches 150, in-circuit test catches 80 of the remaining 100, and functional test catches 14 of the last 20, leaving 6 that reach customers, 1,200 per million. Two lessons follow. First, no single stage needs perfect coverage if the stages catch different faults. Second, the escape count is proportional to the defect rate, so improving the assembly process can reduce escapes as much as adding another test.

When stages each lose some good units too, yields multiply. If three stations pass 98 %, 97 % and 99 % of the units they see, the first-pass yield of the line is 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.99 ≈ 0.941, about 94 %. Every failing unit then needs diagnosis, repair or scrap, so a poor first-pass yield costs labour even if escapes are low.

Calibration and testability

Calibration belongs in the plan

Sensors, analogue front ends, oscillators and radios often need per-unit calibration. Plan it like a test: what reference is used, how it is itself checked, what the pass limits are after calibration, and where the constants are stored. Always verify after calibrating, with a second measurement at a different point, so a bad calibration is caught on the line instead of producing a unit that is confidently wrong. Record every unit's constants and results against its serial number. When a field return arrives, that record is the first thing you will want.

Design for testability starts in the schematic

None of this works if the board was not designed for it. Bring rails and key signals to test pads, expose a programming and boundary-scan port, add a way for the test station to put the firmware into a test mode, and give every unit a readable serial number. A functional test that has to infer everything from the product's normal user interface is slow and catches less.

Common mistakes

  • Testing only what is easy to reach. Coverage holes become field returns.
  • No limits, only "it works". A board that draws twice the expected current may work today and fail next month. Test against numeric limits with margin.
  • Calibrating without verifying. The calibration step itself can fail.
  • Not logging results. Without per-unit data you cannot spot drift or trace returns.

Try it yourself

  1. Run the script above, then change the defect rate and the coverage of each stage and see which change reduces escapes most for your assumptions.
  2. Write a functional test for a board you own in Python with pyserial: power it, read its supply current from a bench meter or a current-sense board, query its firmware over UART and compare every reading against numeric limits, logging results with a serial number.
  3. Look at a board you designed and list which nets a fixture could probe. Mark the faults that no test in your plan would catch.
