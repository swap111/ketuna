Mentoring

Learn from engineers who do the work

Ketuna's mentors are practising engineers from industry. They review your designs and code every week, meet you one to one, and prepare you for interviews, the same way a good senior engineer brings up a new team member.

The weekly rhythm Become a mentor

Who the mentors are

The program is led by Swapnil Sapre, Ketuna's Skilling & Mentoring Partner, who brings 24+ years of taking products from spec to silicon to volume production, and who began his career teaching VLSI and embedded systems. Part-time industry mentors join him for each cohort; two to three are being recruited for the first.

Working engineers

Part-time mentors who work in design, verification, physical design, embedded, systems or product roles, and bring current practice into every review.

Small groups

The pilot plan has two part-time mentors for a cohort of about fifteen: roughly one mentor for every seven or eight students. In the capstone, each team of four gets its own weekly mentor check-in.

Reviewers, not lecturers

Theory is recorded once and discussed live. Mentor time goes where it matters most: reviewing your work, asking "how do you know it works?", and helping you get unstuck.

The weekly rhythm

Every week runs on the same cycle, the one you will meet in an engineering team. Review actions loop back into the next week's work.

Design reviews

Every Friday you present and defend your work against a checklist. Actions are recorded and closed the next week.

Code reviews

Every change goes through a pull request. Mentors and peers comment on correctness, tests and readability, as in a real team.

One-to-ones

A short mid-week check-in: where you are, what blocks you, and whether your estimate still holds.

Career sessions

Resume, portfolio and professional profile reviews, and how to talk about your projects.

Mock interviews

Three per student, technical and HR, with written feedback, using an interview question bank built around the program.

Weekly status

A short written update: done, next, risks. It is graded, because clear status reporting is a skill employers notice.

How AI is used, and checked

AI assistants are part of every lab, because they are part of engineering work now. The skill we teach is checking them.

  • You do each lab by hand first, then again with AI.
  • You review the AI's output before running it, then prove it with tests, lint, formal tools or a reference model.
  • You keep an AI log: what you asked, what came back, what was wrong, and how you found out.
  • Mentors grade the AI part on the quality of your checking, not on speed.
  • Concept exams are taken without AI.

Examples from the tracks

Verification: AI drafts tests and assertions; you show with coverage and design mutation which ones catch bugs.

Physical design: AI suggests timing fixes; you check each one against the STA report first.

Embedded: AI drafts a driver; you check every register access against the datasheet and test each one.

How progress is measured

  • Labs pass automated checks in CI, not just on your laptop.
  • Estimates vs actuals for every lab, so you can see your planning improve.
  • Review records: what was found, and whether you closed it.
  • AI logs show how well you catch an assistant's mistakes.
  • Weekly quizzes on the study guides, auto-graded, with an explanation for every miss.

What you get at the end

A public portfolio

Three or more verified projects with passing CI, specs and test plans, that any employer can open and check.

A reviewed product or tape-out

A team capstone with a working demo and postmortem; on track B, a hardened design ready for an open shuttle.

People who know your work

Mentors who have reviewed you for twelve weeks and can speak to what you did, when you ask them to.

Become a mentor

If you design, verify, lay out, program or ship hardware for a living and enjoy helping people grow, we would like to hear from you.

What we ask

  • Hands-on industry experience in at least one area we teach
  • A few hours a week during a cohort, for reviews, check-ins or labs; much of it can be remote
  • Comfort with open-source tools, or willingness to learn them
  • Never bringing your employer's confidential material or tools into teaching
  • Checking your employer's policy on outside work before you start

What mentors get

  • Paid mentoring, per session
  • Ready-made labs, guides, slides and rubrics, so your time goes into people
  • A say in the curriculum in your area
  • The chance to give back and shape the next generation of engineers
  • A network of peers who care about doing engineering well
