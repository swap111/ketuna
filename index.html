What Ketuna is

Two things, done by the same people: mentoring for individual engineers, and partnering with institutions that want to build STEM skills.

Mentoring

Small cohorts, guided by engineers who do this work for a living. Every week has design reviews, code reviews, a one-to-one check-in and a written status. You learn the engineering and the habits around it: specs, estimates, reviews and postmortems.

How mentoring works

Partnering

Colleges, companies and STEM programs can work with us on faculty development, semester courses, open-source lab setup, sponsored capstone problems and upskilling. Open-source tools mean no licence cost for anyone.

Four pillars

One connected chain from chip to board to firmware to finished product, so you understand how your piece fits the whole.

Embedded

  • Embedded C, startup code, linker scripts
  • Interrupts, drivers, Zephyr and FreeRTOS
  • PCB design and review in KiCad
  • TinyML on a microcontroller, firmware CI

VLSI

  • RTL design, CDC, FIFOs, a RISC-V core
  • Verification: cocotb, pyuvm, coverage, formal
  • Physical design: synthesis to GDS, STA
  • DRC, LVS and tape-out on open PDKs

Systems

  • Needs to traceable requirements
  • Architecture, trade studies, MBSE
  • Power, latency and cost budgets
  • FMEA, verification and validation plans

Products

  • DFM, DFT and manufacturing test
  • BOM costing and supply checks
  • Certification awareness, product lifecycle
  • A team capstone with a working demo

How mentoring works

The same rhythm you will meet in an engineering team, every week.

  1. Plan

    Monday sprint planning. You estimate each task before you start it.

  2. Build

    Daily stand-up, then labs: by hand first, then with AI, and a log of how you checked it.

  3. Review

    Code reviews on every change, a mid-week mentor check-in, and a Friday design review.

  4. Report

    A short written weekly status. Estimates are compared with actuals, so you learn to plan.

More on the mentoring model

The flagship: 12 weeks, silicon to product

Full-time, about 40 hours a week. One shared start, one specialization, one team project, and a professional layer running through all of it.

Weeks 1–2

Common core

Linux, Git and Python; digital logic and Verilog; testbenches; computer architecture and a small RISC-V core; how chips and products are made; AI literacy.

Weeks 3–8

One track

A: design and verification. B: physical design and tape-out. C: analog and mixed-signal (coming later). D: embedded and edge AI. E: systems and product.

Weeks 9–12

Team capstone

Mixed-track teams of four build one small product, such as a vibration monitor or a gesture controller, and demonstrate it on demo day.

How we teach

By hand first, then with AI

Every lab is done twice. First you do it yourself. Then you do it with an AI assistant, and keep an AI log: what you asked, what came back, what was wrong, and how you found out with tests, lint or formal tools. The AI part is graded on how well you checked it, not on speed. Concept exams are taken without AI.

Open-source tools only

Icarus Verilog, Verilator, cocotb, Yosys, OpenROAD, LibreLane, Magic, KLayout, Zephyr, KiCad and more. No licences, ever: you can install every tool at home and keep using it after the program. The full toolchain.

What you leave with

Only things that can be checked. We do not promise placements; we plan to publish real, audited outcomes once cohorts have run.

  • A public portfolio with three or more verified projects, each with passing CI
  • A team capstone with a working demo, reviewed documentation and a postmortem
  • Track B: a hardened design ready for an open shuttle such as Tiny Tapeout
  • Four written documents: a spec, a test plan, a design note and a postmortem
  • AI logs that show how you check AI-written work
  • Three mock interviews and feedback from working engineers

Questions

When does the first cohort start?

The first cohort is planned for 2027. Dates are announced once they are fixed. The free weekend workshop is the best way to try the teaching style first.

What does it cost?

Fees are not published yet. They will be announced, with any tax and the refund policy, before applications open. The weekend workshop is free.

Do I need chip-design or embedded experience?

No. You need engineering fundamentals: digital logic, a little C or Python, and the willingness to work about 40 hours a week for 12 weeks. Admission is a 60-minute online test and a 20-minute call.

Is it online or in person?

The plan is live labs and reviews with theory recorded beforehand and discussed live. The final format for each cohort is announced with its dates.

Which tracks run first?

That is announced with the first cohort. Design and verification (track A) has the most labs ready today; analog and mixed-signal (track C) comes later.

Will I get a job?

We do not promise placements. We help you build a portfolio that employers can check, prepare you with mock interviews, and plan to publish audited outcome numbers, whatever they are.

Can I use AI tools?

Yes, in every lab, after you have done it by hand. You must check the AI's output and log how. Concept exams are taken without AI.

Who are the mentors?

The program is led by Swapnil Sapre, with 24+ years from spec to silicon to volume production, together with practising engineers from industry who mentor part-time. Ketuna is recruiting two to three part-time mentors for the first cohort; see mentoring, and if you are one, consider becoming a mentor.
